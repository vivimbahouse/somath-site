More than getting the answer

What does math enrichment look like?

A student can calculate correctly without being able to explain why a method works. Enrichment adds that next layer: representing a problem, comparing strategies, explaining a solution, and applying an idea when the question looks unfamiliar.

It does not have to mean rushing into the next grade. A first grader can explore place value more deeply; an older student can learn to turn a story into an equation and defend each step. The goal is thoughtful, increasingly independent problem-solving, with fluency as a foundation.

At SOMATH, teachers introduce a concept, guide students through examples, and set progressively more demanding practice. Our published lessons let you see the actual mathematics before choosing a program. For a broader explanation, read what math enrichment means for your child.

Try the difference

Three examples of enrichment thinking

The extra challenge is not always a bigger number. These original examples show how a familiar topic can lead to a question that asks for an explanation, a second method, or a general rule.

Early elementary: notice what stays the same

First solve 46 − 23. Then ask: without starting over, what is 47 − 24? Explain why the answer is unchanged.

See the reasoning

Both answers are 23. Increasing both numbers by one keeps the distance between them the same. A number line or two groups of objects can make that relationship visible.

Upper elementary: explain equivalent decimals

One ribbon is 0.6 meter long and another is 0.60 meter long. A student says the second ribbon is longer because 60 is greater than 6. How would you explain the mistake?

See the reasoning

The ribbons are equal in length. Six tenths is sixty hundredths: dividing each tenth into ten equal pieces changes the number of pieces, not the total length. The place value matters, not just the digits.

Pre-algebra: connect a story to an equation

After a club receives 18 new books, it has 53 books. Write and solve an equation for the original number. Then explain how your answer changes if the final total rises by five while the donation stays the same.

See the reasoning

Let b be the original number: b + 18 = 53, so b = 35. If the final total is 58, then b + 18 = 58 and b = 40. With the donation unchanged, five more books at the end means five more at the start.

These are examples, not a placement test. A teacher can adjust the numbers, representations, and follow-up questions to a student's readiness.

Choose by the learning goal

Math enrichment, tutoring, or independent practice?

These approaches overlap. Tutoring can offer advanced enrichment, and a group class can revisit foundational skills. Rather than choose by a label alone, ask what the student needs and how the teaching will address it.

Parent questions

Math enrichment in NYC: frequently asked questions

What is math enrichment?

Math enrichment develops number sense, reasoning, and problem-solving beyond routine school exercises. It can deepen a familiar topic or introduce new ideas; it does not have to mean moving faster through grade levels.

Is math enrichment only for gifted students?

No. Students who enjoy math may want more challenge, while others need stronger reasoning or confidence with unfamiliar problems. The important question is whether the content and pace fit the child. An evaluation helps identify that fit.

How is enrichment different from tutoring?

Enrichment describes the learning goal, while tutoring describes a teaching format. A tutor can provide enrichment, and an enrichment class can strengthen foundational skills. At SOMATH, the program is taught in person in groups of up to six.

SOMATH offers programs for grades 1–12. Little Newtons serves grades 1–2, Kid Einsteins grades 3–4, and Pre-Algebra grades 5–6. Further courses cover algebra, geometry, advanced math, and exam preparation. Placement depends on readiness as well as grade.
