Understanding two-digit divisors

A division sentence has three parts. The dividend is the total being divided, the divisor is the size of each group, and the quotient is the number of groups. In 156 ÷ 12 = 13, the 156 pencils are packed in boxes of 12, so the quotient 13 counts the boxes.

A two-digit divisor is any whole number from 10 to 99. The method is the same as dividing by a single digit; the only difference is that the multiplication facts are larger, so we lean on multiples, estimation, and checking. In a sharing story the divisor can instead be the number of equal shares, and the quotient then tells the amount in each share.

Q1

In 132 ÷ 11 = 12, name the dividend, the divisor, and the quotient.

Answer

Dividend 132; divisor 11; quotient 12

  1. The dividend 132 is the total being divided. The divisor 11 is the size of each group. The quotient 12 counts the groups: 11 × 12 = 132.

Q2

A store packs 144 crayons into boxes of 12. Write the division sentence and tell how many boxes are filled.

Answer

144 ÷ 12 = 12; 12 boxes

  1. The total 144 is the dividend and the box size 12 is the divisor. Count by 12s: 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 72, 84, 96, 108, 120, 132, 144. That is twelve 12s.
  2. So 144 ÷ 12 = 12. Check: 12 × 12 = 144.

Q3

Which of these numbers can be a two-digit divisor: 9, 10, 45, 99, 100? Explain.

Answer

10, 45, and 99

  1. A two-digit divisor is a whole number from 10 to 99. The numbers 10, 45, and 99 are in that range.
  2. 9 has only one digit, and 100 has three digits, so neither is a two-digit divisor.

Q4

Ninety stickers are shared so that each pack gets 15 stickers, and exactly 6 packs are filled. Write the division sentence and label each part.

Answer

90 ÷ 15 = 6; dividend 90, divisor 15, quotient 6

  1. The total of 90 stickers is the dividend. The 15 stickers in each pack is the divisor. The 6 packs is the quotient.
  2. Check: 15 × 6 = 90.

Q5

A student says that in 195 ÷ 13 = 15 the divisor is 195. Correct the student and explain what each number means.

Answer

The divisor is 13, not 195

  1. 195 is the dividend, the total being divided. 13 is the divisor, the number in each group. 15 is the quotient, the number of groups.
  2. Check: 13 × 15 = 195, so the sentence is true once the parts are named correctly.

Using multiples to prepare for division

Multiplication helps you divide. Before dividing by a two-digit number, build a short list of its multiples by adding the divisor each time: 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 72, 84, 96, 108. Each multiple answers a division question; because 12 × 7 = 84, we know 84 ÷ 12 = 7.

When the dividend is not on the list, find the greatest multiple that does not go past it. That multiple gives the quotient, and the difference gives the remainder. Keeping the list beside your work turns every long-division step into a quick look-up.

Q10

Build multiples of 12 to find the greatest multiple of 12 that is not more than 100. Then give the quotient and remainder for 100 ÷ 12.

Answer

96; 100 ÷ 12 = 8 R 4

  1. Multiples of 12: 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 72, 84, 96, 108. The multiple 108 is past 100, so the greatest multiple that fits is 96 = 12 × 8.
  2. 100 − 96 = 4, so 100 ÷ 12 = 8 R 4. Check: 12 × 8 + 4 = 100, and 4 < 12.

Estimating quotient digits

Round, estimate, then check. Round the dividend and the divisor to friendly numbers, such as 156 ≈ 160 and 24 ≈ 20, and divide the friendly numbers in your head: 160 ÷ 20 = 8. That estimate is only a starting digit, so always test it with the original divisor.

Multiply the divisor by the estimate. If the product is larger than the number you are dividing, lower the digit; if the leftover is as large as the divisor, raise the digit. For 156 ÷ 24, the products 192 and 168 are too large, but 24 × 6 = 144 fits, leaving 156 − 144 = 12. Choose the largest digit whose product does not exceed the number you are dividing.

Q15

A student tries 9 for 158 ÷ 21. Explain why 9 does not work, then find the correct quotient digit and remainder.

Answer

7 R 11

  1. Check 21 × 9 = 189. Since 189 > 158, the digit 9 is too large.
  2. Try 8: 21 × 8 = 168, which is still larger than 158. Try 7: 21 × 7 = 147, and 158 − 147 = 11.
  3. Because 11 < 21, the digit 7 fits. So 158 ÷ 21 = 7 R 11. Check: 21 × 7 + 11 = 158.

Setting up long division

Put each number in the right place. The divisor goes outside the bracket and the dividend goes inside. Then find where to start: look at the first digit of the dividend. If the divisor does not fit into it, use the first two digits. In 156 ÷ 12, 12 does not fit into 1, so we start with 15.

The first quotient digit sits above the last digit of the group you used. Since 12 fits into 15 once, write 1 above the tens digit, 5. Keeping each quotient digit aligned with the dividend's place values prevents answers that are ten times too large or too small.

Q16

Write 182 ÷ 14 in long-division form. Which number goes outside the bracket, which digits do you start with, and where does the first quotient digit go? Then finish the division.

Answer

14 outside; start with 18; first digit 1 above the tens digit; 182 ÷ 14 = 13

  1. The divisor 14 goes outside the bracket and the dividend 182 goes inside. 14 does not fit into 1, so start with the first two digits, 18.
  2. Divide 18 by 14: write 1 in the tens place. Multiply 1 × 14 = 14, then subtract 18 − 14 = 4. Bring down the 2 to make 42.
  3. Divide 42 by 14: write 3 in the ones place. Multiply 3 × 14 = 42, then subtract 42 − 42 = 0.
  4. No digits remain to bring down. The quotient is 13 and the remainder is 0. Check: 14 × 13 = 182.

Q18

For 96 ÷ 16, does 16 fit into 9? Explain where the quotient digit goes, then solve.

Answer

No; the quotient digit goes above the ones digit; 96 ÷ 16 = 6

  2. Divide 96 by 16: multiples of 16 are 16, 32, 48, 64, 80, 96, so 16 × 6 = 96. Write 6 in the ones place. Subtract 96 − 96 = 0.
  3. The quotient is 6 and the remainder is 0. Check: 16 × 6 = 96.

Q20

A student writes the first quotient digit of 168 ÷ 12 above the hundreds digit, 1. Explain the mistake and give the correct quotient.

Answer

The first digit belongs above the tens digit, 6; 168 ÷ 12 = 14

  1. 12 does not fit into 1, so no quotient digit goes above the hundreds place. The first group used is 16, so the first quotient digit belongs above the tens digit, 6.
  2. Divide 16 by 12: write 1 in the tens place. Multiply 1 × 12 = 12, then subtract 16 − 12 = 4. Bring down the 8 to make 48.
  3. Divide 48 by 12: write 4 in the ones place. Multiply 4 × 12 = 48, then subtract 48 − 48 = 0.

Divide, multiply, subtract, bring down

Repeat the steps until every digit is used. In each round, divide to choose a quotient digit, multiply that digit by the divisor, subtract to find what is left, and bring down the next digit of the dividend. For 156 ÷ 12, the first round uses 15: 12 fits once, 1 × 12 = 12, 15 − 12 = 3, and bringing down the 6 makes 36.

The second round uses 36: 12 fits three times, 3 × 12 = 36, and 36 − 36 = 0. No digits remain, so the quotient is 13. Finish with a multiplication check, 13 × 12 = 156. Divide, multiply, subtract, bring down, repeat.

Checking and adjusting estimates

Multiply to test; adjust until it fits. An estimate can be too large or too small. For 175 ÷ 24, trying 8 gives 24 × 8 = 192, which is more than 175, so decrease the digit. Trying 6 gives 24 × 6 = 144 and 175 − 144 = 31; since 31 is at least 24, another group still fits, so increase the digit.

Trying 7 gives 24 × 7 = 168 and 175 − 168 = 7. Because 7 is smaller than 24, the digit is just right: 175 ÷ 24 = 7 R 7, and 24 × 7 + 7 = 175. Two tests decide every digit: the product must fit, and the leftover must be smaller than the divisor.

Solving problems with remainders

A remainder tells us what is left over. For 173 ÷ 12, divide the first two digits: 12 fits into 17 once, 1 × 12 = 12, 17 − 12 = 5, and bringing down the 3 makes 53. Divide again: 12 fits into 53 four times, 4 × 12 = 48, and 53 − 48 = 5. No digits remain, so 5 is the remainder and the quotient is 14.

Every final answer passes two checks. The remainder must satisfy 0 ≤ remainder < divisor; if the remainder is 12 or more, another group still fits. Then verify with multiplication: divisor × quotient + remainder = dividend, so 12 × 14 + 5 = 173.

Applying division to word problems

Read, model, solve, and check. First identify the information: the total, the size of each group, and the unknown. A teacher with 168 pencils and boxes of 12 wants the number of boxes, so the division is 168 ÷ 12. Solve with the long-division steps to get 14, then check with multiplication: 14 × 12 = 168.

Finish with a sentence that answers the question and includes the correct unit: the teacher can fill 14 boxes. When a remainder appears, decide what it means. Count only full groups if the question asks for full groups; add one more group if every person or object must have a place; or report the leftovers if the story asks what remains.

Quick questions and answers

What is a two-digit divisor?

A two-digit divisor is any whole number from 10 to 99. Dividing by it uses the same long-division steps as a single-digit divisor, with larger multiplication facts.

How do multiples help with long division?

A short list of the divisor's multiples shows which quotient digit fits. The greatest multiple that does not go past the number you are dividing gives the digit, and the difference gives what is left.

How do I estimate a quotient digit?

Round the dividend and the divisor to friendly numbers, divide them in your head, and use the result as a trial digit. Then multiply the trial digit by the original divisor and adjust up or down until it fits.

Where does the first quotient digit go?

Above the last digit of the first group you divide. If the divisor does not fit into the first digit of the dividend, use the first two digits and write the quotient digit above the second one.

How do I know when an estimate needs adjusting?

If the product is larger than the number you are dividing, the digit is too large. If the leftover after subtracting is as large as the divisor, the digit is too small. A correct digit leaves a remainder smaller than the divisor.

How do I check an answer with a remainder?

Confirm that 0 ≤ remainder < divisor, then multiply the divisor by the quotient and add the remainder. The result must equal the dividend.
