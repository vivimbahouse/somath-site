What is a logarithm?

A logarithm is an exponent. The statement \(\log_b(a)=c\) means exactly the same thing as \(b^c=a\): the base \(b\) raised to the exponent \(c\) produces the argument \(a\). To evaluate a logarithm, ask the question in the image: the base raised to what power gives the argument?

The restrictions \(b>0\), \(b\neq1\), and \(a>0\) come from the exponential side. A positive base other than 1 produces every positive output exactly once, so the logarithm of a positive number is a single real value. Zero and negative arguments have no real logarithm, because a positive base never produces them.

Practice: try these five questions

Q1

Evaluate the logarithm by asking which exponent is needed. State the question in words first.

\[\log_3(243)\]
Answer

\(\log_3(243)=5\)

  1. Ask: 3 raised to what power gives 243?

  2. List powers of 3 until the argument appears.

    \[3^1=3,\;3^2=9,\;3^3=27,\;3^4=81,\;3^5=243\]

  3. The exponent that works is the logarithm.

    \[\log_3(243)=5\]

Check and interpret

Rewrite in exponential form: \(3^5=243\). The base, exponent, and argument all match the definition.

Q2

Evaluate the logarithm. Then explain why the answer would be the same for any allowed base.

\[\log_6(1)\]
Answer

\(\log_6(1)=0\); every allowed base gives 0.

  1. Ask: 6 raised to what power gives 1?

  2. Any nonzero number to the zero power equals 1.

    \[6^0=1\]

  3. For any base \(b>0\), \(b\neq1\), the same equation holds, so the logarithm of 1 is always 0.

    \[b^0=1\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\log_b(1)=0\]

Check and interpret

Test another base: \(\log_{11}(1)=0\) because \(11^0=1\). The argument 1 always corresponds to the exponent 0.

Q3

Evaluate the logarithm. Write the argument as a power of the base before answering.

\[\log_{10}(10{,}000)\]
Answer

\(\log_{10}(10{,}000)=4\)

  1. Count the zeros to write the argument as a power of 10.

    \[10{,}000=10^4\]

  2. Replace the argument by its power form.

    \[\log_{10}(10^4)\]

  3. The logarithm is the exponent on the base.

    \[\log_{10}(10^4)=4\]

Check and interpret

Exponential form: \(10^4=10{,}000\). Each additional zero in the argument adds 1 to the base-10 logarithm.

Q4

Explain why neither expression represents a real number. Use the exponential form in your reasoning.

\[\log_2(0)\quad\text{and}\quad\log_2(-8)\]
Answer

Neither is defined: no real exponent makes \(2^c\) equal to 0 or to a negative number.

  1. Translate each statement into exponential form with an unknown exponent c.

    \[2^c=0\quad\text{or}\quad 2^c=-8\]

  2. For every real c, a positive base produces a positive output. Large negative exponents make the output small but still positive.

    \[2^{-10}=\frac{1}{1024}>0\]

  3. The equations have no real solution, so the logarithms are undefined. The argument of a real logarithm must be positive.

Check and interpret

Compare with \(\log_2(8)=3\), which is defined because \(8>0\) and \(2^3=8\). Changing the sign of the argument removes the solution.

Q5

Find the base b. Explain why only one value is acceptable.

\[\log_b(49)=2\]
Answer

\(b=7\)

  1. Rewrite in exponential form.

    \[b^2=49\]

  2. Solve the equation; two real numbers square to 49.

    \[b=7\quad\text{or}\quad b=-7\]

  3. The base of a logarithm must be positive and different from 1, so reject the negative value.

    \[b=7\]

Check and interpret

Substitute: \(\log_7(49)=2\) because \(7^2=49\). A base of \(-7\) is not allowed in the definition.

Exponential and logarithmic forms

Every exponential statement has a logarithmic twin. In \(b^c=a\) the base is \(b\), the exponent is \(c\), and the result is \(a\). In \(\log_b(a)=c\) the base stays as the subscript, the result moves inside the parentheses as the argument, and the exponent becomes the value of the logarithm.

When converting, keep the base in its place and remember that the logarithm equals the exponent. This works for negative exponents, fractional exponents, and expressions with variables.

Practice: try these five questions

Q6

Rewrite the equation in logarithmic form. Identify the base, exponent, and argument.

\[6^3=216\]
Answer

\(\log_6(216)=3\)

  1. Identify the parts: base 6, exponent 3, result 216.

  2. Keep the base as the subscript, place the result inside, and set the logarithm equal to the exponent.

    \[\log_6(216)=3\]

Check and interpret

Read it aloud: 6 raised to the power 3 gives 216. Both forms describe the same fact.

Q7

Rewrite the equation in logarithmic form. Keep the sign of the exponent.

\[10^{-3}=0.001\]
Answer

\(\log_{10}(0.001)=-3\)

  1. Identify the parts: base 10, exponent −3, result 0.001.

  2. The logarithm equals the exponent, including its negative sign.

    \[\log_{10}(0.001)=-3\]

Check and interpret

A negative logarithm means the argument is between 0 and 1. Indeed \(0<0.001<1\).

Q8

Rewrite the equation in exponential form.

\[\log_7(343)=3\]
Answer

\(7^3=343\)

  1. Identify the parts: base 7, logarithm (exponent) 3, argument 343.

  2. Raise the base to the exponent to obtain the argument.

    \[7^3=343\]

Check and interpret

Compute: \(7\cdot7\cdot7=49\cdot7=343\). The exponential statement is true.

Q9

Rewrite the equation in exponential form. Then explain what the fractional exponent means.

\[\log_8(2)=\frac{1}{3}\]
Answer

\(8^{1/3}=2\)

  1. Identify the parts: base 8, exponent 1/3, argument 2.

  2. Raise the base to the exponent.

    \[8^{1/3}=2\]

  3. An exponent of 1/3 is a cube root, so the statement says the cube root of 8 is 2.

    \[\sqrt[3]{8}=2\]

Check and interpret

Cube the result: \(2^3=8\). The fractional exponent undoes the cube.

Q10

Rewrite the first equation in exponential form and the second in logarithmic form. Treat the variables as the exponent or argument they represent.

\[\log_b(y)=x+1\qquad\text{and}\qquad 5^{2t}=w\]
Answer

\(b^{x+1}=y\) and \(\log_5(w)=2t\)

  1. In the first equation the whole expression x + 1 is the exponent, so it sits on the base b.

    \[b^{x+1}=y\]

  2. In the second equation the base is 5, the exponent is 2t, and the result is w.

    \[\log_5(w)=2t\]

Check and interpret

Each pair keeps the same base. The logarithm equals the entire exponent, whether it is a number or an expression.

Evaluating logarithms without a calculator

To evaluate \(\log_b(a)\) exactly, rewrite the argument as a power of the base. Positive powers give positive logarithms, an argument of 1 gives 0, fractions with the base in the denominator give negative logarithms, and roots give fractional logarithms.

When the argument is not an obvious power of the base, write both the base and the argument as powers of a smaller common number. Then set the exponents equal and solve for the unknown exponent.

Practice: try these five questions

Q11

Evaluate exactly.

\[\log_4(64)\]
Answer

\(\log_4(64)=3\)

  1. Write the argument as a power of the base.

    \[64=4\cdot4\cdot4=4^3\]

  2. The logarithm is the exponent.

    \[\log_4(4^3)=3\]

Check and interpret

Exponential form: \(4^3=64\).

Q12

Evaluate exactly. Show the negative exponent.

\[\log_5\!\left(\frac{1}{125}\right)\]
Answer

\(\log_5\!\left(\frac{1}{125}\right)=-3\)

  1. Write the denominator as a power of 5.

    \[125=5^3\]

  2. A reciprocal is a negative exponent.

    \[\frac{1}{125}=\frac{1}{5^3}=5^{-3}\]

  3. The logarithm is the exponent.

    \[\log_5(5^{-3})=-3\]

Check and interpret

Exponential form: \(5^{-3}=\frac{1}{125}\). An argument between 0 and 1 gives a negative logarithm.

Q13

Evaluate exactly. Show the fractional exponent.

\[\log_{27}(3)\]
Answer

\(\log_{27}(3)=\frac{1}{3}\)

  1. Ask: 27 raised to what power gives 3? Since \(3^3=27\), the cube root of 27 is 3.

    \[27^{1/3}=\sqrt[3]{27}=3\]

  2. The logarithm is the exponent.

    \[\log_{27}(3)=\frac{1}{3}\]

Check and interpret

Cube both sides of \(27^{1/3}=3\): \(27=27\). A fractional logarithm indicates a root.

Q14

Evaluate exactly by writing the base and the argument as powers of the same number.

\[\log_8(32)\]
Answer

\(\log_8(32)=\frac{5}{3}\)

  1. Write both numbers as powers of 2.

    \[8=2^3,\qquad 32=2^5\]

  2. Set up the exponential form with unknown exponent c.

    \[(2^3)^c=2^5\;\Longrightarrow\;2^{3c}=2^5\]

  3. Equal bases require equal exponents.

    \[3c=5\;\Longrightarrow\;c=\frac{5}{3}\]

Check and interpret

\(8^{5/3}=(8^{1/3})^5=2^5=32\). The answer is between 1 and 2, which fits because \(8<32<64\).

Q15

Evaluate exactly. The base is a fraction.

\[\log_{1/2}(16)\]
Answer

\(\log_{1/2}(16)=-4\)

  1. Set up the exponential form.

    \[\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^c=16\]

  2. Write both sides as powers of 2.

    \[2^{-c}=2^4\]

  3. Equal bases require equal exponents.

    \[-c=4\;\Longrightarrow\;c=-4\]

Check and interpret

\(\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-4}=2^4=16\). With a base between 0 and 1, an argument greater than 1 gives a negative logarithm.

Logarithmic functions: domain and graphs

The function \(f(x)=\log_b(x)\) is the inverse of \(y=b^x\). Its domain is \(x>0\), its range is all real numbers, its graph passes through \((1,0)\) and \((b,1)\), and the y-axis \(x=0\) is a vertical asymptote. For \(b>1\) the graph increases; for \(0<b<1\) it decreases.

To find the domain of a logarithm with an expression inside, require the argument to be positive and solve that inequality. The vertical asymptote sits where the argument equals zero.

Practice: try these five questions

Q16

Make a table of values for x = 1/3, 1, 3, 9, and 27. Then state the x-intercept and the vertical asymptote, and sketch the graph.

\[f(x)=\log_3(x)\]
Answer

Outputs \(-1,\,0,\,1,\,2,\,3\); x-intercept \((1,0)\); vertical asymptote \(x=0\).

  1. Write each input as a power of 3; the logarithm is the exponent.

    \[\tfrac{1}{3}=3^{-1},\;1=3^0,\;3=3^1,\;9=3^2,\;27=3^3\]

  2. Record the outputs in order.

    \[f\!\left(\tfrac13\right)=-1,\;f(1)=0,\;f(3)=1,\;f(9)=2,\;f(27)=3\]

  3. The output is 0 at x = 1, so the x-intercept is (1, 0). As x approaches 0 from the right, the outputs decrease without bound, so x = 0 is the vertical asymptote.

Check and interpret

Each time x is multiplied by 3, the output increases by exactly 1. This constant step is the signature of a logarithm.

Q17

State the domain and the equation of the vertical asymptote.

\[g(x)=\log_2(x-5)\]
Answer

Domain \(x>5\); vertical asymptote \(x=5\).

  1. The argument of a logarithm must be positive.

    \[x-5>0\]

  2. Solve the inequality.

    \[x>5\]

  3. The asymptote is where the argument equals zero.

    \[x-5=0\;\Longrightarrow\;x=5\]

Check and interpret

Test x = 6: \(g(6)=\log_2(1)=0\), defined. Test x = 5: \(\log_2(0)\) is undefined. The graph of \(\log_2(x)\) has shifted 5 units right.

Q18

State the domain in inequality form.

\[h(x)=\log(7-2x)\]
Answer

Domain \(x<\frac{7}{2}\)

  1. Require a positive argument.

    \[7-2x>0\]

  2. Subtract 7 from both sides.

    \[-2x>-7\]

  3. Divide by −2 and reverse the inequality sign.

    \[x<\frac{7}{2}\]

Check and interpret

Test x = 0: \(h(0)=\log(7)\), defined. Test x = 4: \(7-8=-1\), so \(\log(-1)\) is undefined. The domain lies to the left of the asymptote \(x=\frac72\).

Q19

The graph of y = 4ˣ passes through (3, 64). Name a point that must be on the graph of the inverse function, and explain the geometric relationship between the two graphs.

\[y=\log_4(x)\]
Answer

\((64,3)\) lies on \(y=\log_4(x)\); the two graphs are reflections across \(y=x\).

  1. An inverse function swaps inputs and outputs. The point (3, 64) on the exponential becomes (64, 3) on the logarithm.

    \[4^3=64\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\log_4(64)=3\]

  2. Swapping coordinates reflects every point across the line y = x, so the whole graph of the logarithm is the mirror image of the exponential graph.

Check and interpret

The exponential has horizontal asymptote \(y=0\) and y-intercept \((0,1)\); the logarithm has vertical asymptote \(x=0\) and x-intercept \((1,0)\). Each feature is the reflection of the other.

Q20

Compare the two functions: state whether each is increasing or decreasing, name a point they share, and explain how their outputs are related.

\[f(x)=\log_2(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad g(x)=\log_{1/2}(x)\]
Answer

f increases, g decreases; both pass through \((1,0)\); \(g(x)=-f(x)\) for every \(x>0\).

  1. The base of f is greater than 1, so f increases. The base of g is between 0 and 1, so g decreases.

  2. Both give 0 at x = 1, because any allowed base to the zero power is 1.

    \[f(1)=g(1)=0\]

  3. If \(g(x)=c\), then \((1/2)^c=x\), which is \(2^{-c}=x\). So \(-c=\log_2(x)\) and \(c=-\log_2(x)\).

    \[\log_{1/2}(x)=-\log_2(x)\]

Check and interpret

At x = 8: \(f(8)=3\) and \(g(8)=-3\) because \(\left(\tfrac12\right)^{-3}=8\). The graphs are reflections of each other across the x-axis.

Common and natural logarithms

Two bases have their own notation. The common logarithm \(\log(x)\) uses base 10, and the natural logarithm \(\ln(x)\) uses base \(e\approx2.71828\). Both follow the same definition: \(\log(x)=c\) means \(10^c=x\), and \(\ln(x)=c\) means \(e^c=x\).

Because logarithms and exponentials are inverses, \(10^{\log x}=x\) and \(e^{\ln x}=x\) for \(x>0\), while \(\log(10^x)=x\) and \(\ln(e^x)=x\) for every real \(x\). Calculators have keys for both logarithms, which makes them the usual tools for the change-of-base formula.

Practice: try these five questions

Q21

Evaluate both common logarithms exactly.

\[\log(100{,}000)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\log(0.0001)\]
Answer

\(\log(100{,}000)=5\) and \(\log(0.0001)=-4\)

  1. Write each argument as a power of 10.

    \[100{,}000=10^5,\qquad 0.0001=\frac{1}{10^4}=10^{-4}\]

  2. The common logarithm is the exponent on 10.

    \[\log(10^5)=5,\qquad\log(10^{-4})=-4\]

Check and interpret

Five zeros after the 1 give 5; four decimal places give −4. Arguments above 1 have positive common logs, and arguments between 0 and 1 have negative ones.

Q22

Evaluate both natural logarithms exactly.

\[\ln(e^7)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{e^2}\right)\]
Answer

\(\ln(e^7)=7\) and \(\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{e^2}\right)=-2\)

  1. The natural logarithm asks for the exponent on e.

    \[\ln(e^7)=7\]

  2. Rewrite the reciprocal with a negative exponent, then read the exponent.

    \[\frac{1}{e^2}=e^{-2}\;\Longrightarrow\;\ln(e^{-2})=-2\]

Check and interpret

No decimal approximation of e is needed, because each argument is already a power of e.

Q23

Simplify each expression without a calculator.

\[10^{\log 35}\qquad\text{and}\qquad e^{\ln 4.2}\]
Answer

\(10^{\log 35}=35\) and \(e^{\ln 4.2}=4.2\)

  1. \(\log 35\) is the exponent that 10 needs to produce 35. Raising 10 to that exponent returns 35.

    \[10^{\log 35}=35\]

  2. Likewise \(\ln 4.2\) is the exponent e needs to produce 4.2.

    \[e^{\ln 4.2}=4.2\]

Check and interpret

The exponential and the logarithm with the same base undo each other. Both arguments are positive, so each expression is defined.

Q24

Without a calculator, determine the two consecutive integers between which the value lies. Justify with powers of 10.

\[\log(4{,}500)\]
Answer

\(3<\log(4{,}500)<4\)

  1. Find the powers of 10 on either side of the argument.

    \[10^3=1{,}000<4{,}500<10{,}000=10^4\]

  2. Because the common logarithm is increasing, the logarithms keep the same order.

    \[\log(10^3)<\log(4{,}500)<\log(10^4)\]

  3. Evaluate the outer logarithms.

    \[3<\log(4{,}500)<4\]

Check and interpret

A calculator gives \(\log(4{,}500)\approx3.653\), which lies in the predicted interval. The number 4,500 has four digits, and its common log is between 3 and 4.

Q25

Rewrite the first equation in exponential form and the second in logarithmic form, then solve each for the variable.

\[\ln(x)=5\qquad\text{and}\qquad e^{2y}=9\]
Answer

\(x=e^5\) and \(y=\frac{\ln 9}{2}\)

  1. The natural logarithm has base e, so the exponential form raises e to the given value.

    \[\ln(x)=5\;\Longleftrightarrow\;x=e^5\]

  2. In the second equation the exponent is 2y and the result is 9.

    \[e^{2y}=9\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\ln(9)=2y\]

  3. Divide by 2 to isolate y.

    \[y=\frac{\ln 9}{2}\]

Check and interpret

Approximate: \(e^5\approx148.41\) and \(y\approx\frac{2.1972}{2}\approx1.099\). Then \(e^{2(1.099)}=e^{2.197}\approx9\).

Product and quotient properties

Because logarithms are exponents, they follow exponent rules. Multiplying powers adds exponents, so \(\log_b(MN)=\log_b(M)+\log_b(N)\). Dividing powers subtracts exponents, so \(\log_b\!\left(\frac{M}{N}\right)=\log_b(M)-\log_b(N)\). All arguments must be positive and the bases must match.

There is no rule for the logarithm of a sum or difference: \(\log_b(M+N)\) cannot be split. When a product is easier to evaluate in pieces, use the properties; when it is easier to multiply first, do that and evaluate once.

Practice: try these five questions

Q26

Evaluate using the product property, then confirm by multiplying first.

\[\log_3(27\cdot9)\]
Answer

\(\log_3(27\cdot9)=5\)

  1. Split the product into a sum of logarithms with the same base.

    \[\log_3(27)+\log_3(9)\]

  2. Evaluate each logarithm.

    \[3+2=5\]

Check and interpret

Multiply first: \(27\cdot9=243=3^5\), so \(\log_3(243)=5\). Both routes agree.

Q27

Evaluate using the quotient property, then confirm by dividing first.

\[\log_2\!\left(\frac{128}{4}\right)\]
Answer

\(\log_2\!\left(\frac{128}{4}\right)=5\)

  1. Split the quotient into a difference of logarithms.

    \[\log_2(128)-\log_2(4)\]

  2. Evaluate each logarithm.

    \[7-2=5\]

Check and interpret

Divide first: \(\frac{128}{4}=32=2^5\), so \(\log_2(32)=5\).

Q28

Use the given approximations to find each value to three decimal places. Do not use a calculator logarithm key.

\[\log_b(2)\approx0.431,\quad\log_b(3)\approx0.683;\qquad\text{find }\log_b(6)\text{ and }\log_b(1.5)\]
Answer

\(\log_b(6)\approx1.114\) and \(\log_b(1.5)\approx0.252\)

  1. Write 6 as a product of the known arguments and apply the product property.

    \[\log_b(6)=\log_b(2\cdot3)=\log_b(2)+\log_b(3)\approx0.431+0.683=1.114\]

  2. Write 1.5 as a quotient and apply the quotient property.

    \[\log_b(1.5)=\log_b\!\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)=\log_b(3)-\log_b(2)\approx0.683-0.431=0.252\]

Check and interpret

The values are consistent with base 5, where \(\log_5(6)\approx1.113\) and \(\log_5(1.5)\approx0.252\). A product gives a larger logarithm than either factor, and a quotient near 1 gives a small logarithm.

Q29

Write the expression as a single logarithm. State the restrictions on the variables.

\[\log_4(5)+\log_4(x)-\log_4(y)\]
Answer

\(\log_4\!\left(\dfrac{5x}{y}\right)\), with \(x>0\) and \(y>0\)

  1. Combine the sum into the logarithm of a product.

    \[\log_4(5x)-\log_4(y)\]

  2. Combine the difference into the logarithm of a quotient.

    \[\log_4\!\left(\frac{5x}{y}\right)\]

  3. Each original logarithm needs a positive argument, so x and y must both be positive.

Check and interpret

Test x = 4, y = 5: the original expression is \(\log_4(5)+1-\log_4(5)=1\), and the condensed form is \(\log_4(4)=1\).

Q30

Show by direct evaluation that the two expressions are not equal, and explain which property the mistake misuses.

\[\log_2(8+8)\quad\text{versus}\quad\log_2(8)+\log_2(8)\]
Answer

\(\log_2(8+8)=4\) but \(\log_2(8)+\log_2(8)=6\); the product property does not apply to sums.

  1. Evaluate the left side by adding first.

    \[\log_2(16)=4\]

  2. Evaluate the right side term by term.

    \[3+3=6\]

  3. A sum of logarithms equals the logarithm of a product, not of a sum.

    \[\log_2(8)+\log_2(8)=\log_2(8\cdot8)=\log_2(64)=6\]

Check and interpret

The expression \(\log_b(M+N)\) has no splitting rule. Only products, quotients, and powers inside a logarithm can be rewritten.

The power property

Raising to a power multiplies exponents, so \(\log_b(M^p)=p\log_b(M)\) for \(M>0\). An exponent inside the logarithm becomes a coefficient in front of it. The property works for negative exponents, which produce negative coefficients, and for roots, which are fractional exponents.

Used in reverse, a coefficient becomes an exponent: \(p\log_b(M)=\log_b(M^p)\). This is the key step when condensing expressions. Be careful: the property applies to a power of the whole argument, not to a power of the logarithm itself.

Practice: try these five questions

Q31

Evaluate using the power property, then confirm by computing the power first.

\[\log_5(25^4)\]
Answer

\(\log_5(25^4)=8\)

  1. Bring the exponent in front.

    \[4\log_5(25)\]

  2. Evaluate the remaining logarithm.

    \[\log_5(25)=2\]

  3. Multiply.

    \[4\cdot2=8\]

Check and interpret

Compute first: \(25^4=(5^2)^4=5^8\), so \(\log_5(5^8)=8\).

Q32

Evaluate using the power property.

\[\log_2(16^{-1})\]
Answer

\(\log_2(16^{-1})=-4\)

  1. Bring the exponent in front; it is negative.

    \[-\log_2(16)\]

  2. Evaluate the remaining logarithm.

    \[\log_2(16)=4\]

  3. Attach the sign.

    \[-4\]

Check and interpret

\(16^{-1}=\frac{1}{16}=2^{-4}\), so \(\log_2(2^{-4})=-4\).

Q33

Evaluate exactly. Write the root as a fractional exponent first.

\[\log_7\!\left(\sqrt[3]{49}\right)\]
Answer

\(\log_7\!\left(\sqrt[3]{49}\right)=\frac{2}{3}\)

  1. A cube root is an exponent of 1/3.

    \[\sqrt[3]{49}=49^{1/3}\]

  2. Bring the exponent in front.

    \[\frac{1}{3}\log_7(49)\]

  3. Evaluate the logarithm and multiply.

    \[\frac{1}{3}\cdot2=\frac{2}{3}\]

Check and interpret

\(7^{2/3}=(7^2)^{1/3}=49^{1/3}\), which matches the argument. The answer is between 0 and 1 because \(1<\sqrt[3]{49}<7\).

Q34

Use the power property in reverse to write each expression as a single logarithm with coefficient 1. Assume x > 0 and y > 0.

\[5\log_2(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1}{2}\ln(y)\]
Answer

\(\log_2(x^5)\) and \(\ln(\sqrt{y})\)

  1. Move the coefficient 5 into the exponent of the argument.

    \[5\log_2(x)=\log_2(x^5)\]

  2. Move the coefficient 1/2 into the exponent; an exponent of 1/2 is a square root.

    \[\frac{1}{2}\ln(y)=\ln(y^{1/2})=\ln(\sqrt{y})\]

Check and interpret

Test x = 2: \(5\log_2(2)=5\) and \(\log_2(2^5)=5\). Test y = e²: \(\frac12\ln(e^2)=1\) and \(\ln(e)=1\).

Q35

Use the given approximation to find each value to three decimal places.

\[\log_b(2)\approx0.431;\qquad\text{find }\log_b(32)\text{ and }\log_b\!\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)\]
Answer

\(\log_b(32)\approx2.155\) and \(\log_b\!\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)\approx-0.862\)

Expanding and condensing logarithmic expressions

Expanding turns one logarithm into several terms: work from the outside in, splitting quotients into differences, products into sums, and finally moving exponents in front as coefficients. Condensing reverses the process: move coefficients back into exponents first, then combine sums into products and differences into quotients.

Always keep the restrictions from the original expression. Condensing \(\ln(x-2)+\ln(x+1)\) gives \(\ln[(x-2)(x+1)]\), but the domain stays \(x>2\), because each original argument had to be positive.

Change of base

The change-of-base formula \(\log_b(a)=\dfrac{\log(a)}{\log(b)}=\dfrac{\ln(a)}{\ln(b)}\) rewrites any logarithm using a base a calculator has. The argument goes on top and the base on the bottom, and the same kind of logarithm must be used in both places.

Before trusting a decimal, check reasonableness by trapping the argument between two integer powers of the base. Change of base can also run in reverse: a quotient of two logarithms with the same base is a single logarithm.

    \[\log_4(64)=\frac{\log(64)}{\log(4)}\]

Solving logarithmic and exponential equations

To solve a logarithmic equation, first state the domain, then rewrite a single logarithm in exponential form or combine several logarithms into one before rewriting. If both sides are single logarithms with the same base, set the arguments equal. Check every candidate in the original equation and reject any that make an argument zero or negative.

To solve an exponential equation, isolate the power, then take a logarithm of both sides. The power property brings the variable exponent down as a coefficient. When the answer is not exact, trap it between two integer exponents to confirm the decimal is reasonable.

\(10^{2.5}\approx316\) times greater

  1. Rewrite each pH in exponential form to find the concentrations.

    \[[\mathrm{H}^+]_A=10^{-3},\qquad[\mathrm{H}^+]_B=10^{-5.5}\]

  2. Divide, subtracting exponents.

    \[\frac{10^{-3}}{10^{-5.5}}=10^{-3-(-5.5)}=10^{2.5}\]

  3. Approximate using \(10^{2.5}=10^2\cdot10^{0.5}=100\sqrt{10}\).

    \[100\sqrt{10}\approx100(3.1623)\approx316\]

Check and interpret

The pH difference of 2.5 is the logarithm of the concentration ratio, so the ratio is \(10^{2.5}\). It lies between \(10^2=100\) and \(10^3=1{,}000\).

W11

A town of 42,000 people grows 2.3% per year: P(t) = 42000(1.023)ᵗ. In how many years will the population reach 60,000? Round to one decimal place, then identify the first whole year in which the population exceeds 60,000 and show the two bracketing values.

\[P(t)=42000(1.023)^{t}\]
Answer

\(t\approx15.7\) years; the population first exceeds 60,000 in year 16.

  1. Isolate the power.

    \[(1.023)^t=\frac{60000}{42000}=\frac{10}{7}\approx1.428571\]

  2. Take the natural logarithm of both sides.

    \[t\ln(1.023)=\ln\!\left(\frac{10}{7}\right)\]

  3. Divide and approximate.

    \[t=\frac{0.356675}{0.022739}\approx15.7\]

  4. Bracket with whole years.

    \[P(15)\approx59{,}072,\qquad P(16)\approx60{,}431\]

Check and interpret

Year 15 is still below 60,000 and year 16 is above it, so the decimal answer of 15.7 years and the whole-year answer of 16 agree.

W12

A cup of tea cools according to T(t) = 20 + 70e^(−0.08t), where T is the temperature in degrees Celsius and t is minutes after it is poured. Find its initial temperature, then find when the tea reaches 45°C. Round the time to one decimal place.

\[T(t)=20+70e^{-0.08t}\]
Answer

Initial temperature 90°C; the tea reaches 45°C after about 12.9 minutes.

  1. Substitute t = 0; the exponential equals 1.

    \[T(0)=20+70(1)=90\]

  2. Set the model equal to 45 and isolate the exponential.

    \[70e^{-0.08t}=25\;\Longrightarrow\;e^{-0.08t}=\frac{25}{70}=\frac{5}{14}\]

  3. Rewrite in logarithmic form with base e.

    \[-0.08t=\ln\!\left(\frac{5}{14}\right)\approx-1.029619\]

  4. Divide by −0.08.

    \[t\approx\frac{-1.029619}{-0.08}\approx12.9\]

Check and interpret

Substitute: \(20+70e^{-0.08(12.87)}=20+70e^{-1.0296}\approx20+70(0.357)\approx45.0\). The temperature falls toward the room temperature of 20°C, never below it.

Quick questions and answers

What is a logarithm?

A logarithm is an exponent. The statement log base b of a equals c means b raised to the power c equals a. To evaluate one, ask which exponent the base needs in order to produce the argument.

Why must the argument of a logarithm be positive?

A positive base raised to any real exponent is always positive. No exponent produces zero or a negative result, so those arguments have no real logarithm. The base must also be positive and different from 1.

What is the difference between log and ln?

The common logarithm log(x) uses base 10, and the natural logarithm ln(x) uses base e, which is about 2.71828. Both follow the same definition and the same properties, and both appear on calculators.

Can I split the logarithm of a sum?

No. The product property turns a product inside into a sum outside, and the quotient property turns a quotient into a difference. There is no property for log of (M + N); evaluate the sum inside first.

When do I use the change-of-base formula?

Use it when the base is not 10 or e and the argument is not an obvious power of the base. Divide the logarithm of the argument by the logarithm of the base, using the same kind of logarithm in both places.

Why do I have to check solutions of logarithmic equations?

Combining logarithms can create candidate solutions that make an original argument zero or negative. Those values are outside the domain and must be rejected, even though they satisfy the rewritten equation.
