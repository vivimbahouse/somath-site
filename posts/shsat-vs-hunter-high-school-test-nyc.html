Short answer. The SHSAT is the entrance exam for the eight specialized NYC public high schools (Stuyvesant, Bronx Science, Brooklyn Tech, and five smaller schools). It is taken by 8th or 9th graders, is entirely multiple choice, and has a substantial Algebra 1 footprint. The Hunter test (HCHSEE) is the entrance exam for Hunter College High School alone. If they are in 6th grade or later, the SHSAT is the more realistic and wider net. Many families do both — Hunter first, then SHSAT if Hunter does not work out.

1. The two exams at a glance

The SHSAT and the Hunter College High School Entrance Exam are the two most competitive selective public school entrance exams in New York City. They both test math and language, and they both have a fearsome reputation. But they are very different exams, taken by different-aged students, with different consequences.

2. Format: multiple choice vs multiple choice + essay

SHSAT format

The SHSAT is entirely multiple choice, with the math section including some grid-in questions where students write in a numeric answer instead of picking from choices. There is no essay. The exam is administered on Chromebooks in most NYC test sites, and students can flag and revisit questions within a section. The pace is fast — roughly 90 seconds per question — but every question type is a pattern the student has seen before if they have practiced enough.

Hunter format

The Hunter test is a mix of multiple choice AND a written essay. The multiple-choice sections cover math and reading comprehension, and the essay is a substantial timed writing prompt (usually 30-45 minutes) that is graded on organization, argument, evidence, and mechanics. The essay changes the game entirely: a child who is strong at multiple-choice math and reading but a weak writer can be blocked from Hunter admission by the essay alone. Students preparing for Hunter must practice writing under timed conditions, from pre-writing outline through revision, in a way SHSAT-only students never do.

3. Math: pre-algebra vs Algebra 1

This is the single biggest difference for the math-focused parent. The SHSAT math section tests early Algebra 1 substantially — linear equations, systems of equations, inequalities, exponents, roots, quadratics in factored form, and word problems that require translating language into algebraic equations. A student who has not yet taken Algebra 1 can still score well on the SHSAT, but they will use guess-and-check on problems that Algebra 1 students solve in 20 seconds. That is a large disadvantage on a timed test.

The Hunter math section tests pre-algebra plus light early algebra — the focus is on arithmetic fluency, fractions, decimals, percents, ratios, proportions, and geometry, with variables appearing in simple linear equations. The Hunter test math is genuinely harder than most 6th-grade classwork, but it does not require the algebraic depth the SHSAT does. Students who took Algebra 1 early have an advantage on Hunter's harder math questions, but it is not the make-or-break advantage it is on the SHSAT.

The practical implication. A student preparing for both should start with a strong pre-algebra foundation in 4th-5th grade (for Hunter), then move into Algebra 1 in 6th or 7th grade (for SHSAT). A student who only takes Algebra 1 in 9th grade after the SHSAT is not competitive at Stuyvesant math. This is one of the strongest arguments for taking Algebra 1 before high school.

4. ELA: reading only vs reading + writing

The SHSAT ELA has two parts — a revising/editing section that tests grammar, sentence structure, paragraph organization, and rhetorical clarity in short passages, and a reading comprehension section with longer passages and detailed questions. Both are multiple choice. Strong SHSAT ELA prep is about pattern recognition on the revising/editing section and careful evidence-based reading on the comprehension side. There is no writing to do, only to grade.

The Hunter test asks students to write. The reading comprehension multiple-choice section is comparable in style to SHSAT reading (slightly less dense passages, similar question types), but the essay is a substantial writing task where the child must produce organized, evidence-supported, mechanically-clean prose under timed conditions. Preparing for the Hunter essay is a genuine writing pedagogy — outlining, thesis development, evidence integration, revision — and it takes months of practice, not weeks.

5. Scoring, cutoffs, and acceptance rates

SHSAT scoring & cutoffs

The SHSAT gives a composite score out of 700 (350 math + 350 ELA in the standard scaling). Students rank their preferred specialized high schools, and offers are made to the highest-scoring students who ranked each school. Cutoffs vary by year and by school. In recent years, roughly: Stuyvesant ~560-570, Bronx Science ~510-520, Brooklyn Tech ~480-495, and lower cutoffs for the five smaller specialized high schools. Only ~4,000 offers are made from ~26,000+ test takers — a raw acceptance rate around 15%, but far lower for Stuyvesant specifically.

Hunter scoring & acceptance

The Hunter test is scored on math, reading, and essay separately, then combined into an overall score. The scoring formulas and cutoffs are not published transparently, and there is no rank-order preference system — one school, one admission decision. Hunter admits roughly 175-185 students per year from about 3,000 pre-qualified 6th graders (the pre-qualification itself weeds out 90%+ of NYC 5th graders by state test scores). The effective acceptance rate is ~6% of pre-qualified applicants, which is under 1% of the total 5th-grade population in NYC.

7. Prep strategy: can one prep serve both?

The short answer: meaningfully yes on math, only partly on ELA. A student who has done serious Hunter math prep has already covered arithmetic, fractions, decimals, percents, ratios, proportions, and basic geometry — every core topic the SHSAT math section will test. The only additional area for the SHSAT is Algebra 1, which most students do not encounter until 7th or 8th grade anyway. So Hunter math prep is genuine SHSAT preparation on the arithmetic and pre-algebra side.

On the ELA side, the overlap is smaller. The Hunter reading comprehension section trains the same skills that carry over to the SHSAT reading section — careful reading, evidence-based inference, vocabulary in context. But the Hunter essay does not appear on the SHSAT, and the SHSAT revising/editing section (grammar and rhetoric on short passages) does not appear on the Hunter test. Students preparing for both need distinct ELA modules.

The natural strategy for a family targeting both:

Phase 1 — 4th-5th grade Foundation. Strong arithmetic, fractions, decimals, ratios. Read widely. Practice timed multiple choice on age-appropriate material. Begin structured Hunter prep in the summer before 6th grade if pre-qualification is achieved.
Phase 2 — 6th grade Hunter test in January. All-in on Hunter prep from September through test day — math depth, reading comprehension, essay technique. After the test, decompress for the rest of 6th grade.
Phase 3 — 7th grade Whether or not Hunter came through, begin pre-algebra to Algebra 1 transition. Start SHSAT diagnostic and weekly prep. Focus on the algebra fluency that will separate Stuyvesant scorers from the middle of the pack.
Phase 4 — 8th grade Intensive SHSAT prep summer through October. Weekly full-length timed sections. Weakness-driven review. Test day late October.

8. Which one is right for your child?

The honest decision framework:

  1. Age of the child. If your child is in 5th grade and testing in the top 5% on the NY State test, Hunter is worth the shot — the acceptance rate is brutal but the school is extraordinary. If your child is in 6th grade or later, the Hunter window is closed and SHSAT is the realistic path.
  2. Writing vs math strength. A strong writer plays to Hunter's essay-heavy scoring. A strong algebra student plays to the SHSAT's algebra footprint.
  3. School diversity of outcomes. The SHSAT gives access to eight schools with a range of cutoffs — a stronger candidate can aim for Stuyvesant, a slightly weaker one can still get into Brooklyn Tech or one of the smaller specialized schools. Hunter is one binary outcome.
  4. Family time and energy. Serious Hunter prep is 12-18 months of weekly work in 5th and 6th grade. Serious SHSAT prep is 12-18 months of weekly work in 7th and 8th grade. Doing both means 3+ years of continuous test prep. Some families choose one path just to reclaim the child's childhood.

10. FAQ

What is the difference between the SHSAT and the Hunter High School Test?

The SHSAT is the 8th/9th-grade entrance exam for eight specialized NYC public high schools and is entirely multiple choice. The Hunter test (HCHSEE) is the 6th-grade entrance exam for Hunter College High School (grades 7-12) and includes a written essay in addition to multiple choice. The SHSAT tests early Algebra 1; Hunter tests pre-algebra plus light early algebra.

Can my child prepare for both at the same time?

Yes, but sequenced: Hunter first (5th-6th grade), then SHSAT (7th-8th grade). Math prep overlaps meaningfully; ELA prep diverges.

Which is harder?

They are hard in different ways. Hunter is harder in raw selectivity (~6% of pre-qualified applicants); SHSAT is harder in the algebra depth and pace at the Stuyvesant cutoff.

When should my child start preparing?

Hunter prep: summer before 5th grade. SHSAT prep: start of 7th grade. Starting earlier does not help; starting later closes the door on tighter cutoffs.

Does the SHSAT have an essay?

No. The SHSAT is entirely multiple choice. Only the Hunter test has a written essay.

What math is on the SHSAT that is not on the Hunter test?

Substantial Algebra 1 — linear equations, systems, inequalities, exponents, roots, algebraic word problems. Hunter tests pre-algebra plus light early algebra.

If my child only has time to prep for one, which should I pick?

Age decides it. 5th grade and top 5%: Hunter is worth the shot. 6th grade or later: SHSAT is the realistic path and gives access to eight schools rather than one.
