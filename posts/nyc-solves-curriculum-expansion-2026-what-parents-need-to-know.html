What NYC Solves is and where it came from

NYC Solves launched in the 2022–2023 school year as New York City’s response to a well-documented problem: even within the same building, two 6th-grade classrooms might be using different math materials at different paces with different rigor. The initiative mandates that participating districts adopt one of a set of vetted high-quality instructional materials (HQIM) — curricula aligned to New York State Learning Standards — and that teachers receive professional development to implement them faithfully. The core premise: coherent curriculum, consistently delivered, outperforms an uncoordinated patchwork.

The evidence that drove the expansion is concrete. Bronx District 11 saw math proficiency among grades 3–8 students climb from 25.8% to 50.6% over three years of NYC Solves implementation. Bronx District 7 went from 18% to 42% in the same period, documented in a Columbia CPRL case study. These are not rounding errors; they represent tens of thousands of children reaching grade-level math who previously were not.

The three curricula NYC Solves districts use

NYC Solves does not mandate a single textbook citywide. Districts choose among three approved curricula, all rated “meets expectations” by EdReports. They share strong alignment to standards but feel quite different in the classroom.

Illustrative Mathematics (IM Math) Problem-based, discussion-heavy Students explore a “warm-up” problem before instruction; conceptual understanding precedes procedures Students who engage with the “why” of math; verbal processors; strong readers

Illustrative Mathematics (IM Math)

Illustrative Mathematics is the most widely adopted of the three across existing NYC Solves districts and the one most likely to appear at a D3 middle school. Lessons start not with instruction but with a short “warm-up” problem that activates prior knowledge; students then work in small groups on an activity that leads them to discover a concept before the teacher formalizes it. Homework problems are wordy, multi-step, and require written reasoning — nothing like a traditional drill sheet. The payoff is stronger conceptual understanding and the ability to handle novel problems. The trade-off is that procedural fluency builds more slowly than in traditional curricula, which we address honestly below.

i-Ready Mathematics Classroom

i-Ready Mathematics Classroom from Curriculum Associates pairs structured classroom lessons with the widely used i-Ready digital diagnostic. Many NYC students have already taken that diagnostic; the classroom curriculum is a distinct product. It is more explicitly structured than IM Math, with clearer direct-instruction components — a format teachers managing large, mixed-readiness classes often prefer.

Amplify Desmos Mathematics

Amplify Desmos Mathematics is the newest of the three in NYC adoption and the most technology-dependent. Built in partnership with the nonprofit Desmos studio — the team behind the graphing calculator used on most state tests — it is visually interactive and exploration-driven. Lessons run through digital activities on the Desmos platform, giving students early familiarity with a tool they will use on Regents and SAT exams. Consistent device access is a prerequisite worth confirming at your specific school.

The case for it: real evidence from NYC districts that have already implemented

Bronx District 11 adopted Illustrative Mathematics across grades 3–8 in 2022 and spent the first year in deep professional development. Math proficiency on state assessments rose from 25.8% to 50.6% over three years — a 25-point gain sustained across multiple cohorts, not a one-year anomaly, documented by Columbia’s Center for Public Research and Leadership (CPRL).

Bronx District 7 (Mott Haven, Melrose, Longwood) went from 18% to 42% proficiency in three years. A May 2026 Illustrative Mathematics case study identifies the key factors: a superintendent who protected implementation from shortcuts, instructional coaches who spent time in classrooms rather than offices, and a multi-year professional development cycle that treated teachers as learners.

Why these results are meaningful — and the asterisks parents should know about

The Bronx results are real, but honest context matters. D11 and D7 started from low baselines (18–26% proficiency), leaving more room to grow. District 3, with higher average proficiency, will not see a 25-point gain; the ceiling effect is real. The gains also reflect sustained, well-resourced implementation — coaches in classrooms, not just new textbooks distributed. Whether D3 schools receive that same quality of support depends on how the $17.3M is allocated. The evidence clearly supports: coherent curriculum reduces the worst-performing-classroom effect, and that floor-raising benefit applies even in higher-performing districts.

The honest parent concerns about standardized curriculum rollouts

“But my child is ahead — will this slow them down?”

This concern is partially valid. NYC Solves curricula are grade-level tools designed to bring struggling students to proficiency, not to extend already-proficient ones. A student who understands ratios fluently may find the 6th-grade IM Math ratios unit unchallenging. Extension problems exist within each lesson and strong teachers use them — but in a mixed-readiness class of 28–30, they are not always reached. One period of school math will not cap your child’s ceiling if the right enrichment is in place. See our guide on enrichment vs. tutoring on the UWS for how to think about the distinction.

“What about advanced math tracks?”

District 3 has historically offered less formal middle school acceleration than District 2, which has had accelerated sequences allowing students to complete Algebra I or Geometry before high school. NYC Solves standardizes the on-grade-level sequence but does not create or eliminate accelerated tracks. If your child is aiming for a rigorous high school math trajectory, that conversation needs to happen directly with the school’s math department. Our post on gifted math programs on the UWS covers the options.

“Less calculation practice?”

This is a legitimate critique. IM Math prioritizes conceptual understanding before procedures, meaning less class time on drilling multiplication facts or fraction algorithms. Critics — credible ones — argue that procedural fluency is a prerequisite for higher-order thinking, not something to defer. A child entering 6th grade without solid fact recall or fraction arithmetic will struggle because the curriculum assumes those skills and does not rebuild them. If your child has gaps there, summer work is genuinely valuable. See our 5th grade parent guide for the specific fluency targets to hit before middle school.

“Homework will look different — I won’t know how to help”

This concern is widely reported. IM Math problems are more verbal and open-ended than the “here is the rule, here are 20 problems” format most parents recognize. A typical problem: “A store sells juice in two sizes. The large costs 40% more. Is it always the better deal? Explain your reasoning.” Helping your child means engaging with the reasoning, not just checking a number. Free family resources are available at illustrativemathematics.org. The real solution is ensuring your child has a trusted adult — teacher, tutor, or enrichment instructor — who can work through the reasoning with them.

What UWS parents should actually do over summer 2026 to prepare

Stay enriched if your child is already ahead — but don’t overdo it

One to two weekly enrichment sessions over the summer keeps skills sharp and builds problem-solving habits that NYC Solves curricula reward. Five days a week of drill is counterproductive. Our SOMATH summer programs 2026 page lists specific options for UWS public school students.

Build problem-solving habits that IM Math actually rewards

IM Math rewards students who can explain their thinking in writing. If your child has never been asked to justify mathematical reasoning, this summer is the time to start. “Why does this work? Can you explain it differently?” asked over a kitchen-table problem beats any workbook. See our guide on when to start math enrichment for more on building these habits early.

Talk to your child’s teacher in the first two weeks of September

Every teacher implementing a new curriculum is in an adjustment period in September. Go to open school night. Email the math teacher with one specific question about how differentiation works within the new curriculum. Teachers at MS 54, MS 245, and MS 247 will be navigating their own learning curve; a curious, engaged parent is a much easier conversation than an adversarial one.

Frequently asked questions

Which UWS public schools are affected by the NYC Solves 2026 expansion?

Manhattan District 3 middle schools join NYC Solves in September 2026. D3 middle schools include MS 54 Booker T. Washington, MS 245 The Computer School, MS 247 Dual Language Middle School, MS 256, MS 258, and MS 297. The school-by-school participation list had not been released by NYCPS at the time of writing — check schools.nyc.gov/learning/subjects/math/nyc-solves for updates.

Will my child still be able to take honors or accelerated math at their middle school?

NYC Solves standardizes the curriculum framework, not whether a school offers accelerated tracks. Acceleration policies are set by individual schools. D3 has historically had limited formal middle school acceleration compared to D2 — that is a separate policy from NYC Solves adoption. Ask the math department directly in September. If your child needs more challenge than the standard track offers, supplemental enrichment outside school is the most reliable path.

Is NYC Solves replacing my child’s teacher’s existing curriculum?

Yes. NYC Solves requires participating schools to adopt one of three approved curricula and follow the standardized scope and sequence. Teachers retain discretion in how they facilitate lessons, but the materials and pacing are standardized. The goal is eliminating the wide variation that previously existed between classrooms in the same school.

Does NYC Solves use Illustrative Mathematics in District 3?

Districts choose among the three approved curricula. As of the May 2026 announcement, D3’s specific selection had not been disclosed publicly. Illustrative Mathematics is the most widely adopted across existing NYC Solves districts. Watch for a communication from the D3 superintendent’s office over the summer.

Does SOMATH align with the NYC Solves curriculum?

Yes. SOMATH’s small-group instruction reinforces and extends the conceptual, problem-based mathematics that all three NYC Solves curricula emphasize. We work from students’ current school assignments so sessions complement rather than conflict with what kids see in class. We also build the procedural fluency that IM Math develops more slowly than many parents prefer.

When does the new curriculum start in District 3 middle schools?

September 2026 — the first day of the 2026–2027 school year. There is no transition period. Students entering 6th, 7th, or 8th grade at a participating D3 middle school start on day one.

Will Brearley, Trinity, Columbia Prep, or private schools also adopt NYC Solves?

No. NYC Solves applies only to participating NYCPS public schools. Trinity, Collegiate, Brearley, Spence, Columbia Grammar & Preparatory, Dalton, and Riverdale Country make independent curriculum decisions. Private school families on the UWS are welcome at SOMATH; our work is calibrated to each student’s actual school curriculum.
