Understanding division

The dividend is the amount being divided, and the divisor is the number we divide by. The quotient can tell us the number of complete groups or the amount in each group, depending on the story. The remainder is what is left after making equal whole-number groups.

In this lesson, dividends are nonnegative whole numbers and divisors are positive single-digit numbers. A final remainder must be at least zero and smaller than the divisor. If enough objects remain to make another group, the division is not finished.

Practice: try these five questions

Q1

In 23 ÷ 4 = 5 R 3, name the dividend, divisor, quotient, and remainder.

Answer

Dividend 23; divisor 4; quotient 5; remainder 3

  1. We divide the 23 objects into groups of 4. Five complete groups use 4 × 5 = 20 objects, leaving 23 − 20 = 3. The remainder is valid because 3 < 4.

Q2

Make groups of 6 from 19 counters. How many complete groups can you make, and how many counters remain?

Answer

3 complete groups; 1 counter remains

  1. In the ones place, divide 19 by 6: write 3. Multiply 3 × 6 = 18, then subtract 19 − 18 = 1.
  2. No digits remain to bring down. The quotient is 3 and the remainder is 1. Check: 6 × 3 + 1 = 19, and 1 < 6.

Q3

Share 26 whole pencils equally among 4 children. How many does each child receive, and how many remain?

Answer

6 pencils each; 2 pencils remain

  1. In the ones place, divide 26 by 4: write 6. Multiply 6 × 4 = 24, then subtract 26 − 24 = 2.
  2. No digits remain to bring down. The quotient is 6 and the remainder is 2. Check: 4 × 6 + 2 = 26, and 2 < 4.
  3. Here the quotient means pencils per child, not the number of children. The divisor is the number of equal shares.

Q4

Can 7 objects make a complete group of 9? Give the quotient and remainder.

Answer

0 R 7

  1. No complete group of 9 can be made from only 7 objects. Therefore the quotient is 0 and all 7 objects remain. Check: 9 × 0 + 7 = 7, and 7 < 9.

Q5

A student writes 33 ÷ 5 = 5 R 8. Explain why this is not a finished answer, then correct it.

Answer

6 R 3

  1. Although 5 × 5 + 8 = 33, the remainder 8 is not smaller than 5. Make one more group from those 8 objects. This gives 6 groups and 3 remaining: 5 × 6 + 3 = 33.

Using multiplication to choose a quotient

Ask which multiple of the divisor is the greatest one that does not go past the amount you are dividing. For an exact division, that multiple equals the dividend. Otherwise, subtract it to find the remainder.

The next multiple should be too large. This checks that you have made as many complete groups as possible. During long division, use this same idea for each partial dividend.

Following the long-division steps

Work from left to right: divide, multiply, subtract, and bring down the next digit. Keep each quotient digit above the place value you are working with. If the first digit is smaller than the divisor, consider the first two digits together.

After subtracting, the amount left at that stage is smaller than the divisor, but you may still have digits to bring down. Only the amount left at the very end is the final remainder. Bringing down a digit means regrouping the amount left into the next smaller place value.

Dividing two-digit numbers exactly

An exact division ends with zero left over. A zero remainder means all the objects have been placed into equal groups; you may write the quotient without “R 0.”

Some examples let you divide the tens and ones separately. Others require regrouping a leftover ten into ones before continuing. Follow the place values, even when you expect an exact answer.

When no digits remain to bring down, record any amount left over as the remainder. The remainder must be smaller than the divisor; otherwise, one more complete group can still be made.

A correct answer passes two checks: divisor × quotient + remainder equals the dividend, and 0 ≤ remainder < divisor. Use both checks, not just multiplication.

Working across three-digit numbers

Use the same steps for hundreds, tens, and ones. A quotient digit belongs above the last digit of the partial dividend used at that step. Keep bringing down digits until every digit has been used.

A zero inside a quotient is important. If a place-value step makes no complete groups, write 0 in that position and continue. Leaving it out would move another digit into the wrong place and change the answer.

Checking and repairing an answer

Multiply the divisor by the quotient, then add the remainder. The result must equal the original dividend. Also compare the remainder with the divisor.

An answer may pass the multiplication-and-addition check but still have a remainder that is too large. In that case, form another complete group from the remainder and adjust the quotient. Missing zero digits can also make an otherwise familiar-looking answer wrong.

Deciding what a remainder means

Read what the story asks before deciding what to do with the remainder. A sharing problem may ask for an amount per person and the leftovers. A packing problem may ask only for complete groups.

If every person or object needs a place, a nonzero remainder often requires one more container or vehicle. Do not change the arithmetic quotient; interpret it in the final sentence. State what the quotient and the remainder count, and use the correct units.

Quick questions and answers

What do dividend, divisor, quotient, and remainder mean?

The dividend is the amount divided, the divisor is the number divided by, the quotient counts complete groups or the amount in each equal share, and the remainder is the amount left over.

What are the long-division steps?

Divide, multiply, subtract, and bring down the next digit. Work from left to right and repeat until no digits remain to bring down.

Why must the remainder be smaller than the divisor?

If the remainder is as large as the divisor, it can make another complete group. For positive divisors and nonnegative whole-number dividends, a valid final remainder satisfies 0 ≤ remainder < divisor.

How do I check an answer?

Multiply the divisor by the quotient and add the remainder. The result must equal the dividend. Also check that the remainder is nonnegative and smaller than the divisor.

When should a word-problem answer round up?

When every person or object needs a place and a nonzero remainder needs an additional vehicle, shelf, or container, add one to the number of complete groups. If the question asks only for full groups, do not round up.

Why does a quotient sometimes need a zero?

A zero holds a place where no complete groups can be made at that stage. For example, 612 divided by 3 is 204, not 24; the tens digit is zero.
