How to use this practice set

Sit with your child. Read each question out loud, then read the four choices A, B, C, D. Let the child point to or say their answer before tapping Show answer. The explanation under each answer is written so a parent can read it aloud — we never just give the number, we always show why.

All 20 questions stay inside the range 0–20, which is the official ceiling for the Common Core 1st grade operations and algebraic thinking standards (1.OA). They lean toward the medium end of difficulty: crossing 10, missing-addend problems, comparison, and short word problems. None of them require carrying with two-digit numbers — that comes in 2nd grade.

20 multiple-choice questions

Level: Mid · 1st grade

Question 1 of 20

What is 8 + 5?

  1. 12
  2. 13
  3. 14
  4. 15

Answer: B — 13

A great trick for crossing 10: break the 5 into 2 + 3. First do 8 + 2 = 10, then add the remaining 3 → 13. This "make a ten" strategy is the backbone of 1st grade addition.

Question 2 of 20

What is 14 − 6?

  1. 6
  2. 7
  3. 8
  4. 9

Answer: C — 8

Subtract by going back to 10 first: 14 − 4 = 10, then take away the remaining 2 → 8. Or use the addition fact you already know: 6 + 8 = 14, so 14 − 6 must be 8.

Question 3 of 20

Fill in the missing number: 7 + ? = 12

  1. 3
  2. 4
  3. 5
  4. 6

Answer: C — 5

A "missing addend" question is really a subtraction in disguise: 12 − 7 = 5. Or count up from 7 on your fingers until you reach 12: 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 — that's five jumps.

Question 4 of 20

What is 9 + 6?

  1. 14
  2. 15
  3. 16
  4. 17

Answer: B — 15

Use "make a ten." Split the 6 into 1 + 5. Then 9 + 1 = 10, plus 5 more → 15. The doubles+1 trick also works: 9 + 6 is one less than 10 + 6, which is 16, so the answer is 15.

Question 5 of 20

Word problem. Ana had 13 crayons. She gave 4 to her brother. How many crayons does Ana have now?

  1. 7
  2. 8
  3. 9
  4. 10

Answer: C — 9

"Gave away" is the keyword for subtraction (−). So 13 − 4 = 9. Going back to 10 first helps: 13 − 3 = 10, then take away 1 more → 9.

Question 6 of 20

Which number sentence is TRUE?

  1. 6 + 7 = 12
  2. 8 + 5 = 14
  3. 9 + 4 = 13
  4. 7 + 8 = 14

Answer: C — 9 + 4 = 13

Check each one. 6 + 7 = 13 (not 12). 8 + 5 = 13 (not 14). 9 + 4 = 13 — correct. 7 + 8 = 15 (not 14). True/false questions train careful reading and slow checking, two of the most useful first-grade habits.

Question 7 of 20

What is 11 − 4?

  1. 5
  2. 6
  3. 7
  4. 8

Answer: C — 7

Subtract back to 10: 11 − 1 = 10, then 10 − 3 = 7. Or use the addition fact: 4 + 7 = 11, so 11 − 4 = 7. Every subtraction has a matching addition fact.

Question 8 of 20

What is 6 + 6?

  1. 10
  2. 11
  3. 12
  4. 13

Answer: C — 12

This is a doubles fact. 1st graders should memorize the doubles: 1+1=2, 2+2=4, 3+3=6, 4+4=8, 5+5=10, 6+6=12, 7+7=14, 8+8=16, 9+9=18, 10+10=20. Doubles are the springboard for trickier problems — e.g., 6+7 is just 6+6 plus 1.

Question 9 of 20

Fill in the missing number: ? + 9 = 16

  1. 5
  2. 6
  3. 7
  4. 8

Answer: C — 7

Think 16 − 9 = 7. Or count up from 9 to 16 on your fingers: 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 — that's seven jumps. Missing-addend questions are the gateway to algebra: the "?" is the same idea as "x" in 7th grade.

Question 10 of 20

Word problem. There are 7 red apples and 6 green apples on the table. How many apples are there in all?

  1. 11
  2. 12
  3. 13
  4. 14

Answer: C — 13

"In all" means addition. So 7 + 6 = 13. Use doubles: 6 + 6 = 12, plus one more → 13. The vocabulary list to memorize for 1st grade addition words: in all, total, altogether, together, sum, plus, more.

Question 11 of 20

What is 15 − 7?

  1. 6
  2. 7
  3. 8
  4. 9

Answer: C — 8

Go to 10 first: 15 − 5 = 10, then 10 − 2 = 8. Or use the matching addition: 7 + 8 = 15, so 15 − 7 = 8. Every subtraction within 20 has a partner addition fact — the family of facts for 7, 8, 15 is: 7+8=15, 8+7=15, 15−7=8, 15−8=7.

Question 12 of 20

Which symbol goes in the box: 9 + 4  [ ? ]  6 + 8

  1. > (greater than)
  2. < (less than)
  3. = (equal to)
  4. None of the above

Answer: B — < (less than)

Solve both sides first: 9 + 4 = 13 and 6 + 8 = 14. Then compare: 13 < 14. A great way to remember the symbols: the wide end of < or > always opens toward the bigger number. The little point points at the smaller one.

Question 13 of 20

What is 10 + 7?

  1. 15
  2. 16
  3. 17
  4. 18

Answer: C — 17

"Ten and some more" is the easiest addition family in 1st grade and the foundation for place value. 10 + 7 = 17 means one ten and seven ones — literally what the digits in "17" tell you. Mastering 10 + n speeds up every other addition.

Question 14 of 20

What is 12 − 5?

  1. 6
  2. 7
  3. 8
  4. 9

Answer: B — 7

Go to 10: 12 − 2 = 10, then 10 − 3 = 7. Or use the addition partner: 5 + 7 = 12, so 12 − 5 = 7. Counting back on fingers also works: start at 12 and go back five — 11, 10, 9, 8, 7.

Question 15 of 20

Word problem. Leo has 5 toy cars. His friend Maya has 8 toy cars. How many more toy cars does Maya have than Leo?

  1. 2
  2. 3
  3. 4
  4. 13

Answer: B — 3

"How many more" is a comparison question, which is subtraction: 8 − 5 = 3. Maya has 3 more cars than Leo. Watch out for choice D (13) — that would be the total number of cars, not the difference. Comparison problems are a classic 1st grade trap.

Question 16 of 20

What is 7 + 7?

  1. 12
  2. 13
  3. 14
  4. 15

Answer: C — 14

Another doubles fact: 7 + 7 = 14. Once doubles are memorized, "near-doubles" become easy: 7 + 8 = 14 + 1 = 15; 7 + 6 = 14 − 1 = 13. Doubles unlock at least 20 other facts.

Question 17 of 20

Fill in the missing number: 13 − ? = 6

  1. 5
  2. 6
  3. 7
  4. 8

Answer: C — 7

The "?" is the amount taken away. We need 13 − ? = 6, which means ? = 13 − 6 = 7. Check: 13 − 7 = 6. ✓ Another way: count from 6 up to 13 — 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 — that's 7 jumps, so we took away 7.

Question 18 of 20

What is 3 + 4 + 5?

  1. 11
  2. 12
  3. 13
  4. 14

Answer: B — 12

Adding three numbers is a 1st grade standard (1.OA.2). The smart move is to look for a pair that makes 10 first: 5 + 5 doesn't fit here, but 3 + 4 = 7, then 7 + 5 = 12. Or rearrange: 3 + 5 = 8, then 8 + 4 = 12. Addition can be done in any order — that's the commutative property.

Question 19 of 20

Word problem. A bus has 9 children on it. At the next stop, 6 more children get on. How many children are on the bus now?

  1. 13
  2. 14
  3. 15
  4. 16

Answer: C — 15

"Get on" means add (more children join). So 9 + 6 = 15. Use "make a ten": split 6 into 1 + 5; then 9 + 1 = 10, plus 5 → 15. The "9 + n" family is one of the most useful sets of facts to overlearn in 1st grade.

Question 20 of 20

Challenge. Which expression is equal to 16?

  1. 9 + 6
  2. 8 + 8
  3. 7 + 8
  4. 20 − 5

Answer: B — 8 + 8

Solve each one: 9 + 6 = 15. 8 + 8 = 16 (doubles fact). 7 + 8 = 15. 20 − 5 = 15. Only B equals 16. This kind of "match the value" question shows that the same number can be written in many ways — the beginning of the idea of equivalent expressions, which becomes central in algebra.

How to coach a 1st grader through these

Three habits from our Upper West Side classroom:

  1. Always ask "how did you decide?" Even when the answer is right. A 1st grader who can explain "I broke the 5 into 2 and 3 so I could make a ten" is building real fluency. A 1st grader who only says "I just knew it" is still guessing.
  2. Use the addition family for every subtraction. 13 − 5 is hard. But "5 + ? = 13" feels easier to most kids. Remind them: every subtraction problem has a partner addition fact. Find the partner, then answer.
  3. Catch the wrong-operation trap in word problems. When a problem says "how many more," young students often add instead of subtract. Have your child circle the key phrase and say out loud whether it means + or − before doing any math.

If your child gets stuck on a question:

  • "Can you draw it — with circles, sticks, or a number line?"
  • "Is there a way to make a ten first?"
  • "What's the matching addition (or subtraction) fact for this one?"

If those three don't unlock it, the issue is a missing prerequisite — usually the addition facts to 10, or the meaning of an operation word. That's exactly what we diagnose in our free 60-minute evaluation.
