The Best Activity for College Admissions and Scholarships: Why Math Beats Sports, Chess, and Camp

A data-backed guide for Manhattan parents: why math enrichment is the most predictable path to college admission, merit scholarships, and career success, and how it compares with baseball, swimming, chess, music, and summer camp.

The short answer: if you want one activity that most predictably improves your child’s chances of college admission and merit scholarships, choose serious math enrichment. Math is the one area where effort today can be traced, almost line by line, to outcomes that colleges and scholarship committees actually measure: course level, SAT and ACT math scores, AP Calculus, PSAT/NMSQT results, and math competitions. Baseball, swimming, chess, and summer camp can be wonderful for a child. But their path to an admission letter or a scholarship check is narrow, crowded, and very hard to predict. Math enrichment is the investment you can plan, measure, and compound.

The question every Manhattan parent is asking

Parents now type a version of the same question into Google and into AI assistants: “What is the best activity my child can do to have better chances in college and scholarships?” It is a fair question, and the usual answers are frustratingly vague. “Follow their passion.” “Colleges want well-rounded students.” “Do what makes them happy.” All of that is kind. None of it helps you decide between Tuesday baseball practice, Thursday swim team, Saturday chess club, an eight-week sleepaway camp, or a math class.

So let me reframe the question the way a mathematician would. For each activity, ask three things:

  1. Is the outcome measurable? Does the activity produce something an admissions officer or scholarship committee can read, compare, and trust?
  2. Is the path predictable? If my child works hard at it for five years, how confident can I be that the work will show up in the application?
  3. Does it compound? Does each year of effort make the next year easier and more valuable, or does it reset every season?

When you score activities against those three questions, math stands alone. Not because sports, music, or chess lack value, but because math is the only activity where nearly every hour of serious work becomes visible on a transcript, on a test score, and later in a career. Everything else is, in the best sense of the word, imponderable: it may lead somewhere wonderful, but you cannot draw the line in advance.

Why math is the most predictable investment

Math has a property no other childhood activity has: a fixed, nationally shared ladder. A child who is solid in arithmetic can move to pre-algebra. A child who masters pre-algebra can start Algebra 1. Algebra 1 opens Geometry and Algebra 2, which open Pre-Calculus, which opens Calculus. Everyone, from a public school counselor in Manhattan to an admissions reader at Stanford, knows where each rung sits.

That shared ladder is why a math head start is so valuable. A child who reaches Algebra 1 in seventh or eighth grade has room for Calculus, and often AP Statistics or multivariable math, before graduation. A child who arrives in high school behind has no room to catch up; the ladder simply runs out of years. A national study of high school counselors found that most advise students aiming at selective colleges to take at least one calculus course, ideally AP, and that many believe selective colleges implicitly expect calculus even when they do not formally require it (“Calculating the Odds,” ERIC). The same report notes that counselors increasingly see colleges expecting courses beyond introductory calculus.

Compare that with sports. In baseball, a child’s progress depends on growth spurts, which coach is running the team, whether a shoulder holds up, and how many other strong players happen to be in the same graduating class. Those are real factors, and none of them are in your family’s control. In math, the main variables are instruction quality and consistent practice. Those are almost entirely in your control.

That is what I mean by predictability. When a family commits to strong math enrichment from early elementary school, we can describe with reasonable confidence where that child is likely to be at 14 and at 17: further up the ladder than peers who are not studying math outside school, with test scores to prove it. No responsible coach can promise anything comparable about a varsity roster or a recruiting call.

The advantage is unusually large because most children are not pursuing it

There is a second reason math enrichment pays off so reliably: relatively few children do it seriously, and the national baseline is low. In 2024, only 39 percent of American fourth graders performed at or above the NAEP Proficient level in mathematics, and 24 percent scored below NAEP Basic (The Nation’s Report Card). In a field where most children are not reaching proficiency, a child who studies math deliberately every week does not have to be a prodigy to stand out. The same hour that produces a marginal gain in a crowded sport can produce a large relative gain in math.

Think about the competition. According to the National Federation of State High School Associations, a record 8,266,244 students participated in high school sports in 2024–25 (NFHS). Your child’s athletic résumé is being compared with millions of others. Your child’s Algebra 1 grade in seventh grade, AMC 10 score in ninth grade, and AP Calculus score in eleventh grade are compared with a much smaller pool of students who put in that same deliberate work.

What the research says about math

I do not want you to take this on faith. Here is what some of the strongest research says.

Early math is the strongest predictor of later achievement. In one of the most cited studies in education research, Greg Duncan and colleagues analyzed six large longitudinal data sets. Across all six, the strongest predictors of later achievement were school-entry math, reading, and attention skills, and a meta-analysis showed that early math skills had the greatest predictive power, ahead of reading and then attention (Duncan et al., Developmental Psychology, 2007). Early math predicted later reading too, not just later math. That is the opposite of what most people expect.

Advanced math courses are linked to higher earnings a decade later. Economists Heather Rose and Julian Betts used detailed high school transcripts to estimate how math courses relate to earnings about ten years after graduation. They found that more advanced courses had larger effects, and estimated that an additional year of calculus is associated with roughly 19.5 percent higher earnings, compared with about 8 percent for an additional year of algebra or geometry, even after accounting for family background, school characteristics, final degree, and college major. Their conclusion is blunt: “math matters” (Rose and Betts, Review of Economics and Statistics).

Test scores predict college success better than high school grades. Researchers at Opportunity Insights studied Ivy-Plus colleges and found that, among students with the same high school grades, those with an SAT of 1600 or ACT of 36 earned first-year college GPAs 0.43 points higher than students with a 1200 SAT or a 25 ACT. A perfect high school GPA, by contrast, predicted less than 0.1 points of difference compared with a 3.2 (Opportunity Insights, 2024). In a world of grade inflation, standardized math scores are among the few signals colleges still trust.

Elite colleges specifically point to math. When MIT reinstated its testing requirement, its dean of admissions wrote that considering SAT and ACT performance, “particularly the math section,” substantially improves how well MIT predicts student success, likely because of “the centrality of mathematics” in an MIT education, where every student must pass two semesters of calculus (MIT Admissions). At Dartmouth, faculty researchers found that the SAT by itself was the best predictor of first-year GPA, and similarly strong across demographic subgroups (Dartmouth Admissions).

Standardized tests are back, and math leads the way

For a few years after 2020, many families were told tests no longer mattered. That era is ending. As of October 2026, six of the eight Ivy League schools require the SAT or ACT for the 2026–27 cycle: Brown, Cornell, Dartmouth, Harvard, Penn, and Yale. MIT, Caltech, Stanford, Georgetown, and Johns Hopkins require scores as well, and Columbia and Princeton have announced requirements beginning with the 2027–28 cycle (10exam testing tracker; Dewey Smart tracker). Harvard was explicit when it returned to required testing for the Class of 2029 (AP via WFRV).

Policies can change, so always confirm on each college’s admissions website. But the direction is clear. For a child in first through eighth grade today, it is safest to assume that test scores, and particularly math scores, will matter when they apply.

One more point about tests. The SAT has two sections, Reading and Writing, and Math. Reading and writing skills grow from years of reading at home and at school; many Upper West Side children are strong readers. Math is where scores tend to separate, and where structured preparation makes the clearest difference, because the content is a defined body of skills that can be taught, practiced, and mastered.

The honest math of sports

I love that children play sports. Sports teach teamwork, resilience, and physical health, and I would never tell a family to stop. But if the reason for a heavy athletic commitment is “it will help with college” or “maybe a scholarship,” the numbers deserve a careful look.

The NCAA publishes its own estimates of how many high school athletes go on to compete in college. Of more than eight million high school athletes, only about 550,000 will compete at NCAA schools (NCAA). For the sports Upper West Side families most often ask me about, the odds look like this:

Read that carefully. Even in baseball, one of the more favorable sports, more than 91 of every 100 high school players never play in college at any NCAA level, and about 97 of 100 never reach Division I. And playing in college is not the same as receiving money. Reporting that draws on scholarship data puts the share of high school athletes who earn an NCAA Division I or II athletic scholarship at under 2 percent, with full rides far rarer still; Division III programs offer no athletic scholarships at all (Durango Herald). Many athletic awards are partial, and one guide estimates the average at about $8,700 a year (CollegeHelpGuide).

There is also a quieter cost. A structured review of the research on early sport specialization found it associated with higher injury risk in several youth athlete populations, particularly overuse injuries, along with concerns about burnout, stress, and reduced time away from sport, though the findings vary by sport and study (PMC review, 2026). A fifteen-hour-a-week club swimming schedule can be a joyful choice. It is rarely a reliable admissions strategy.

What about recruited athletes at Ivy-Plus colleges? Opportunity Insights found that athletic recruitment is real and that it disproportionately benefits students from the highest-income families: the share of admitted students who are recruited athletes rises from 5 percent for families in the bottom 60 percent of income to 13 percent for the top 1 percent (Opportunity Insights, 2023). Those spots typically go to athletes in sports with specialized, expensive pipelines, and they are filled years in advance. For most children, even very athletic ones, this is not a plan. It is a lottery ticket.

Chess, music, and the myth of automatic transfer

Many families choose chess or music because they have heard these activities “make kids smarter” or “are basically math.” I understand why. Chess is full of calculation and patterns. Music is full of ratios and rhythm. But the research is more sobering than the folklore.

Psychologists Giovanni Sala and Fernand Gobet ran meta-analyses of experimental studies on chess and music instruction for children. They found small to moderate effects on cognitive and academic skills overall, but the effects shrank as study quality improved. When children were compared with active control groups, the effect of music instruction fell to essentially zero, about 0.03 standard deviations. Their conclusion: “far transfer of learning rarely occurs” (Sala and Gobet, Current Directions in Psychological Science). Some studies do report benefits of chess for math, particularly when chess replaces part of math instruction (review in SciELO), but the strongest controlled evidence does not support chess or music as a substitute for learning math.

The lesson for parents is simple and, I think, freeing. If you want your child to be better at math, the most efficient path is to study math. Chess makes children better at chess. Piano makes children better at piano. Both are worth doing for their own sake. But neither is a back door to Algebra 2.

The reverse direction is more promising

Here is where I will make my own argument, as an educator rather than as a summary of any single study. Mathematics trains habits that serve almost every other demanding pursuit: holding several steps in mind, checking whether an answer makes sense, recognizing structure in something unfamiliar, and persisting when the first attempt fails. Children who learn these habits early tend to bring them into the chess club, the orchestra, the science lab, and the student council. I see it every week at SOMATH. A child who has proved that \(n^2 + n = n(n+1)\) is always even has learned what it feels like to know something is true for every case. That feeling travels.

We should be careful not to overclaim. Research rarely shows that one skill transfers automatically to another. But math is the foundation of the activities that do have clear payoffs: physics, engineering, computer science, economics, data science, medicine, and finance. Music theory, chess openings, and debate strategy are all more accessible to a child who already thinks in structures. That is why I describe math as the one activity that develops the mind for many others, rather than the other way around.

Camps and the imponderables

Summer camp, “base camp” adventures, travel programs, and short enrichment experiences can create lovely memories. Some build independence. Many are expensive. But almost none produce anything a college application can show. There is no score, no level, no national comparison, and no clear next step the following summer. When a parent asks me whether a particular camp will help with college, my honest answer is: you cannot know, and neither can the camp.

That is the key distinction of this whole guide. Most activities are imponderable. Their benefit might be huge or might be nothing, and you cannot trace a line from today’s effort to tomorrow’s outcome. With math, you can. A summer spent closing a gap in fractions, starting algebra early, or preparing for the AMC 8 shows up the very next school year, in class, on the report card, and on the next test.

Where scholarship money actually comes from

Parents often picture scholarships as athletic awards. In reality, merit money at many universities is tied directly to test scores and GPA, and it can be read off a published chart. As of October 2026, one analysis counted 63 colleges that publish merit grids linking awards to SAT or ACT scores and GPA, with 340 separate tiers (Reach research).

The University of Alabama is a clear example. Its automatic scholarships for out-of-state and international freshmen are based on test scores and GPA and require no separate application (University of Alabama):

Look at the jump from 1350 to 1360: $15,000 to $24,000 a year, or $36,000 over four years, for a handful of SAT points. Because math is the section where preparation most reliably moves scores, those points are often math points. Compare that certainty with the odds in Table 4.

The National Merit Scholarship Program works the same way. Entry is through the PSAT/NMSQT, usually taken in eleventh grade. About 1.3 million juniors enter each year and about 50,000 earn scores high enough for recognition (College Board). The Selection Index doubles the Reading and Writing score and adds the Math score, so math counts for one third of the index. For strong readers, it is often the difference between recognition and not. National Merit standing also unlocks scholarships at many universities. Math and career success

College is not the finish line. The real question is what your child can do at 25, 35, and 50. Here the case for math is even stronger, because the highest-paying and fastest-growing occupations lean heavily on quantitative skills.

Even outside STEM, math is the gatekeeper. Economics, finance, consulting, medicine, public policy, and law all reward quantitative fluency. For twenty years, my co-founder Marcelo and I prepared adults for the quantitative sections of the GMAT and GRE on their way to top business schools. Again and again, the candidates who struggled were not less intelligent. They were bright adults who had stopped taking math seriously at 14 and were now trying to rebuild algebra at 28, under pressure, with careers on the line. The cheapest, easiest time to build that foundation is childhood.

The Rose and Betts estimate bears repeating: an extra year of calculus is associated with roughly 19.5 percent higher earnings a decade later, even after controlling for degree and major (Rose and Betts). No youth sport has a comparable, documented, population-wide link to adult earnings.

How math enrichment builds the whole mind

Let me return to the idea that math develops the brain for many other things, including music, STEM, chess, and leadership. Here is how I think about it as a teacher.

Leadership is a good example. Young leaders are not simply the loudest children in the room. They are the ones who can break a messy problem into parts, explain a plan clearly, and stay calm when the first plan fails. Those are exactly the habits that a good math class rehearses every week. A child who has spent years presenting solutions to peers, defending a step, and accepting a correction has practiced leadership in miniature.

Math also gives children something rare in modern childhood: honest feedback. In many activities, praise is generous and standards are vague. In mathematics, an answer is right or wrong, and a proof works or it does not. Children who grow up with that clarity learn to separate effort from results and feedback from judgment. That is invaluable later, whether they are running a lab, a company, or a team.

Competitions: the activity that is also math

If your child loves competition, math offers its own arena. The Mathematical Association of America’s pathway starts with the AMC 8, AMC 10, and AMC 12; strong performers are invited to the AIME and then to the USAJMO or USAMO. For the 2026–27 season, the AMC 10 and 12 were scheduled for November 5 and November 13, 2026 (OnePrep), and the USAJMO and USAMO for March 20–21, 2027 (EduAvenues). Unlike a varsity spot, an AIME qualification is a national, objective result that admissions readers immediately understand. Our AMC 10 guide and AMC 8 guide explain how NYC students prepare.

Questions parents ask

What is the best activity for my child to improve college admission and scholarship chances?

For most children, serious math enrichment. It produces measurable results colleges and scholarship programs use, including course level, SAT/ACT Math, AP Calculus, PSAT/NMSQT, and math competitions, and its progress is far more predictable than sports, camps, or most other activities. No single activity guarantees admission or scholarships.

Should my child quit baseball, swimming, or chess?

No. Sports, music, and chess have real value for health, friendship, and joy. The point is priority: protect two to four hours of math each week first, then size other activities so they do not crowd it out, especially if the main reason for them is college.

Does chess or music make children better at math?

Not reliably. High-quality meta-analyses by Sala and Gobet found that the effects of chess and music training on academic skills shrink to minimal or zero when compared with active control groups. If the goal is stronger math, the most efficient path is to study math directly.

Are athletic scholarships a realistic goal?

For very few families. The NCAA estimates that only about 550,000 of more than eight million high school athletes compete at NCAA schools, and fewer than 2 percent of high school athletes receive athletic scholarships, many of them partial. Merit scholarships tied to test scores and grades are available to far more students.

When should we start math enrichment?

As early as first grade. Research by Duncan and colleagues found that early math skills are the strongest predictor of later achievement. Starting early also creates room to reach Algebra 1 by eighth grade and Calculus in high school.
