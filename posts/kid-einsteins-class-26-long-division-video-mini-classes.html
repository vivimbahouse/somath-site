Mini Class 1: Long division with and without remainders

In this video

  • Setting up a long-division problem: divisor outside the bracket, dividend inside
  • Dividing, multiplying, subtracting, and bringing down, one digit at a time
  • Solving problems with no remainder
  • Understanding what a remainder means

A long-division problem has three parts. The dividend is the total being divided and goes inside the bracket; the divisor is the size of each group and goes outside; the quotient is the answer and is written on top, one digit at a time. In the video, Marcelo divides three-digit numbers by one-digit numbers using the same four moves for every digit: divide, multiply, subtract, bring down.

Start at the left. Ask how many times the divisor fits into the first digit, write that digit on top, multiply, subtract, and bring down the next digit. Repeat until no digits remain. When the last subtraction leaves 0, the division is exact, as in 648 ÷ 3 = 216. When something is left that is smaller than the divisor, that number is the remainder: 725 ÷ 4 = 181 R 1 means 4 groups of 181 use 724, with 1 left over. A remainder must always be smaller than the divisor; if it is not, the quotient digit was too small.

Worked example from the video: 648 ÷ 3

  1. Start with the first digit, 6. 3 fits into 6, so the first quotient digit goes in the hundreds place.
  2. Divide 6 by 3: write 2 in the hundreds place. Multiply 2 × 3 = 6, then subtract 6 − 6 = 0. Bring down the 4.
  3. Divide 4 by 3: write 1 in the tens place. Multiply 1 × 3 = 3, then subtract 4 − 3 = 1. Bring down the 8 to make 18.
  4. Divide 18 by 3: write 6 in the ones place. Multiply 6 × 3 = 18, then subtract 18 − 18 = 0.
  5. No digits remain to bring down. The quotient is 216 and the remainder is 0. Check: 3 × 216 = 648.

So 648 ÷ 3 = 216.

Mini Class 2: What if the first digit is too small?

In this video

  • Recognizing when the divisor does not fit into the first digit
  • Starting with the first two digits instead
  • Following the divide, multiply, subtract, bring-down steps
  • Solving problems with and without remainders

Sometimes the divisor is larger than the first digit of the dividend. In 324 ÷ 6, the divisor 6 does not fit into 3. Do not write 0 on top and do not skip the digit: simply use the first two digits together. Ask how many times 6 fits into 32, write that digit above the 2, and continue as usual.

Because you started with two digits, the quotient has one digit fewer than the dividend. 324 ÷ 6 = 54 and 456 ÷ 8 = 57 both have two-digit answers. Everything else is unchanged: multiply, subtract, bring down, and read any leftover as the remainder, as in 325 ÷ 6 = 54 R 1. Place your first quotient digit carefully; writing it one column too far left makes the answer ten times too large.

Worked example from the video: 324 ÷ 6

  1. 6 does not fit into 3, so use the first two digits, 32. The first quotient digit goes in the tens place.
  2. Divide 32 by 6: write 5 in the tens place. Multiply 5 × 6 = 30, then subtract 32 − 30 = 2. Bring down the 4 to make 24.
  3. Divide 24 by 6: write 4 in the ones place. Multiply 4 × 6 = 24, then subtract 24 − 24 = 0.
  4. No digits remain to bring down. The quotient is 54 and the remainder is 0. Check: 6 × 54 = 324.

So 324 ÷ 6 = 54.

Mini Class 3: Long division with two-digit divisors

In this video

  • Setting up division with a two-digit divisor
  • Choosing and checking each quotient digit
  • Multiplying, subtracting, and bringing down
  • Recognizing when an answer has a remainder

A two-digit divisor such as 12, 15, or 24 follows exactly the same steps as a one-digit divisor. The difference is the multiplication facts: you may not know 24 × 3 instantly, so choose a trial digit, multiply it by the divisor, and check. If the product is larger than the number you are dividing, the digit is too big; if what is left after subtracting is as large as the divisor, the digit is too small.

In 336 ÷ 12, the divisor 12 does not fit into 3, so start with 33. It fits 2 times (24), leaving 9; bring down the 6 to make 96, and 12 fits exactly 8 times. So 336 ÷ 12 = 28. A short list of the divisor's multiples, written beside your work, makes every trial digit quick to find. Remainders work the same way: 347 ÷ 15 = 23 R 2 because 15 × 23 = 345 and 2 is left over.

Worked example from the video: 336 ÷ 12

  1. 12 does not fit into 3, so use the first two digits, 33. The first quotient digit goes in the tens place.
  2. Divide 33 by 12: write 2 in the tens place. Multiply 2 × 12 = 24, then subtract 33 − 24 = 9. Bring down the 6 to make 96.
  3. Divide 96 by 12: write 8 in the ones place. Multiply 8 × 12 = 96, then subtract 96 − 96 = 0.
  4. No digits remain to bring down. The quotient is 28 and the remainder is 0. Check: 12 × 28 = 336.

So 336 ÷ 12 = 28.

Mini Class 4: Five-digit numbers divided by two-digit divisors

In this video

  • Dividing five-digit numbers by two-digit numbers
  • Choosing and checking each quotient digit
  • Knowing when to write a zero in the quotient
  • Multiplying, subtracting, and bringing down
  • Identifying and writing a remainder

Larger dividends do not need new rules, only more cycles of divide, multiply, subtract, bring down. The key is keeping every digit in the correct column. In 24,864 ÷ 24, the divisor fits into 24 exactly once, leaving 0. Bring down the 8: 24 does not fit into 8, so you must write 0 in the quotient before bringing down the next digit. Skipping that zero would turn the correct answer 1,036 into 136.

Write a quotient digit for every dividend digit after the first group, even when that digit is 0. In 35,729 ÷ 32, the digits come one at a time (1, 1, 1, 6) and the final subtraction leaves 17, so 35,729 ÷ 32 = 1,116 R 17. Finish every problem with a check: multiply the divisor by the quotient, add the remainder, and compare with the dividend.

Worked example from the video: 24,864 ÷ 24

  1. 24 does not fit into 2, so use the first two digits, 24. The first quotient digit goes in the thousands place.
  2. Divide 24 by 24: write 1 in the thousands place. Multiply 1 × 24 = 24, then subtract 24 − 24 = 0. Bring down the 8.
  3. 24 does not fit into 8, so write 0 in the hundreds place. Bring down the 6 to make 86.
  4. Divide 86 by 24: write 3 in the tens place. Multiply 3 × 24 = 72, then subtract 86 − 72 = 14. Bring down the 4 to make 144.
  5. Divide 144 by 24: write 6 in the ones place. Multiply 6 × 24 = 144, then subtract 144 − 144 = 0.
  6. No digits remain to bring down. The quotient is 1,036 and the remainder is 0. Check: 24 × 1,036 = 24,864.

So 24,864 ÷ 24 = 1,036.

Quick questions and answers

What are the four steps of long division?

Divide, multiply, subtract, bring down. Ask how many times the divisor fits, write that digit on top, multiply it by the divisor, subtract, and bring down the next digit. Repeat until no digits remain.

What do I do when the first digit is smaller than the divisor?

Use the first two digits together. In 324 ÷ 6, 6 does not fit into 3, so divide 32 by 6 and write the first quotient digit above the 2. Do not write a 0 above the 3.

How is a two-digit divisor different?

The steps are identical; only the multiplication facts are larger. Choose a trial digit, multiply it by the divisor, and adjust: lower the digit if the product is too big, raise it if the leftover is as large as the divisor. A list of the divisor's multiples helps.

When do I write a zero in the quotient?

Whenever you have brought a digit down and the divisor does not fit into the number you now have. Write 0 on top, then bring down the next digit. In 24,864 ÷ 24 the quotient is 1,036; skipping the zero would give the wrong answer 136.

How do I check an answer with a remainder?

Confirm that the remainder is smaller than the divisor, then multiply the divisor by the quotient and add the remainder. The result must equal the dividend. For 347 ÷ 15 = 23 R 2, check 15 × 23 + 2 = 347.
