Choosing a Tutor

Choosing a Math Tutor on the Upper West Side: a parent's guide.

Kumon, Russian Math, Mathnasium, private 1:1, small group — what each is genuinely good at, where each falls short, and how to actually pick.

School of Math April 17, 2026 10 min read

Within fifteen blocks of our space on West 79th, you can find half a dozen math tutoring options at every price point. I get asked all the time — by parents at the playground, by neighbors, by friends — which one is best.

The honest answer is that "best" depends entirely on what your child needs right now. A program that's perfect for a 5th grader who needs arithmetic fluency is wrong for an 11th grader prepping for the SAT. A program that builds great reasoning skills can be the wrong call if your child is missing fact fluency.

So instead of telling you which to pick, let me give you the framework I'd give a friend. I'm going to be fair to every option here — these are all real programs that work for real kids. The question is matching.

Kumon — what it's good for

Kumon is the worksheet-and-drill program. The student completes daily packets at home, plus a couple of short sessions per week at the center. It's structured, repetitive, and predictable.

Where it shines: arithmetic fact fluency (times tables, addition without thinking), procedural smoothness with fractions and decimals, and the habit of daily math practice. For a 2nd through 5th grader who is slow with arithmetic and needs the muscle built, Kumon does this very well. The repetition works.

Where it doesn't: reasoning, word problems, conceptual understanding, anything where the question is "why" rather than "what." Kumon is, by design, a fluency program. It's not trying to build mathematicians. So if your child can already compute fluently and is hitting walls on word problems or geometry reasoning, Kumon won't move that needle. It also doesn't prep for the SHSAT, the SAT, or AP Calculus.

Who it's right for: elementary students who need the foundation drilled in. Less helpful past 6th grade unless there's a specific fluency gap.

Russian Math — what it's good for

The Russian Math School (and similar programs that use the Russian curriculum) is rigorous, methodical, and several grade levels ahead of typical US public school math. It's curriculum-driven and group-paced.

Where it shines: deep conceptual development. Russian Math takes its time on number sense, algebraic thinking, and geometric reasoning. Kids who stick with it for years often emerge with genuinely strong mathematical foundations — much stronger than school provides.

Where it doesn't: the pacing is the pacing. If your child is behind the cohort, catching up inside the program is hard. If your child is ahead, the cohort holds them back. It's also relatively impersonal — large groups, curriculum-first, less responsive to individual diagnostic needs. And it's not test-prep oriented; it teaches math, not the SAT or SHSAT.

Who it's right for: motivated kids whose parents commit to it long-term, starting in early elementary, who want a strong foundation rather than test scores. Less ideal as a fix-it for a kid behind in school or prepping for a specific test in the next 12 months.

Mathnasium — what it's good for

Mathnasium is the storefront drop-in model. Your child shows up two or three times a week, gets paired with whatever instructor is on shift, works through Mathnasium's curriculum on whatever topics they're behind on.

Where it shines: flexibility, accessibility, and broad coverage. If your 4th grader needs help with the weekly homework and you want a place to send them after school where someone will sit with them, Mathnasium does this well. It's a homework support program with a curriculum attached.

Where it doesn't: depth. The instructors are often college students or recent graduates without specialized training in math pedagogy. They rotate, so your child rarely works with the same person twice. There's no single teacher building a long-term picture of your child's mathematical thinking. For elite test prep — SHSAT, SAT, AP — most parents I've talked to have not seen the kind of score movement they hoped for.

Who it's right for: elementary and middle schoolers who need ongoing homework support and modest enrichment. Less ideal for high-stakes test prep or for kids who need a teacher who actually knows them.

Private 1:1 tutors — what they're good for

The private tutor route — typically $100 to $300 an hour in Manhattan — is the most personalized option. Your child works one-on-one with a single tutor, weekly.

Where it shines: individual attention, flexibility, and (with the right tutor) genuine depth. A great 1:1 tutor can move scores faster than any other format for the right student.

Where it doesn't: quality is wildly variable and you have no way to evaluate it from the outside. Many private tutors are college students or recent graduates; some are excellent, many are not. Even strong tutors often lack a curriculum — sessions can drift into "do your homework together," which is not the same as building skills. There's no peer environment, which matters more than parents think (kids learn from watching other kids work). And the cost is high — sustained 1:1 tutoring runs $400–$1,200 a month for one weekly hour.

Who it's right for: students with very specific, narrow needs — a particular SAT score gap, a challenging AP teacher to navigate, a single subject they're drowning in. If you find a great one, hold onto them.

Small-group, university-level instruction (where SOMATH lives)

This is the format Vivianne and I built SOMATH around, so I'll be transparent: I'm biased here. But I'll explain the thinking and you can decide.

Small-group means three to six students working with a single teacher who knows each of them — what they understand, where they get stuck, what their handwriting looks like when they're confused. That continuity matters. It's why our students don't have to re-explain themselves every week.

University-level means the teachers are not college kids working part-time. They are educators with graduate-level training in math or education. I have a B.S. in Applied Math from Northwestern and an MBA; Vivianne has an M.Ed. from Harvard. Our other teachers are similarly credentialed. Math is hard to teach well. The teacher matters more than the worksheet.

Where it shines: kids get the cost-efficiency of group with the diagnostic depth of private. They learn from each other (the second-best way to learn math is to watch another student get it right; the best is to explain it yourself). And test prep — SHSAT, SAT, AP Calculus — works better in group format because the shared timing pressure mirrors the real test.

Where it doesn't: if your child has highly unusual needs (severe math anxiety, a specific learning difference that requires a specialist, or scheduling constraints that don't fit our hours), 1:1 is likely a better fit. We'll tell you this honestly during the evaluation.

So how should you actually pick?

Start with the question your child needs answered. It's usually one of these:

Match the program to the actual question

  • "My elementary kid is slow at arithmetic and we need fluency." Kumon, daily home practice, or our Little Newtons (1–5) track if you want depth alongside the fluency.
  • "My 6th–8th grader is solid at school but I want them genuinely strong at math." Russian Math (long-term commitment) or our Kid Einsteins (6–8) track for Pre-Algebra and Algebra I depth.
  • "My 7th–8th grader is targeting Stuyvesant or Bronx Science." A specialized SHSAT program. Mathnasium and Russian Math will not get you there. Look for SHSAT-specific prep.
  • "My high schooler needs to move an SAT score from 620 to 740." Either a strong 1:1 SAT tutor or a small-group SAT-specific program with diagnostic-driven sessions.
  • "My kid is in AP Calc and needs help." A teacher who actually teaches AP Calc — not a general tutor.
  • "My kid hates math and we need to repair the relationship." A patient teacher in a small-group setting with peers, not a worksheet program.

Three questions to ask any program before you sign up

Whether you end up at Kumon, Russian Math, Mathnasium, a private tutor, or with us, ask these three things before you commit:

  1. Will my child have the same teacher every session? If the answer is "we rotate" or "whoever's on shift," that's a flag for serious work.
  2. Will you give me a written diagnostic before we start? Without one, the program is teaching whatever the curriculum says, not what your child needs.
  3. How will I know if it's working? Specific answer, with metrics, or it's not a real plan.

Any program worth your money should answer all three with a straight face.

The best math program is the one that actually answers the question your child needs answered. Not the one with the most marketing.
