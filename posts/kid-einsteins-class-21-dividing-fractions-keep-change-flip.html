How many groups fit?

Division asks a question: how many groups of one size fit inside an amount? The problem 12 ÷ 3 asks how many 3s fit in 12. The problem 3 ÷ \(\frac{1}{2}\) asks the same kind of question: how many halves fit in 3?

Picture the juice in the image. Each whole cup fills two \(\frac{1}{2}\)-cup servings. Three cups fill 3 × 2 = 6 servings, so 3 ÷ \(\frac{1}{2}\) = 6. The answer is bigger than 3 because the servings are smaller than 1 cup, and many small groups fit.

With a unit fraction, there is a quick pattern: every whole holds as many pieces as the denominator. One whole holds 4 fourths, so 5 wholes hold 5 × 4 = 20 fourths: 5 ÷ \(\frac{1}{4}\) = 20.

Q1

A bag holds 4 cups of rice. A scoop holds \(\dfrac{1}{3}\) cup. How many scoops of rice are in the bag?

Answer

12 scoops

  1. The question asks how many \(\frac{1}{3}\)-cup scoops fit in 4 cups: 4 ÷ \(\frac{1}{3}\).
  2. Each cup holds 3 thirds.
  3. 4 cups hold 4 × 3 = 12 thirds, so there are 12 scoops.

Q2

Find 2 ÷ \(\dfrac{1}{4}\). Explain your answer using wholes and fourths.

Answer

8

  1. 2 ÷ \(\frac{1}{4}\) asks how many fourths fit in 2 wholes.
  2. Each whole has 4 fourths.
  3. 2 wholes have 2 × 4 = 8 fourths, so 2 ÷ \(\frac{1}{4}\) = 8.

Q3

A rope is 5 meters long. It is cut into pieces that are each \(\dfrac{1}{2}\) meter long. How many pieces are there?

Answer

10 pieces

  1. Count how many half-meters fit in 5 meters: 5 ÷ \(\frac{1}{2}\).
  2. Each meter makes 2 half-meter pieces.
  3. 5 meters make 5 × 2 = 10 pieces.

Q4

Write a division sentence for this question, then solve it: How many \(\dfrac{1}{6}\)s fit in 3?

Answer

3 ÷ \(\dfrac{1}{6}\) = 18

  1. “How many \(\frac{1}{6}\)s fit in 3” is 3 ÷ \(\frac{1}{6}\).
  2. Each whole holds 6 sixths.
  3. 3 wholes hold 3 × 6 = 18 sixths, so 3 ÷ \(\frac{1}{6}\) = 18.

Q5

Which is greater, 4 ÷ \(\dfrac{1}{2}\) or 4 ÷ \(\dfrac{1}{4}\)? Explain why without only computing.

Answer

4 ÷ \(\dfrac{1}{4}\) = 16 is greater than 4 ÷ \(\dfrac{1}{2}\) = 8

  1. 4 ÷ \(\frac{1}{2}\) counts halves in 4 wholes: 4 × 2 = 8.
  2. 4 ÷ \(\frac{1}{4}\) counts fourths in 4 wholes: 4 × 4 = 16.
  3. Fourths are smaller pieces than halves, so more of them fit. 16 > 8.

Counting jumps on a number line

A number line turns division into counting jumps. To find \(\frac{3}{4}\) ÷ \(\frac{1}{4}\), start at 0 and jump \(\frac{1}{4}\) at a time: \(\frac{1}{4}\), \(\frac{1}{2}\), \(\frac{3}{4}\). Three jumps reach \(\frac{3}{4}\), so \(\frac{3}{4}\) ÷ \(\frac{1}{4}\) = 3.

When both fractions have the same denominator, the pieces are the same size, so you can simply divide the numerators. \(\frac{3}{4}\) ÷ \(\frac{1}{4}\) is 3 fourths ÷ 1 fourth = 3 ÷ 1 = 3, just like 3 apples ÷ 1 apple = 3.

The jumps do not have to be one piece long. To find \(\frac{6}{8}\) ÷ \(\frac{2}{8}\), each jump covers 2 eighths. Six eighths make 6 ÷ 2 = 3 jumps.

6 jumps

  2. Count jumps of \(\frac{1}{3}\): \(\frac{1}{3}\), \(\frac{2}{3}\), 1, \(\frac{4}{3}\), \(\frac{5}{3}\), 2.

Answer

3

  1. The pieces are the same size, tenths.
  2. 6 tenths ÷ 2 tenths = 6 ÷ 2.
  3. \(\frac{6}{10}\) ÷ \(\frac{2}{10}\) = 3 jumps.

When only part of a group fits

Sometimes the last group is not full. In the image, \(\frac{3}{4}\) cup is three quarter-cups. A \(\frac{1}{2}\)-cup serving uses two quarters. Two quarters fill 1 serving, and the last quarter fills half of another serving. So \(\frac{3}{4}\) ÷ \(\frac{1}{2}\) = \(1\frac{1}{2}\) servings.

A quotient can be a mixed number. The whole-number part counts full groups, and the fraction part tells what part of one more group is filled.

A reliable method is to rename both fractions with the same denominator, then divide the numerators. \(\frac{3}{4}\) ÷ \(\frac{1}{2}\) = \(\frac{3}{4}\) ÷ \(\frac{2}{4}\) = 3 ÷ 2 = \(\frac{3}{2}\) = \(1\frac{1}{2}\).

Q11

Find \(\dfrac{5}{8}\) ÷ \(\dfrac{1}{4}\). Rename \(\dfrac{1}{4}\) in eighths first.

Answer

\(2\dfrac{1}{2}\)

  1. \(\frac{1}{4}\) = \(\frac{2}{8}\), so the problem is \(\frac{5}{8}\) ÷ \(\frac{2}{8}\).

  3. \(\frac{4}{3}\) = \(1\frac{1}{3}\).

Q13

You have \(\dfrac{1}{2}\) cup of yogurt. Each serving is \(\dfrac{1}{3}\) cup. How many servings can you make?

\(1\dfrac{1}{2}\) servings

Q14

\(2\dfrac{1}{2}\) groups

  1. \(\frac{1}{3}\) = \(\frac{2}{6}\), so each group is 2 sixths.

Q15

A pitcher has \(\dfrac{7}{10}\) liter of lemonade. Each glass holds \(\dfrac{1}{5}\) liter. How many glasses can be filled? What part of another glass is filled?

Answer

3 full glasses and \(\dfrac{1}{2}\) of another (\(3\dfrac{1}{2}\) glasses)

  1. \(\frac{1}{5}\) = \(\frac{2}{10}\), so the problem is \(\frac{7}{10}\) ÷ \(\frac{2}{10}\) = 7 ÷ 2.
  2. 7 ÷ 2 = 3 remainder 1, so 3 glasses are full.
  3. The remaining \(\frac{1}{10}\) liter is half of a \(\frac{2}{10}\)-liter glass: \(3\frac{1}{2}\) glasses.

Why flipping works

Two numbers are reciprocals when their product is 1. The reciprocal of \(\frac{2}{3}\) is \(\frac{3}{2}\), because \(\frac{2}{3}\) × \(\frac{3}{2}\) = \(\frac{6}{6}\) = 1. To find a reciprocal, swap the numerator and the denominator. The reciprocal of 4 (which is \(\frac{4}{1}\)) is \(\frac{1}{4}\).

Multiplying both numbers in a division by the same amount does not change the answer. For example, 6 ÷ 2 = 3, and doubling both gives 12 ÷ 4 = 3. The number of groups stays the same.

So multiply both numbers by the reciprocal of the divisor. In the image, \(\frac{3}{4}\) ÷ \(\frac{2}{3}\) becomes (\(\frac{3}{4}\) × \(\frac{3}{2}\)) ÷ (\(\frac{2}{3}\) × \(\frac{3}{2}\)) = \(\frac{9}{8}\) ÷ 1. Dividing by 1 changes nothing, so the answer is \(\frac{9}{8}\) = \(1\frac{1}{8}\). That is why “flip and multiply” works: the divisor turns into 1, and the dividend is multiplied by the flipped fraction.

\(\dfrac{5}{3}\), \(\dfrac{1}{4}\), and 7

What number times \(\dfrac{5}{6}\) equals 1?

\(\dfrac{6}{5}\)

  1. Divisor: \(\frac{2}{3}\) × \(\frac{3}{2}\) = \(\frac{6}{6}\) = 1.

\(1\dfrac{1}{6}\)

  1. The reciprocal of \(\frac{3}{4}\) is \(\frac{4}{3}\). Divisor: \(\frac{3}{4}\) × \(\frac{4}{3}\) = 1.
  2. Dividend: \(\frac{7}{8}\) × \(\frac{4}{3}\) = \(\frac{28}{24}\) = \(\frac{7}{6}\).
  3. \(\frac{7}{6}\) ÷ 1 = \(\frac{7}{6}\) = \(1\frac{1}{6}\).

Q20

Explain why \(\dfrac{2}{5}\) ÷ \(\dfrac{1}{3}\) gives the same answer as \(\dfrac{6}{5}\) ÷ 1. Then find the answer.

Answer

Both numbers were multiplied by 3; the answer is \(1\dfrac{1}{5}\)

  1. The reciprocal of \(\frac{1}{3}\) is 3. Multiply both numbers by 3.
  2. \(\frac{2}{5}\) × 3 = \(\frac{6}{5}\) and \(\frac{1}{3}\) × 3 = 1, so the problem becomes \(\frac{6}{5}\) ÷ 1.
  3. Scaling both numbers by the same factor keeps the quotient the same: \(\frac{6}{5}\) = \(1\frac{1}{5}\).

Keep, Change, Flip

Keep-Change-Flip is the short way to write what you learned in the last topic. Keep the first fraction. Change ÷ to ×. Flip the second fraction (the divisor) to its reciprocal. Then multiply across and simplify.

In the image, \(\frac{5}{6}\) ÷ \(\frac{2}{3}\) = \(\frac{5}{6}\) × \(\frac{3}{2}\) = \(\frac{15}{12}\). Both 15 and 12 can be divided by 3, so \(\frac{15}{12}\) = \(\frac{5}{4}\) = \(1\frac{1}{4}\).

Only the divisor is flipped. The first fraction is the amount you have, and it stays exactly as it is. You may simplify before multiplying: in \(\frac{5}{6}\) × \(\frac{3}{2}\), divide 3 and 6 by 3 to get \(\frac{5}{2}\) × \(\frac{1}{2}\) = \(\frac{5}{4}\).

Q25

Dividing a mixed number

Before you divide, change each mixed number to an improper fraction. Multiply the whole number by the denominator and add the numerator: \(2\frac{1}{4}\) = \(\frac{2 \times 4+1}{4}\) = \(\frac{9}{4}\). That is 9 quarter-yards of ribbon.

Then use Keep-Change-Flip as usual. In the image, \(\frac{9}{4}\) ÷ \(\frac{3}{4}\) = \(\frac{9}{4}\) × \(\frac{4}{3}\) = \(\frac{36}{12}\) = 3. Each piece uses 3 quarter-yards, and 9 quarter-yards make 3 pieces.

Do not flip the mixed number part by part. Change it to an improper fraction first. If the divisor is a mixed number, change it too: \(3\frac{3}{4}\) ÷ \(1\frac{1}{4}\) = \(\frac{15}{4}\) ÷ \(\frac{5}{4}\) = 3.

Predicting the size of the quotient

Before you compute, predict. Dividing by a number less than 1 makes the answer bigger, because many small groups fit. Dividing by a number greater than 1 makes the answer smaller, because the amount is shared into large groups. Dividing by exactly 1 keeps the number the same.

In the image, \(\frac{4}{5}\) ÷ \(\frac{1}{2}\) counts half-cup servings, so the answer is double: \(\frac{8}{5}\) = \(1\frac{3}{5}\), which is more than \(\frac{4}{5}\). But \(\frac{4}{5}\) ÷ 2 shares \(\frac{4}{5}\) cup between two people, so each gets less: \(\frac{2}{5}\).

Use the prediction to check your work. If you divide by \(\frac{1}{3}\) and get an answer smaller than the starting number, something went wrong.

Less; \(\dfrac{3}{10}\)

  1. 3 is greater than 1, so the quotient is less than \(\frac{9}{10}\).
  2. \(\frac{9}{10}\) ÷ 3 = \(\frac{9}{10}\) × \(\frac{1}{3}\) = \(\frac{9}{30}\).

Q33

Order from least to greatest without computing first: \(\dfrac{2}{3}\) ÷ \(\dfrac{1}{2}\), \(\dfrac{2}{3}\) ÷ 2, \(\dfrac{2}{3}\) ÷ 1. Then check.

Answer

\(\dfrac{2}{3}\) ÷ 2 = \(\dfrac{1}{3}\) < \(\dfrac{2}{3}\) ÷ 1 = \(\dfrac{2}{3}\) < \(\dfrac{2}{3}\) ÷ \(\dfrac{1}{2}\) = \(1\dfrac{1}{3}\)

  1. Dividing by 2 (more than 1) makes it smaller, by 1 keeps it the same, and by \(\frac{1}{2}\) (less than 1) makes it bigger.
  2. Check: \(\frac{2}{3}\) × \(\frac{1}{2}\) = \(\frac{1}{3}\); \(\frac{2}{3}\) ÷ 1 = \(\frac{2}{3}\); \(\frac{2}{3}\) × 2 = \(\frac{4}{3}\) = \(1\frac{1}{3}\).
  3. Order: \(\frac{1}{3}\) < \(\frac{2}{3}\) < \(1\frac{1}{3}\).

Q34

Which is greater than \(\dfrac{6}{7}\): \(\dfrac{6}{7}\) ÷ 3 or \(\dfrac{6}{7}\) ÷ \(\dfrac{1}{3}\)? Find both.

Answer

\(\dfrac{6}{7}\) ÷ \(\dfrac{1}{3}\) = \(2\dfrac{4}{7}\); \(\dfrac{6}{7}\) ÷ 3 = \(\dfrac{2}{7}\)

  1. Dividing by \(\frac{1}{3}\) (less than 1) gives an answer greater than \(\frac{6}{7}\).
  2. \(\frac{6}{7}\) ÷ \(\frac{1}{3}\) = \(\frac{6}{7}\) × 3 = \(\frac{18}{7}\) = \(2\frac{4}{7}\).
  3. \(\frac{6}{7}\) ÷ 3 = \(\frac{6}{7}\) × \(\frac{1}{3}\) = \(\frac{6}{21}\) = \(\frac{2}{7}\), which is less than \(\frac{6}{7}\).

Q35

Four children share \(\dfrac{2}{3}\) pound of clay equally. Will each child get more or less than \(\dfrac{2}{3}\) pound? How much does each get?

Answer

Less; \(\dfrac{1}{6}\) pound each

  1. Sharing among 4 divides by a number greater than 1, so each gets less than \(\frac{2}{3}\) pound.
  2. \(\frac{2}{3}\) ÷ 4 = \(\frac{2}{3}\) × \(\frac{1}{4}\) = \(\frac{2}{12}\).
  3. Simplify: \(\frac{2}{12}\) = \(\frac{1}{6}\) pound each.

Spot the mistake and check by multiplying

The most common mistake is flipping the wrong fraction. In the image, the student flipped \(\frac{2}{3}\) instead of the divisor \(\frac{4}{5}\). Remember: keep the first, flip the second.

Every division can be checked with multiplication. Because 12 ÷ 3 = 4, we know 4 × 3 = 12. In the same way, if \(\frac{2}{3}\) ÷ \(\frac{4}{5}\) = \(\frac{5}{6}\), then \(\frac{5}{6}\) × \(\frac{4}{5}\) must equal \(\frac{2}{3}\). It does: \(\frac{20}{30}\) = \(\frac{2}{3}\).

Other mistakes to watch for: flipping both fractions, flipping nothing, or dividing by 2 when the problem says ÷ \(\frac{1}{2}\). Predict the size first, then check by multiplying the quotient by the divisor.

Quick questions and answers

What does dividing fractions really mean?

Dividing asks how many groups of one size fit in an amount. 3 ÷ 1/2 asks how many halves fit in 3. Each whole holds 2 halves, so the answer is 6.

What is Keep-Change-Flip?

Keep the first fraction, change division to multiplication, and flip the second fraction (the divisor). For example, 5/6 ÷ 2/3 = 5/6 × 3/2 = 15/12 = 5/4 = 1 1/4.

Why does flipping the divisor work?

Multiplying both numbers in a division by the same amount does not change the answer. Multiplying both by the reciprocal of the divisor turns the divisor into 1, and dividing by 1 changes nothing, so the answer is the first fraction times the reciprocal.

How do you divide a mixed number by a fraction?

Change the mixed number to an improper fraction first, then use Keep-Change-Flip. 2 1/4 ÷ 3/4 = 9/4 × 4/3 = 3.

Why can dividing by a fraction make a number bigger?

Dividing by a number less than 1 counts small groups, and many small groups fit, so the quotient is larger. 4/5 ÷ 1/2 = 1 3/5, but 4/5 ÷ 2 = 2/5.

How do you check a fraction division answer?

Multiply the quotient by the divisor. You should get back the starting amount. If 2/3 ÷ 4/5 = 5/6, then 5/6 × 4/5 = 2/3.
