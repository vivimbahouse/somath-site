Reviewing tens and ones

Every two-digit number is made of tens and ones. A tens rod is worth ten, and a small cube is worth one. The image shows 34 as 3 tens rods and 4 cubes, so 34 = 30 + 4.

Ten loose ones have the same value as one ten. This fact is the key to subtraction with regrouping: when we do not have enough ones, we can open a ten and turn it into ten ones without changing the value of the number.

Practice: try these five questions

Q1

A model has 5 tens rods and 7 ones cubes. What number does it show?

Answer

57

Five tens are 50. Add the 7 ones: 50 + 7 = 57.

Q2

Write 83 as tens plus ones.

Answer

83 = 80 + 3 (8 tens and 3 ones)

The 8 is in the tens place, so it is worth 80. The 3 is in the ones place, so it is worth 3. Together, 80 + 3 = 83.

Q3

How many ones are in 4 tens?

Answer

40 ones

Each ten is worth 10 ones. Four tens are 10 + 10 + 10 + 10 = 40 ones.

Q4

A model shows 6 tens rods and no loose cubes. What number is it, and why do we write a 0?

Answer

60

Six tens are 60. There are no loose ones, so we write 0 in the ones place to show that the ones place is empty.

Q5

Mateo has 2 tens rods and 15 ones cubes. He says his model shows 35. Is he right? Use 10 ones = 1 ten to explain.

Answer

Yes, it shows 35

Two tens are 20, and 20 + 15 = 35. You can also trade 10 of the 15 cubes for 1 rod. Then there are 3 tens and 5 ones, which is 35. The value did not change.

When do we regroup?

Before subtracting, look at the ones column. Compare the top ones digit with the bottom ones digit. If the top ones digit is the same or larger, subtract right away: in 46 − 23, 6 ones can take away 3 ones.

If the top ones digit is smaller, there are not enough ones. In 42 − 18, 2 ones cannot take away 8 ones, so we trade 1 ten for 10 ones first. Comparing the tens is not enough: always check the ones.

Practice: try these five questions

Q6

Look at 57 − 24. Do you need to regroup? Then solve.

Answer

No regrouping; 33

Look at the ones: 7 is not smaller than 4, so no trade is needed. Ones: 7 − 4 = 3. Tens: 5 − 2 = 3. The answer is 3 tens and 3 ones, or 33. Check: 33 + 24 = 57.

Q7

Look at 63 − 27. Do you need to regroup? Then solve.

Answer

Yes, regroup; 36

Look at the ones: 3 is less than 7, so regroup. Trade 1 ten for 10 ones: 6 tens become 5 tens, and 3 ones become 13 ones. Ones: 13 − 7 = 6. Tens: 5 − 2 = 3. The answer is 3 tens and 6 ones, or 36. Check: 36 + 27 = 63.

Q8

Which of these need regrouping: 85 − 41, 72 − 36, 94 − 58, 68 − 35?

Answer

72 − 36 and 94 − 58

Compare the ones. In 85 − 41, 5 is not less than 1, so no trade. In 72 − 36, 2 is less than 6, so regroup. In 94 − 58, 4 is less than 8, so regroup. In 68 − 35, 8 is not less than 5, so no trade. (The answers are 44, 36, 36, and 33.)

Q9

Look at 50 − 13. Do you need to regroup? Why?

Answer

Yes; 0 ones cannot take away 3 ones

The top ones digit is 0, which is smaller than 3. Trade 1 ten for 10 ones: 5 tens become 4 tens and 0 ones become 10 ones. Then 10 − 3 = 7 and 4 − 1 = 3, so 50 − 13 = 37.

Q10

Lily says 44 − 19 does not need regrouping because 4 tens is more than 1 ten. Is she right? Explain.

Answer

No; regroup because 4 ones are fewer than 9 ones

We decide by looking at the ones, not the tens. There are 4 ones, and we must take away 9 ones, so we need to regroup. Look at the ones: 4 is less than 9, so regroup. Trade 1 ten for 10 ones: 4 tens become 3 tens, and 4 ones become 14 ones. Ones: 14 − 9 = 5. Tens: 3 − 1 = 2. The answer is 2 tens and 5 ones, or 25. Check: 25 + 19 = 44.

Trading one ten for ten ones

Regrouping in subtraction is a fair trade. Take one tens rod and exchange it for ten ones cubes. The image shows 32 first as 3 tens and 2 ones, then as 2 tens and 12 ones.

After the trade there is one fewer ten and ten more ones. Add the 10 new ones to the ones already there: 10 + 2 = 12. The number is still 32; we are only showing it a new way, so there are now enough ones to subtract.

Fill in the blank: 53 = 4 tens + ___ ones.

Answer

13

53 is 5 tens and 3 ones. Trading one ten leaves 4 tens and gives 10 + 3 = 13 ones. Check: 40 + 13 = 53.

Q13

After a trade, a model shows 6 tens and 11 ones. What number is it, and what did the model look like before the trade?

Answer

71; 7 tens and 1 one

Six tens are 60, and 60 + 11 = 71. Before the trade there was one more ten and ten fewer ones: 7 tens and 1 one.

Q14

Trade one ten in 80. How many tens and ones do you have now?

Answer

7 tens and 10 ones

80 is 8 tens and 0 ones. Trading one ten leaves 7 tens and gives 0 + 10 = 10 ones. Check: 70 + 10 = 80.

Q15

Ben trades one ten in 37 and says he now has 2 tens and 7 ones. What did he forget? Show the correct model.

Answer

He forgot the 10 new ones; 2 tens and 17 ones

Ben's model, 2 tens and 7 ones, is only 27, so it lost ten. When a ten is opened, its 10 ones must be added to the 7 ones already there: 10 + 7 = 17. The correct model is 2 tens and 17 ones, which still equals 37.

Subtracting with base-ten blocks

Blocks show exactly what happens in subtraction with regrouping. For 32 − 15, build 32 with 3 rods and 2 cubes. Taking away 5 ones is impossible with only 2 cubes, so trade one rod for ten cubes: now there are 2 tens and 12 ones.

Next take away the second number: remove 1 rod and 5 cubes. Count what is left: 1 ten and 7 ones, or 17. Only the first number is built; the second number tells us how much to remove.

25

Q18

After a trade, a model of 64 shows 5 tens and 14 ones. Take away 2 tens and 8 ones. What is left, and what subtraction did you do?

Answer

36; 64 − 28 = 36

Ones: 14 − 8 = 6. Tens: 5 − 2 = 3. Three tens and 6 ones make 36. Taking away 2 tens and 8 ones means taking away 28, so 64 − 28 = 36. Check: 36 + 28 = 64.

Q19

To solve 33 − 17, a student builds 33, removes 1 rod and all 3 cubes, and says she cannot take away more. What should she do instead?

Answer

Trade a ten first; 33 − 17 = 16

She needs to remove 7 ones, but only has 3. Before removing anything, trade one rod for ten cubes: 2 tens and 13 ones. Then remove 1 rod and 7 cubes. Left: 1 rod and 6 cubes, which is 16. Check: 16 + 17 = 33.

Q20

Draw a quick sketch for 75 − 48, using a line for each ten and a dot for each one. Show the trade and the answer.

Answer

27

Draw 7 lines and 5 dots. Five dots cannot lose 8, so cross out one line and draw 10 more dots: 6 lines and 15 dots. Cross out 4 lines and 8 dots. Left: 2 lines and 7 dots, which is 27. Check: 27 + 48 = 75.

Writing regrouping clearly in columns

Written subtraction records the block trade. First line up the digits: tens under tens, ones under ones. When the top ones digit is too small, cross out the tens digit and write one less above it. Cross out the ones digit and write ten more above it.

For 42 − 18, the 4 becomes 3 and the 2 becomes 12. Then subtract the ones (12 − 8 = 4) and the tens (3 − 1 = 2). Neat, small numbers above the columns show exactly what was traded, so nothing is counted twice or forgotten.

Q22

46

Q23

In 74 − 39, what small numbers do you write above the tens and ones after trading? What is the answer?

Answer

6 above the tens, 14 above the ones; 35

Four ones cannot take away 9, so the 7 tens become 6 tens and the 4 ones become 14 ones. Ones: 14 − 9 = 5. Tens: 6 − 3 = 3. The answer is 35. Check: 35 + 39 = 74.

Q24

A student wrote 62 − 28 = 46. He found 8 − 2 = 6 in the ones column. What went wrong? Find the correct answer.

Answer

He subtracted bottom minus top; 62 − 28 = 34

In subtraction we always take the bottom number away from the top number. The top ones digit is 2, so we must regroup instead of flipping the digits. Look at the ones: 2 is less than 8, so regroup. Trade 1 ten for 10 ones: 6 tens become 5 tens, and 2 ones become 12 ones. Ones: 12 − 8 = 4. Tens: 5 − 2 = 3. The answer is 3 tens and 4 ones, or 34. Check: 34 + 28 = 62.

Q25

Solve 92 − 57 in columns, showing the trade.

Answer

35

Cross out 9 and write 8; cross out 2 and write 12. Look at the ones: 2 is less than 7, so regroup. Trade 1 ten for 10 ones: 9 tens become 8 tens, and 2 ones become 12 ones. Ones: 12 − 7 = 5. Tens: 8 − 5 = 3. The answer is 3 tens and 5 ones, or 35. Check: 35 + 57 = 92.

Subtracting across zero

A zero in the ones place means there are no loose ones. In 60 − 24, 0 ones cannot take away 4 ones, so we must regroup. Trade 1 ten: 6 tens become 5 tens, and 0 ones become 10 ones.

Now subtract: 10 − 4 = 6 ones and 5 − 2 = 3 tens, so 60 − 24 = 36. A common mistake is writing 4 in the ones place because “0 minus 4 is 4.” Zero ones cannot give away 4 ones; always trade a ten first.

Solving subtraction word problems

Read the story carefully and ask: what is the starting amount, and how much is taken away or compared? Words such as left, gives away, fewer, how many more, and how many are still often signal subtraction.

Write the subtraction with the starting amount first, then regroup and subtract in columns. Finish with a sentence that answers the question, using the right label, such as “Mia has 24 stickers left.”

Checking subtraction with addition

Subtraction and addition undo each other. To check a subtraction, add the answer to the amount taken away. If the sum is the starting number, the subtraction is correct: 42 − 18 = 24 because 24 + 18 = 42.

If the check does not give the starting number, look for a mistake, often a missed trade or a ones digit subtracted the wrong way. Fix it and check again. Remember: answer + amount taken away = starting number.

Quick questions and answers

What does regrouping mean in subtraction?

Regrouping means trading one ten for ten ones so there are enough ones to subtract. The number keeps the same value; for example, 32 is both 3 tens and 2 ones and 2 tens and 12 ones.

How do I know when to regroup?

Look at the ones column first. If the top ones digit is smaller than the bottom ones digit, trade one ten for ten ones before subtracting. If it is the same or larger, subtract without regrouping.

Is borrowing the same as regrouping?

Yes. Many families learned the word borrowing. Regrouping or trading describes the idea more accurately: nothing is borrowed and paid back; one ten is simply exchanged for ten ones.

How do you subtract from a number ending in zero?

A zero in the ones place means there are no loose ones, so trade one ten for ten ones. In 60 − 24, 6 tens become 5 tens and 0 ones become 10 ones; then 10 − 4 = 6 and 5 − 2 = 3, so the answer is 36.

What is the most common subtraction mistake?

Subtracting the smaller ones digit from the larger one no matter which is on top, such as writing 8 − 2 for 42 − 18. Always take the bottom number away from the top number, and regroup when the top ones digit is too small.

How can a child check a subtraction answer?

Add the answer to the amount taken away. If the sum equals the starting number, the subtraction is correct: 42 − 18 = 24 because 24 + 18 = 42.
