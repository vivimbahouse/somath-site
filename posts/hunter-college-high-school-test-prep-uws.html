What is the Hunter exam (and why it’s different)

Hunter College High School sits at 94 E 71st Street on the Upper East Side, but it pulls heavily from Upper West Side feeder schools. Walk into any 5th-grade classroom at PS 87, PS 199, or the Anderson School and you will find parents who are already thinking about the Hunter exam timeline. The reason is simple: Hunter is one of the only truly selective middle-to-high school pipelines in New York City that admits students at the 6th-grade level.

What makes Hunter different from every other NYC selective school track:

  • Entry point is 6th grade, not 9th grade. A student who enters Hunter in 6th grade is on a seven-year track through 12th grade. That means the admissions moment is earlier — and the stakes of that single January exam feel higher.
  • No retake. Unlike the SHSAT, which students can attempt in both 7th and 8th grade, the Hunter exam is offered once per cohort. A student who misses it cannot retry the following year.
  • Eligibility is gatekept by NYS assessments, not by the family. There is no application to sit for Hunter. The NYC DOE identifies eligible students based on their 5th-grade NYS scores. Families do not opt in — they either qualify or they do not.
  • The exam tests reasoning, not coverage. A student who has memorized the standard K–5 curriculum but has never been asked to explain their thinking or work through a novel problem will find the Hunter exam hard. That gap is exactly what prep should close.

For context: NEST+m, another school many UWS families research, selects students through a different process entirely. The Hunter exam is its own track, and Hunter prep is its own discipline.

Math content tested in 2026

The Hunter math section is not a straight-line extension of the 5th-grade classroom curriculum. It tests whether a student can apply mathematical thinking in unfamiliar contexts. The core content areas in 2026:

Content area What it looks like on the exam
Pre-algebra conceptsVariable expressions, simple equations, unknowns embedded in word problems
Proportional reasoningRatios, rates, scaling, unit conversion, percentage problems
Logic & multi-step word problemsProblems that require building a solution from scratch, often with multiple steps and no template
Number theory fundamentalsFactors, multiples, primes, divisibility, remainders
Pattern recognitionSequences, geometric patterns, functional reasoning at an introductory level
Arithmetic fluencyOperations with fractions, decimals, and mixed numbers — under time pressure

What is not tested: full Algebra I, geometry proofs, statistics, or any content from the standard 6th-grade curriculum. The exam is designed so that a well-prepared 5th grader can sit for it — but “well-prepared” means something specific. In our experience, the students who struggle are those who have fast arithmetic reflexes but have never been asked to reason through a problem they have never seen before.

The exam is timed, and the math section rewards both speed and flexibility. A prep program that only drills computation will leave a student flat-footed on the logic and word-problem sections.

Eligibility: the 90th-percentile NYC State ELA + Math threshold

Before prep strategy, one fact that many UWS families get confused about: not every 5th grader can sit for the Hunter exam. Eligibility is determined by New York State assessment scores, not by school recommendation or parental request.

The threshold: A student must score at or above the 90th percentile on the NYS ELA and Math assessments — taken in 5th grade — to receive an invitation to sit for the Hunter entrance exam. The NYC DOE sends notification to qualifying students. There is no application process.

A few important clarifications:

  • Both subjects count. The threshold is a composite of ELA and Math. A student who is a strong math performer but scores below threshold on ELA will not receive an invitation. This surprises some UWS math-focused families.
  • Do not anchor on a specific raw score. NYS assessment scoring is adjusted from year to year. The 90th-percentile standard is what matters; the raw number that corresponds to it can shift. Programs that tell families “you need a 4 plus X points” are guessing at a moving target.
  • 4th-grade is not too early to think about this. The NYS assessments are administered in 5th grade, but the habits that produce 90th-percentile scores — reading widely, explaining mathematical reasoning, working through novel problems — are built over years, not weeks.

When UWS families should start prep (and what to do in 5th grade)

The Hunter exam is administered in January of 5th grade. That means the practical prep window for a current 5th grader is September through January — roughly 16–20 weeks.

Here is an honest breakdown by starting point:

  • Starting in 4th grade (ideal). Build the enrichment foundation first: pre-algebra concepts, proportional reasoning, and problem-solving habits. A student who has worked through these ideas for a full year will use the 5th-grade prep window to sharpen and practice rather than learn from scratch. This is the timeline we recommend to UWS families who ask.
  • Starting in September of 5th grade (on-track). Sixteen to twenty weeks is enough time if the student already has a strong arithmetic foundation and is a confident reader. The prep work should focus on pre-algebra, logic problems, proportional reasoning, and timed practice under exam conditions.
  • Starting in November of 5th grade (tight). Eight to ten weeks is feasible only if the student is genuinely strong across all content areas. The risk is that you are closing content gaps and practicing strategy simultaneously — that is a lot to ask of a 10-year-old.
  • Starting in December for a January exam (too late). There is not enough time to change habits. At this point the student should take the exam for the experience, and if they do not get in, use the feedback to plan for a strong SHSAT track starting in 6th grade.

What to do in 5th grade, concretely: Two to three hours per week of focused Hunter math prep is the right dose for most 5th graders. More than that tends to produce burnout before January. The work should alternate between content-building sessions (proportional reasoning, pre-algebra intro, number theory) and timed problem-set practice. Every session should include at least one multi-step word problem the student has never seen before.

Hunter math prep vs SHSAT math prep — how they differ

This is the most common question UWS parents ask when they have a child on the Hunter track and a younger sibling on the SHSAT horizon. The honest answer: they overlap, but they are not the same preparation.

Hunter exam SHSAT
Grade of exam5th grade (January), for 6th-grade entry7th or 8th grade, for 9th-grade entry
Math contentPre-algebra, proportional reasoning, logic, number theoryFull Algebra I, data analysis, geometry concepts, multi-topic problem sets
Retake optionNo — one exam per cohortYes — students can attempt in both 7th and 8th grade
ELA componentYes — the Hunter exam includes an ELA sectionYes — the SHSAT includes a verbal section (ELA/reading)
Eligibility gate90th-percentile NYS ELA + Math composite (5th grade)No eligibility gate — any NYC 7th or 8th grader can sit
Prep start (recommended)4th grade enrichment, September of 5th grade for structured prep6th grade enrichment, 7th grade for structured SHSAT prep

The core difference in math is depth and content coverage. The SHSAT math section assumes a full Algebra I curriculum; Hunter does not. A student who preps only for Hunter and then faces the SHSAT two years later will need to fill in the Algebra I gap. A strong Hunter prep track that continues into 6th-grade enrichment bridges this naturally — which is why we structure our UWS programs with a long arc in mind, not just exam-to-exam.

The other key difference is the eligibility gate. Every NYC public-school 7th and 8th grader can sit for the SHSAT. The Hunter exam is only available to students who have already cleared the 90th-percentile NYS threshold. This means Hunter prep self-selects for students who are already academically strong — and the competition inside the exam pool is stiff.

What a 12-week prep arc looks like

A 12-week Hunter math prep arc, starting in early October for a January exam, should be structured as follows. This is the model we use in SOMATH's Hunter cohorts at 226 W 79th Street.

Weeks 1–2
Diagnostic and baseline. Written diagnostic across all Hunter math content areas. Identify where the student is fast, where they slow down, and where they make systematic errors. Build the prep plan from the diagnostic, not from a fixed syllabus.
Weeks 3–5
Content: pre-algebra and proportional reasoning. Variable expressions, unknowns in word problems, ratios, rates, scaling problems, percentage applications. Focus on building intuition, not memorizing procedures.
Weeks 6–8
Content: logic, word problems, and number theory. Multi-step word problems with no template provided. Factors, multiples, primes, remainder problems, divisibility rules. Pattern sequences and functional reasoning at the introductory level.
Weeks 9–10
Mixed practice under timed conditions. Full timed math sections from prior exams and parallel practice sets. Review every wrong answer for reasoning errors, not just computational ones.
Weeks 11–12
Exam simulation and decompression. One or two full simulated exam sessions including both math and ELA. Debrief strategy. The goal of the final week is confidence maintenance, not new content.

Two to three hours per week is the right dose for most 5th graders across this arc. We do not run longer sessions for 10-year-olds than that — the marginal return past two hours in a single sitting drops sharply, and fatigue the night before an exam matters.

Common UWS prep mistakes

UWS families are organized, motivated, and research-driven — which is mostly a strength. But it also produces a specific set of prep mistakes that come up every year.

1. Treating Hunter prep as SHSAT prep for younger kids. Hunter math is pre-algebra reasoning, not Algebra I. Starting a 5th grader on a full SHSAT prep curriculum will cover content she does not need yet and miss the logic and word-problem depth the Hunter exam actually tests. Hunter prep is its own discipline.

2. Drilling computation instead of reasoning. The Hunter math section is not a speed-arithmetic test. A student who can calculate fast but cannot build a solution to an unfamiliar word problem will struggle on the exam. The prep work should include — every week — problems the student has never seen before that require building a solution from scratch.

3. Starting too late and then over-prepping to compensate. A December start before a January exam is too late to change habits. The response many UWS parents choose — adding a second tutor, doubling session hours, working through weekends — burns out a 10-year-old without adding meaningful performance. Start earlier; do not overload in the final stretch.

4. Ignoring the ELA component. The Hunter exam tests both math and ELA. Families who focus entirely on math prep and assume their child’s ELA is “fine” are leaving points on the table. The ELA section is reading-comprehension and verbal-reasoning focused — it rewards wide reading and the ability to draw inferences from complex text, both of which take time to build.

5. Conflating the eligibility threshold with exam performance. Clearing the 90th-percentile NYS threshold is a necessary condition to sit for the exam — not a predictor of performance on it. The Hunter exam pool is composed entirely of students who cleared that threshold. The competition inside the pool is stiff, and a student who qualified easily on the NYS assessments can still underperform on the Hunter exam if they have not done the specific reasoning prep the exam requires.

6. Not getting a written diagnostic first. Every serious prep program should start with a diagnostic that tells the family where the child actually is on Hunter-relevant content areas. A sales conversation is not a diagnostic. FAQ

When should my UWS 5th grader start Hunter prep?

Most UWS families should begin structured Hunter math prep at the start of 5th grade — September of the year before the January entrance exam. That gives roughly 16–20 weeks to build the pre-algebra and problem-solving skills the exam tests, without burning out the child before test day. Families who start in 4th grade are building an enrichment foundation, not cramming, which is even better. The worst outcome is starting in December for a January exam — there is not enough time to change habits under that pressure.

What kind of math is on the Hunter exam?

The Hunter College High School entrance exam math section tests pre-algebra concepts, proportional reasoning, logic and multi-step word problems, number theory fundamentals, and pattern recognition. It does not test full Algebra I or Geometry. The emphasis is on applying mathematical reasoning under time pressure — not on procedural computation. A 5th grader who can grind through arithmetic quickly but has never been asked to explain why something works will find the Hunter math section challenging.

Is Hunter prep the same as SHSAT prep?

No. Hunter prep and SHSAT prep are meaningfully different in timing, content, and format. Hunter is a 6th-grade exam taken in January of 5th grade — it tests pre-algebra reasoning, logic, and proportional thinking. SHSAT is an 8th-grade exam (with a 7th-grade option) that tests full Algebra I, data analysis, and multi-topic problem solving at greater depth and length. Hunter prep belongs in 4th–5th grade; SHSAT prep belongs in 6th–8th grade. A strong Hunter foundation helps with SHSAT, but they are not the same preparation.

What ELA + Math percentile do you need to qualify for Hunter?

To sit for the Hunter College High School entrance exam, a student must score at or above the 90th percentile on the New York State ELA and Math assessments at the end of 5th grade. This is a composite threshold — both subjects count. New York City DOE notifies eligible students based on those scores. Families should not rely on specific raw score targets, as the NYS assessment scoring can shift from year to year; focus on what the 90th-percentile standard means in terms of demonstrated mastery, not on a particular number.
