What the ISEE is and which NYC schools require it

The ISEE (Independent School Entrance Exam) is administered by ERB (Educational Records Bureau) and is the most widely used standardized admissions test for NYC independent schools. Unlike the SSAT, which is managed by a separate organization, the ISEE is ERB’s own product, and ERB also provides many of the other assessments (ELA, math) that NYC private schools use for internal placement. That alignment matters: the ISEE is designed to measure the same skills ERB schools value year-round, not just on test day.

Three levels, three applicant pools

The ISEE comes in four levels. For NYC private school applicants, three are relevant:

  • Lower Level: Students applying for grades 5 or 6. Tests content through 4th grade (for grade-5 applicants) or 5th grade (for grade-6 applicants).
  • Middle Level: Students applying for grades 7 or 8. Tests content through 6th grade (for grade-7 applicants) or 7th grade (for grade-8 applicants).
  • Upper Level: Students applying for grades 9 through 12. Tests content through 8th grade algebra, geometry, and pre-algebra.

The Primary Level (grades 2–4) exists but is less commonly required for NYC private school admissions at the schools discussed here.

Which schools require the ISEE

All of the schools discussed in this guide require the ISEE (or SSAT — schools typically accept either). The ERB ISEE page maintains a current list of member schools. For NYC purposes, the ISEE is required by or accepted at Horace Mann, Trinity School, Collegiate School, Dalton, Brearley, Spence, Columbia Grammar & Prep, Riverdale Country School, and Ethical Culture Fieldston, among others. Each school’s specific admissions page will confirm current test requirements, which can change from year to year.

What stanine each school actually wants

Here is where parents need straight talk. None of these schools publish stanine cutoffs. Horace Mann’s admissions page does not say “you need a stanine 8 in math.” Trinity’s does not either. What the stanine data below reflects is what admissions consultants, experienced tutors, and families in the NYC private school community commonly report — based on patterns across admitted classes — not official policy. Treat these as realistic targets, not guarantees.

A stanine score of 9 puts a student in the top 4% of their norm group nationally. Stanine 8 corresponds to roughly the top 12%. Stanine 7 is the top 23%. The competitive NYC private school applicant pool is already a self-selected high-achieving group, which means the stanine distribution within actual applicants skews higher than the national norms suggest. Stanine inflation is real: a stanine 7 that would be impressive in most parts of the country is table stakes in Manhattan.

The two math sections explained: Quantitative Reasoning vs. Mathematics Achievement

The ISEE has four scored sections: Verbal Reasoning, Quantitative Reasoning, Reading Comprehension, and Mathematics Achievement. For math prep, the two sections you are working on are Quantitative Reasoning and Mathematics Achievement. They are different in important ways, and conflating them is one of the most common prep mistakes.

Quantitative Reasoning

Quantitative Reasoning (QR) is the more unusual of the two sections. It tests mathematical reasoning and problem-solving through word problems and quantitative comparisons, not computation per se. You will see questions like “Which is greater, A or B?” and multi-step word problems that require you to set up a mathematical relationship before solving it. The QR section has no straightforward arithmetic drills. It is testing whether students can think mathematically — translate a verbal problem into a mathematical structure, identify what information is relevant, and reason to a conclusion efficiently. There is no calculator, which means students need to be comfortable with mental math estimation and number sense under time pressure.

At the Upper Level, QR has 37 questions in 35 minutes. At the Middle Level, 38 questions in 35 minutes. At the Lower Level, 38 questions in 35 minutes. The timing is tight, and students who are not fast with mental arithmetic tend to run out of time before they run out of knowledge.

Mathematics Achievement

Mathematics Achievement (MA) is closer to what students think of as a traditional math test. It tests curriculum content directly: arithmetic, fractions, decimals, percentages, algebra, geometry, and data interpretation, depending on the level. The questions are more procedural than QR — you need to know the content, apply the right method, and compute correctly. Again, no calculator. Mental computation under test conditions, combined with content mastery, is what separates stanine 8–9 scorers from stanine 6–7 scorers on this section.

At the Upper Level, MA has 47 questions in 40 minutes. At the Middle Level, 47 questions in 40 minutes. At the Lower Level, 30 questions in 30 minutes.

No calculator, no partial credit, no penalty: The ISEE does not penalize wrong answers (no “guessing deduction” like the old SAT). This means students should answer every question. Time management matters more than avoiding wrong answers. The no-calculator constraint is non-negotiable — students who have been calculator-dependent in school math are at a significant disadvantage on both ISEE math sections, and addressing that dependency is one of the first things we work on in prep.

Math content by level: Lower, Middle, and Upper

One of the most important things to understand about the ISEE is that it tests below-grade content relative to where the student currently is. The exam tests what students should have already learned in prior grades, not what they are currently studying. Knowing exactly which content appears at each level — and identifying the specific gaps — is the foundation of effective ISEE math prep.

Lower Level (applying for grades 5–6)

Norm group: students currently in grades 4–5

Quantitative Reasoning topics: Number concepts and operations (place value, rounding, estimation), fractions and mixed numbers, basic ratio and proportion, simple patterns, geometric measurement (perimeter, area of rectangles), basic data interpretation (bar graphs, pictographs), and word problems drawing on all of the above. Mental math strategies for addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division of whole numbers.

Mathematics Achievement topics: Multi-digit addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division; fractions (adding, subtracting, simplifying, comparing); decimals (adding, subtracting, place value); basic percentages; order of operations (no exponents); introductory geometry (lines, angles, basic polygons, perimeter and area); simple algebraic thinking (find the missing number); units of measurement (length, weight, time, money); basic statistics (mean/average, reading tables and charts).

What Lower Level students most often struggle with: Multi-step word problems, fraction operations, and translating between visual representations and numerical ones. Students from PS 87, PS 199, PS 9, or PS 84 on the UWS who are at or above grade level in NYC public school math are well-positioned for the content — the challenge is test-taking fluency and pacing, not primarily new material. See also our 5th grade math parent guide and 6th grade math parent guide for grade-level benchmarks.

Middle Level (applying for grades 7–8)

Norm group: students currently in grades 6–7

Quantitative Reasoning topics: All Lower Level content, plus ratio and proportion (including unit rates), percent increase/decrease, introductory probability, algebraic word problems (one- and two-step equations), basic geometric reasoning (angles in parallel lines, triangle properties), coordinate plane (graphing points and lines), and quantitative comparison problems that require algebraic manipulation to solve.

Mathematics Achievement topics: All Lower Level content, plus integer operations (negative numbers), operations with fractions and mixed numbers (all four operations), ratio, proportion, and percent; introductory pre-algebra (one-variable equations and inequalities); properties of geometric figures (angles, triangles, quadrilaterals, circles — perimeter, area, circumference); the coordinate plane; scientific notation; basic statistics (mean, median, mode, range); and data interpretation in more complex forms (scatter plots, two-way tables).

What Middle Level students most often struggle with: Percent change, ratio problems with multiple steps, and pre-algebra word problems requiring two equations or variables. Students transitioning from 6th or 7th grade math — whether at West End Secondary, Booker T. Washington (MS 54), Mott Hall II, or a private middle school like Columbia Grammar & Prep — should be comfortable with pre-algebra before their test date. See our 7th grade math parent guide and 8th grade math parent guide for curriculum alignment.

Upper Level (applying for grades 9–12)

Norm group: students currently in grades 8–11

Quantitative Reasoning topics: All Middle Level content, plus Algebra I and II concepts (systems of equations, quadratic expressions, function notation), advanced ratio and proportion, complex percent and number theory problems, geometric reasoning involving parallel lines and transversals, similar triangles, and the Pythagorean theorem; word problems requiring multi-step algebraic setup; quantitative comparisons involving algebraic expressions.

Mathematics Achievement topics: Full Algebra I and pre-algebra curriculum; introductory Algebra II (factoring polynomials, quadratic equations, radical expressions); geometry (the full set: angle relationships, triangle congruence and similarity, quadrilateral properties, circles, surface area, volume of 3D figures); coordinate geometry (slope, distance formula, midpoint); introductory statistics and probability (combinations, permutations for simpler cases); and data interpretation in algebraic contexts.

What Upper Level students most often struggle with: Algebra word problems that require setting up equations (not just solving them), geometry proofs and reasoning, and questions that combine algebra and geometry in a single problem. Upper Level is the most demanding ISEE level, and for students applying to Collegiate, Brearley, or Horace Mann at the high school level, stanine 8–9 on the Upper Level is genuinely difficult. This is where prep that started in middle school pays dividends. The 5 most common ISEE math mistakes

After working with many UWS students through ISEE prep at 226 W 79th St, we see the same errors come up repeatedly. Here are the five most consequential ones, with concrete examples.

Mistake 1: Misreading the question in Quantitative Reasoning

QR word problems are written precisely, and a single misread word changes the answer. The most common version: a student reads “what is the smallest value?” and answers the largest, or reads “how many more than?” and gives a total instead of a difference. The fix is not more math — it is the habit of circling the question word before computing anything. What is the question actually asking? Students who underline the key quantity being asked for before setting up the problem make far fewer of these errors under time pressure.

Mistake 2: Calculator dependency in no-calculator conditions

Students who have used a calculator for school math since 4th or 5th grade often cannot quickly compute 6/7 × 14 mentally, or estimate whether 3.7 × 52 is closer to 150 or 200. On the ISEE, there is no calculator, and both math sections move fast. The specific skill that breaks down most often: fraction operations. Students who cannot add 2/3 + 3/4 without a calculator in under 20 seconds are at a structural disadvantage on both QR and MA. Mental arithmetic fluency — built through practice, not drill sheets — is the most high-leverage skill to develop 12 weeks before the test.

Mistake 3: Skipping unit conversion in word problems

ISEE word problems at Middle and Upper levels frequently involve unit conversions embedded in longer problems. A student computes the right formula and gets the right number — but in the wrong unit. Example: a question asks for the total distance in feet, the problem gives information in yards and inches, and the student reports the answer in yards. This costs the point even though the math reasoning was correct. The fix is disciplined labeling: write the unit next to every number you write down, and check that your final answer matches the unit asked for in the question.

Worked Example — Quantitative Reasoning (Middle Level)

A rectangular garden has a perimeter of 48 feet. Its length is twice its width. What is the area of the garden in square feet?

Common mistake: Students use perimeter = length + width (forgetting to use all four sides) and get an incorrect dimension, then compute area from wrong values.

Correct approach: Let width = w, length = 2w. Perimeter = 2(length + width) = 2(2w + w) = 2(3w) = 6w. So 6w = 48, which gives w = 8 feet. Length = 16 feet. Area = 8 × 16 = 128 square feet.

The lesson: Write the formula first (P = 2l + 2w), substitute carefully, solve for the variable, then compute the target quantity. Skipping steps under time pressure is where points are lost.

Mistake 4: Mishandling percent problems

Percent increase and percent decrease problems are among the most frequently missed question types on both QR and MA at the Middle and Upper levels. The specific error: a student computes percent change correctly but applies it to the wrong base. Example: if a price increases from $80 to $100, the percent increase is 25% (change divided by original: 20/80 = 0.25). Students who compute 20/100 = 20% are applying the change to the new value instead of the original. This also appears in reverse: if a $100 price is reduced by 20%, the new price is $80 — not $90, not $85. Percent problems punish memorized procedures applied without understanding. The structural remedy is to always write “percent of what?” before computing.

Mistake 5: Running out of time because of over-commitment to hard problems

The ISEE does not reward heroic effort on one hard problem at the expense of three easy ones you never reached. The per-problem time budget on Mathematics Achievement is roughly 50–55 seconds per question. Students who spend three minutes wrestling with one difficult algebra problem are giving up points on questions later in the section that they would have answered correctly. The tactic: mark hard questions, skip and return. Answer every question (no penalty for wrong answers). Come back to hard ones with remaining time. This is a test-taking skill, not a math skill — but it is one of the clearest stanine-separating behaviors we observe.

Worked Example — Mathematics Achievement (Upper Level)

If 3x − 7 = 2(x + 4), what is the value of x?

Correct approach: Distribute the right side: 3x − 7 = 2x + 8. Subtract 2x from both sides: x − 7 = 8. Add 7 to both sides: x = 15.

Common mistake: Distributing incorrectly — writing 2(x + 4) as 2x + 4 instead of 2x + 8 — and getting x = 11. This is a two-point error triggered by a single distribution error that takes under 10 seconds to make under pressure. The fix: slow down on the distribution step, write it out fully rather than doing it in your head.

A realistic 12-week ISEE math prep timeline

Twelve weeks is sufficient for most students to improve by 1–2 stanines if their foundational math is sound. If your child has significant gaps in prior-grade content, the 12-week plan should follow a longer enrichment phase — not replace it. The following is written for a student at or near grade level who needs to sharpen ISEE-specific skills and content.

Weeks 1–2 Diagnostic and gap identification. Take a full timed practice test under real conditions (no calculator, proper timing per section). Identify the 3–5 content areas with the most missed questions. Build a targeted content list — not “do more practice tests,” but “work on fraction operations, percent change, and multi-step QR word problems specifically.”
Weeks 3–4 Content repair for identified gaps. If Middle Level: focus on fraction/decimal/percent fluency and pre-algebra setup. If Upper Level: review Algebra I (linear equations, systems), percent change, and geometry fundamentals (angle relationships, triangle area, coordinate plane). This is curriculum work, not test practice.
Weeks 5–6 Quantitative Reasoning strategy. Work through QR-specific problem types: quantitative comparisons, multi-step word problems, translating words into mathematical expressions. Practice the habit of reading the question precisely and writing the question word before computing. Time yourself per problem (~55 seconds) to build pacing instinct.
Weeks 7–8 Mathematics Achievement by content area. Structured practice section by section: arithmetic/number theory one session, fractions/ratios/percents the next, algebra the next, geometry the next. The goal is mastery of question types, not exposure to as many problems as possible.
Weeks 9–10 Mixed practice and test strategy. Start taking timed sections (not full tests yet). Practice skip-and-return on hard problems. Refine mental arithmetic fluency: fraction operations, percent calculations, and basic geometry formulas should be automatic without paper.
Week 11 Full timed practice test under real conditions. Simulate the testing environment as closely as possible: no calculator, use only the time allotted, no interruptions. Score and review immediately — understand every wrong answer before moving on.
Week 12 Light review and confidence maintenance. No new content. Review the most common error types from weeks 1–11. Short timed practice sessions on weak areas only. Sleep, food, logistics — the practical test-day stuff matters. Students who arrive rested and having eaten outperform students who crammed the night before.

When to start: timing relative to ISEE test dates and ERB registration

ERB offers ISEE testing in three seasons each year: fall (roughly September through November), winter (December through January), and spring/summer (April through July). For NYC private school admissions with a January–February application deadline, the fall testing season is the primary window. Most families testing for fall 2027 admission will sit the ISEE in October or November 2026.

The ERB ISEE test dates page is updated annually with specific registration windows and test center availability. Test centers fill up, particularly in the NYC metro area during the fall season — register early, not the week before the application deadline.

Timing relative to prep

Working backwards from a November 2026 test date:

  • Start prep in late August or September 2026 for a focused 8–12 week sprint. This is sufficient for students with a strong underlying math foundation and no significant content gaps.
  • Start prep in June or July 2026 for a 16–20 week window that allows time to address content gaps before layering test-taking strategy on top. This is the recommended timeline for most Upper Level students applying to the more selective schools.
  • Start enrichment in fall 2025 or earlier if your child is one or more grade levels behind in foundational math. Test prep on a weak foundation does not move stanines. Enrichment that closes the foundational gap, followed by test prep, does.

Students who have been in a structured enrichment program — like SOMATH’s ongoing courses — are typically ready to transition into focused ISEE prep 12 weeks before the test because they are not spending the first half of prep fixing foundational gaps. FAQ

What stanine does Horace Mann require for ISEE math?

Horace Mann does not publish a stanine cutoff. What admissions consultants and experienced tutors commonly report, based on patterns across admitted classes, is that competitive applicants score in stanines 8–9 across all four ISEE sections. Horace Mann’s admissions selectivity is among the highest in NYC, and the applicant pool is deeply self-selected. A stanine 7 in math is not automatically disqualifying in a strong overall application, but for students with Horace Mann as a target, stanine 8 or higher in both Quantitative Reasoning and Mathematics Achievement is the realistic prep target. Visit the Horace Mann admissions page for current application requirements.

Is the ISEE harder than the SSAT?

Both tests cover similar content, but they differ in structure and scoring. The ISEE uses stanines (1–9), while the SSAT uses percentile rankings and scaled scores. The ISEE has no wrong-answer deduction; the SSAT deducts a quarter point for wrong answers on some versions. The ISEE’s Quantitative Reasoning section, with its word-problem-heavy format and no-calculator constraint, is generally considered more demanding than the SSAT’s math sections by tutors and students who have worked with both. Whether one is “harder” depends on the individual student’s strengths — students with strong logical reasoning often adapt quickly to QR; students strong in computation may find the SSAT more aligned with what they know.

Can you take the ISEE more than once in a year?

Yes. ERB allows students to take the ISEE up to three times in a 12-month period, with each test in a different testing season (fall, winter, spring/summer). You cannot take the ISEE twice in the same testing season. For NYC applicants targeting fall admission with January deadlines, the primary attempt is typically in the fall season (September–November) with an optional winter retake (December–January) if needed. Schools receive a score report showing all attempts; most focus on the highest scores per section.

Do NYC private schools use ISEE for transfer applicants?

Yes. Most NYC independent schools require the ISEE (or SSAT) for mid-year transfer applicants and grade-to-grade transfers, not just first-time applicants entering a school’s gateway grade. The level used depends on the grade the student is applying to enter. Some schools accept either the ISEE or the SSAT for transfers. Check each school’s admissions page for current policy, as requirements can vary by grade level and application year.

When should my UWS-area child start ISEE math prep?

For a fall testing window, starting prep in June or July — roughly 16–20 weeks before the test — is the recommended timeline for Upper Level students targeting highly selective schools. For Lower and Middle Level students with strong foundational math, 12 weeks is typically sufficient. If your child has significant foundational gaps, enrichment should precede test prep, not overlap with it. A diagnostic evaluation in the spring before the application year gives you enough runway to address gaps before the prep clock starts.

What’s a realistic stanine improvement from 12 weeks of prep?

A 1–2 stanine improvement is common with consistent, focused work over 12 weeks. Moving from stanine 6 to stanine 7 or 8 is realistic for a motivated student with identified gaps and a structured plan. Moving from stanine 4 to stanine 8 in 12 weeks is not realistic — that gap reflects foundational deficits that require longer-term enrichment. The students who see the largest stanine gains in 12 weeks are those who were already close to the next threshold, had specific identifiable gaps, and worked consistently on targeted content rather than doing random practice tests. Consistent work means two to three sessions per week with real feedback, not one practice test followed by a week of inactivity.
