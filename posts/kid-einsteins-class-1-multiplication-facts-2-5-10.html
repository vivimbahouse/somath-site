Understanding multiplication

Multiplication helps us find the total in equal groups. First count the groups, then count how many objects are in each group. In this lesson, we write the number of groups first: groups × objects in each group = total objects.

Repeated addition shows the same total by adding the size of each group again and again. The groups must be equal to use one group size for all of them. The numbers being multiplied are called factors, and their answer is called the product.

Reversing the factors gives the same product. For example, 7 groups of 2 and 2 groups of 7 both total 14, although the pictures show different groupings. This explains why a doubles strategy can help with groups of 2.

Q1 Practice 1 of 5

There are 7 small bowls with 2 beads in each bowl. Write a repeated-addition sentence and a multiplication sentence.

Answer

2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 14; 7 × 2 = 14.

  1. Count 7 equal groups. Each group contains 2 beads.
  2. Add seven 2s, or multiply 7 by 2.

Answer

  1. Write 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 = 20.
  2. The multiplication sentence is 4 × 5 = 20.

Q3 Practice 3 of 5

Which addition sentence matches 6 groups of 2: A: 6 + 2, or B: 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2? Find the total.

Answer

B; the total is 12.

  1. Six groups of 2 means adding 2 six times.
  2. 6 + 2 adds two numbers but does not show six groups of 2.

Q4 Practice 4 of 5

Three boxes contain 2, 2, and 5 buttons. Can 3 × 2 describe all the buttons? Explain, then find the actual total.

Answer

No. The actual total is 9 buttons.

  1. The third box does not contain 2, so the groups are unequal.
  2. Add the actual amounts: 2 + 2 + 5 = 9.

Q5 Practice 5 of 5

Answer

18 stars; 2 × 9 = 18 and 9 × 2 = 18.

  1. Add 9 + 9 to count the two rows.
  2. Reversing the factors does not change the total.

Multiplying by 2

Multiplying a whole number by 2 gives its double. You can add the number to itself, count pairs, or skip count in 2s. Choose the strategy that helps you explain the fact rather than guessing.

If you know one ×2 fact, the next fact is 2 more because it adds one more group of 2. All the products in the whole-number ×2 facts are even, including 0. An even total can be arranged in pairs without an object left over.

Multiplying by 5

Each new group adds 5 more. Keep track of how many groups you have counted: the first group gives 5, the second gives 10, and so on. The last number you say after counting all the groups is the product.

Whole-number multiples of 5 end in 0 or 5. That pattern can help you catch a mistake, but the last digit alone does not tell you the full answer. To extend a fact you know, add 5 for one extra group or subtract 5 for one fewer group.

Multiplying by 10

Each group of 10 is one ten. For example, 8 groups of 10 make 8 tens. In whole-number multiplication by 10, each digit has ten times its previous value and shifts one place to the left; the ones place is 0.

Use place value rather than only memorizing “add a zero.” For 12 × 10, twelve ones become twelve tens, which is 120. Zero groups still contain zero objects: 0 × 10 = 0.

Skip counting on a number line

Start at 0 and make equal forward jumps. The number of jumps tells how many groups there are, and the jump size tells how many are in each group. The landing point gives the product.

Do not count the starting 0 as a jump. Six jumps have seven marked positions if you include the start. If you start somewhere other than 0, the distance traveled still equals jumps × jump size, but you must add the starting value to find the final position.

Recognizing multiplication patterns

For whole-number inputs, ×2 products are even, ×5 products end in 0 or 5, and ×10 products end in 0. These statements describe patterns in the products, not rules for choosing the number of groups.

Patterns can show that an answer is impossible. An odd answer cannot be a whole-number ×2 product. However, an even answer is not automatically the correct answer to a particular ×2 question. Check the exact fact after using the pattern.

Some numbers belong to more than one pattern. Every multiple of 10 is also a multiple of 5 and of 2. A number ending in 5 is a multiple of 5 but not a multiple of 10.

Answer

  2. 6 × 5 = 30, so one more group of 5 gives 7 × 5 = 35.

Connecting ×5 and ×10 facts

Ten is twice as much as five. With the same number of groups, changing from 5 objects in each group to 10 objects doubles the total. Double an ×5 product to find the matching ×10 product.

You can work backward too: half of an ×10 product gives the matching ×5 product. Keep the number of groups the same when making this comparison. We are doubling the size of each group, not doubling both factors.

Solving multiplication word problems

Read the story and identify the equal groups. Ask: how many groups are there, and how many objects are in each group? Write a multiplication sentence and include the unit, such as buttons, pencils, or dollars, in your answer.

If a story adds extra objects or gives some away, first find the total in the equal groups, then make the additional change. Choose the operation from what happens in the story, not from a single keyword.

Quick questions and answers

How do children learn multiplication facts for 2, 5, and 10?

Start with equal groups and repeated addition. Use doubles for ×2, count in fives for ×5, and use groups of ten and place value for ×10. Then connect the strategies to word problems.

Why does multiplying by 2 give a double?

Multiplying a number by 2 gives the same total as adding that number to itself. Reversing the order of two factors preserves the product, so this also helps count groups of 2.

Why do multiples of 5 end in 0 or 5?

When counting from 0 in steps of 5, the ones digit alternates between 5 and 0. This applies to whole-number multiples and provides a useful check, but the exact product still needs to be calculated.

How are the ×5 and ×10 facts connected?

For the same number of groups, 10 items per group is twice as many as 5. Double the ×5 product to get the matching ×10 product, or halve the ×10 product to find the matching ×5 product.

Do you count zero as a jump on a number line?

No. Zero is the starting position. Count each move to a new landing point as one jump. If the start is not zero, add the distance traveled to the starting position.
