What the SHSAT actually is

The Specialized High Schools Admissions Test is the single test that decides admission to eight NYC specialized high schools, including Stuyvesant, Bronx Science, and Brooklyn Tech. It's offered to 8th graders each October. Score is the only criterion — no GPA, no essay, no interview. Just the test.

There are two sections, equally weighted:

  • English Language Arts (ELA) — revising and editing passages (grammar, syntax, paragraph structure) and reading comprehension (literary and informational passages).
  • Math — pre-algebra, basic algebra, basic geometry, ratios and percentages, probability, word problems, and some logic.

Both sections are multiple choice. The test is officially 180 minutes (with extra time for grid-in math questions), and students can move freely between sections. The math is not hard topic-wise — it's hard under time pressure, with sneaky question phrasing and grid-in numerical entry that punishes careless arithmetic.

Three reasons, in our experience.

First, geography. Upper West Side families are within a short subway ride of Stuyvesant in Tribeca, and Bronx Science is one Metro-North or 1 train transfer away. Many UWS 8th graders apply to all three flagship specialized schools and Hunter College HS (which has its own separate entrance exam) as a complete admissions slate.

Second, peer effect. UWS middle schools — Booker T. Third, the cost math. Specialized public high schools are free. Comparable private high schools on the UWS run $60,000+ a year. For UWS families who can afford either option, scoring into a specialized school is one of the highest-leverage academic decisions in their child's life.

What UWS families actually need from SHSAT prep

We've watched a lot of families spend a lot of money on the wrong things. Here is what actually moves a score:

  1. A clear diagnostic. Before any prep, a student needs to know exactly which skills are strong, which are shaky, and which haven't been built yet. Most prep programs skip this and go straight into practice tests — which is like training for a marathon without checking whether you can run a mile.
  2. Foundational math, not just test tricks. Kids who score well on the SHSAT math section are kids who actually understand fractions, ratios, and proportional reasoning at a mechanical level. "Tricks" only work when the underlying number sense is already there.
  3. Real reading practice on real passages. The ELA section is brutal for kids who read fast but shallow. UWS kids tend to be over-confident readers and under-confident annotators. Slow, deliberate annotation practice on dense passages is the single biggest ELA score driver.
  4. Timed practice — but not too early. Practice tests are diagnostic instruments, not training tools. Doing four practice tests in October without first fixing the underlying gaps just teaches a student to lock in their mistakes.
  5. A teacher who knows the test cold. The SHSAT has specific question types that come back year after year. A teacher who has taught it for five or ten years will spot a student's weakness in 30 seconds; a tutor who is "good at math" but new to the SHSAT will not.

How to choose between Manhattan SHSAT options

UWS families generally compare four kinds of providers:

  • Chain centers (Kumon, Russian School of Math, Mathnasium). Structured, predictable, worksheet-driven. Good for families who need childcare-adjacent structure. Less good for kids who already move fast and need individual attention.
  • Test-prep specialists (Kaplan, Test Innovators). Test-content depth, but typically large class sizes or fully online. Strong on test mechanics, weaker on foundational math gap-closing.
  • Independent tutors. One-on-one and personal, but quality varies enormously. The good ones charge $200+/hour and book out a year in advance. The cheap ones often don't know the SHSAT.
  • Small-group programs like SOMATH. Real teachers, small classroom, custom curriculum. More expensive than chain centers, less expensive than serious one-on-one tutoring, with cohort energy that 1:1 doesn't have.

Honest summary: there is no single right answer. The right SHSAT prep is the one matched to your child's current skill profile, your family's schedule, and what your child actually responds to. The free evaluation exists to figure that out.

Frequently asked questions from UWS parents

When is the SHSAT given?
Once per year, in late October, for current 8th graders applying to 9th grade. Registration opens through the student's middle school guidance counselor in early September.

Can my child take the SHSAT in 9th grade?
Yes — current 9th graders applying to 10th grade can also test, but the available seats are much smaller and the cutoffs are higher. The 8th-grade administration is the realistic shot.

Does extra time help on the SHSAT?
For students with documented learning differences who qualify for accommodations, yes. But the SHSAT is already structured with relatively generous timing — adding more time doesn't transform a score unless the underlying content is solid.

How do I know if my UWS 6th grader is "on track"?
The honest answer is that there is no single benchmark. What you want to see in 6th grade: comfortable command of fractions, ratios, and proportional reasoning; ability to read a dense passage and actually retain it; willingness to sit with a hard problem for 10 minutes without giving up. A free SOMATH evaluation will tell you specifically where your child stands.
