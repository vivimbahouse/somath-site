How much college costs in 2026 (and why it’s worse for NYC families)

The sticker price at selective private universities is no longer a negotiating fiction — for middle-to-upper-income families who do not qualify for need-based aid, it is close to the real number. According to NCES Digest of Education Statistics, the average total cost of attendance at 4-year private nonprofit institutions was approximately $58,600 in 2022–23 — a figure that has risen steadily and, after inflation adjustments, sits closer to $62,000–$65,000 for 2025–26 at comparable schools. The most selective universities charge significantly more: NYU and Columbia are both approaching $95,000 per year all-in when tuition, room, board, and fees are combined.

For UWS families earning $150,000–$300,000 — comfortably above need-based aid cutoffs at most schools — these numbers are largely non-negotiable. The only reliable levers are merit scholarships and tuition-free public schools. Both levers turn on academic performance. Both turn, disproportionately, on math.

The NYC factor: UWS families face a compounding cost pressure. Many are already paying $45,000–$60,000 per year for private middle school (Trinity, Collegiate, Brearley, Dalton, Spence, Riverdale Country). A student who exits 8th grade at a school like that and enters a public specialized high school — Stuyvesant, Bronx Science, Brooklyn Tech — saves that entire amount for four years before college costs even begin.

Merit aid is real — and math is the lever that moves it

There is a persistent myth that merit scholarships only exist at schools nobody wants to attend. That is false. NACAC data shows that over the past two decades, merit-based aid at private institutions has grown significantly, and a substantial majority of 4-year private colleges now offer institutional merit awards. Across all of these programs, the SAT math section is the most differentiated piece of the academic profile. On the College Board’s Digital SAT, more students cluster near the top of the Evidence-Based Reading and Writing section than the math section. A student who scores 750–800 in math is a smaller, more distinctive cohort than a student who scores 750–800 in reading. For merit scholarship committees evaluating thousands of near-identical academic profiles, a strong math score — often the result of years of enrichment rather than a single test-prep sprint — is among the clearest differentiators.

The SAT math score improvement from roughly 650 to 750 is realistic over two to three years of structured work for a motivated student who starts in middle school. That 100-point jump, depending on the school and the award level, often translates to $5,000–$25,000 per year in merit aid at the right institutional targets. The key word is “targets”: this analysis is most powerful when families identify schools with transparent merit programs — Tulane, BU, USC, Northeastern, Emory, WUSTL, Case Western, and others — rather than applying only to schools that rarely post large merit awards.

The middle-school window: why grades 6–8 are the highest-ROI years

The single most common regret we hear from UWS families at our 226 W 79th Street classroom is a version of the same sentence: “We wish we had started in middle school.” Here is why the window matters so much.

The math sequence through high school is a chain. Each link depends on the previous one. Algebra I → Geometry → Algebra II/Trigonometry → Pre-Calculus → AP Calculus AB → AP Calculus BC. A student who reaches Algebra I in 8th grade — one year ahead of the standard public-school track — can complete the entire chain and sit in AP Calculus BC by 12th grade. A student who reaches Algebra I in 9th grade — the default at many NYC public schools — often peaks at Pre-Calculus or AP Calculus AB, one or two steps back on the same chain.

That one-step difference matters for two reasons. First, AP Calculus BC on a transcript signals to selective colleges that a student navigated the most demanding high school math sequence, and the course-rigor component of college applications is real. Second, the SAT math section covers material through Algebra II and introductory statistics — exactly what a student on the accelerated track has already internalized and practiced for three years before the test.

Students at public middle schools across the UWS — West End Secondary, MS 54 (Booker T. Washington), Mott Hall II — as well as those at private middle schools like Columbia Prep, Trevor Day, Calhoun, or Ethical Culture Fieldston, can all benefit from enrichment that moves them ahead of their school’s sequence. The question is simply whether they start early enough for the chain to complete on time.

How SHSAT and Hunter add another layer of savings

New York City’s specialized high schools — Stuyvesant, Bronx Science, Brooklyn Tech, and five others — are among the most academically rigorous public high schools in the country, and they are free. Admission is determined entirely by the Specialized High School Admissions Test (SHSAT), which is approximately 57 questions in math and 57 in reading. The NYC DOE specialized high schools page has the official current-year information on the test and application process.

The financial argument for SHSAT prep is straightforward. A student who gains Stuyvesant admission is comparing a $0 tuition bill to a $50,000–$60,000/year private school bill. That is a four-year savings of $200,000–$240,000. The SHSAT math section covers arithmetic, number theory, pre-algebra, and geometry through 8th grade — precisely the curriculum SOMATH’s 6th–8th grade enrichment tracks are designed to build. Our SHSAT math 12-month roadmap covers exactly how to sequence that preparation.

Hunter College High School operates on a different track: it is a grades 7–12 school with its own admissions test taken in 6th grade, and it is also tuition-free. A Hunter admission saves six years of private-school tuition, not four. For families paying for elementary school at PS 87, PS 199, PS 452, or PS 9 who are evaluating the path through 6th grade, the Hunter option is worth planning around explicitly. For preparation details, see our guide to Hunter College High School test prep on the UWS.

It is worth saying plainly: SHSAT and Hunter prep is not the same as general math enrichment, and preparing for both simultaneously requires sequencing. The SHSAT is in October of 8th grade; the Hunter test is in January of 6th grade. Both windows require students to be working at a level one to two years ahead of their school sequence. That acceleration is exactly what middle-school enrichment is designed to produce — the test prep layer is added in the final six to twelve months before the exam.

FAQ

Does math enrichment actually improve SAT math scores enough to matter for merit aid?

In our experience, yes — but the timeline matters. Students who begin consistent enrichment in 6th or 7th grade enter high school with a stronger algebra and pre-calculus foundation, which directly underpins the SAT Math section. A student who arrives at 9th grade already comfortable with linear equations and systems of equations is practicing SAT-level material two to three years before the test. That compounding makes a real difference. A 100-point SAT math improvement — from roughly 650 to 750 — is realistic over two to three years of structured work, and at schools like BU, USC, Tulane, and Northeastern, that range corresponds to meaningful merit aid differences. For context on the College Board’s Digital SAT math section structure, see the official SAT Suite pages.

What SAT score do you need for merit scholarships at competitive private universities?

The threshold varies by school, but the most significant merit aid at selective private universities typically starts around SAT 1450–1500. Tulane’s Dean’s Honor Scholarship (full tuition) lists typical recipients with SAT 1500+ and ACT 33+. BU’s Trustee Scholarship (full tuition) winners typically have SAT scores above 1500 and top-5% class rank. USC’s Presidential and Trustee scholarships are awarded to the most academically outstanding admitted students. Less selective schools offer meaningful merit aid — $15,000–$25,000 per year — starting at SAT scores in the 1300–1400 range. The implication: a student moving from 1300 to 1500 on the SAT (primarily via math improvement) is not just crossing a psychological threshold. They are entering the merit-aid eligibility window at a significant number of schools.

Is it worth investing in math enrichment if my child might get into Stuyvesant or Bronx Science?

The financial case is especially strong here. Stuyvesant, Bronx Science, and Brooklyn Tech are tuition-free public high schools — a four-year cost of zero versus $50,000+ per year at a private school like Trinity, Collegiate, or Dalton. If your child would otherwise attend a private high school, gaining Stuyvesant admission saves ~$200,000 in high school costs alone, before any college savings are counted. The SHSAT math section covers arithmetic, algebra, and geometry through 8th grade — exactly the curriculum SOMATH’s enrichment tracks build.

How early should a UWS student start math enrichment to maximize college merit aid?

Grades 6–8 are the highest-ROI window. The math a student builds in middle school — number sense, pre-algebra, Algebra I — determines their high school course trajectory. A student who completes Algebra I by 8th grade can take Geometry in 9th, Algebra II/Trig in 10th, Pre-Calculus in 11th, and AP Calculus BC in 12th. That progression produces the transcript and SAT math score profile that unlocks top merit awards. Starting in 9th grade is not too late, but the ceiling is lower. Starting in 6th or 7th grade gives the most runway. See our guide on when to start math enrichment for NYC students for a grade-by-grade breakdown.
