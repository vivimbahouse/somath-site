SHSAT Prep Timeline: when to start if you live on the Upper West Side.

A month-by-month timeline starting in 6th grade, the mistakes I watch families make every August, and what "ready for Stuyvesant" actually looks like from the inside.

School of Math May 1, 2026 11 min read

Every August, three or four families walk into our 79th Street space with the same panic on their face. Their child is going into 8th grade. The SHSAT is in October. They've heard that Stuyvesant takes only the very top scorers. They want to know if there's still time.

The honest answer is: sometimes. Some kids walk in already strong on math and reading and just need eight weeks of test familiarization. Most don't. For most, eight weeks is not enough to move the needle the way it needs to move — and the family ends up with a kid who is exhausted, anxious, and still ten points short of the school they wanted.

The kids who get into specialized high schools comfortably are almost always the kids who started thinking about the SHSAT in 6th or early 7th grade. Not grinding away on practice tests for two years. Just building the right foundation slowly, so that when prep starts in earnest, there's something to sharpen rather than something to construct from scratch.

Here's the timeline I give Upper West Side families when they ask. It's not the only way, but it's the one that works.

What the SHSAT actually tests

Quick refresher, because a lot of parents arrive with a vague idea. The SHSAT has two sections — English Language Arts and Math — both untimed within an overall time limit, both multiple choice. Math covers Pre-Algebra, Algebra I, basic Geometry, ratios, percentages, probability, word problems, and some logic.

Two things make it hard. First, the math content runs through early Algebra I, which most NYC public school 7th graders have not finished yet by the time they take the test. Second, the ELA section is dense and the time pressure across both sections is real. A child who is "good at math at school" but has never been timed, never seen Algebra I word problems, and reads a chapter book a month is not yet competitive — and that's not their fault.

The realistic score-to-school landscape

I'll be careful here, because the cutoffs shift every year based on test difficulty and applicant pool. Don't take any number you read online — including this one — as gospel.

That said, historically: Stuyvesant has typically required something around the 560 range out of 800, with Bronx Science and Brooklyn Tech a notch below, and schools like Brooklyn Latin, HSMSE (High School for Math, Science and Engineering at City College), and HSAS (High School of American Studies at Lehman) somewhere in between. The exact numbers shift; the rough hierarchy doesn't. What matters for your child's preparation is that the top three schools require comfort with the hardest 20% of the test, which is mostly Algebra I word problems and dense reading passages under time pressure.

Most of the kids I've coached into Stuyvesant didn't have natural genius. They had eighteen months of consistent work, finished Algebra I before the test, and learned how to manage time. That's it.

The timeline

Here's the month-by-month I recommend, working backward from a late-October 8th grade test date.

6th grade — Spring (March–June)

Don't start "SHSAT prep." Do start strengthening fundamentals: fraction fluency, decimals, ratios, negatives, basic equation-solving. Read every night — fiction, longer chapter books, biographies. Anything dense. The goal isn't test prep yet. It's foundation.

Summer before 7th grade

Begin Pre-Algebra in earnest if school hasn't already. The single biggest predictor of an SHSAT score is whether the child has finished Algebra I before the test. Summer is when this gets done.

7th grade — Fall

Continue Pre-Algebra and start Algebra I. Take one diagnostic SHSAT — not for prep, just to see the format and where the gaps are. No score pressure. Just calibration.

7th grade — Spring

Begin formal SHSAT prep, two sessions a week. Math is content-driven (finish Algebra I, sharpen geometry, lock in ratio and percent reasoning). ELA is reading-volume-driven plus passage technique. One full-length practice test every 4–6 weeks, with structured review.

Summer before 8th grade

The most important ten weeks. Three to four hours of work per day, mixing content review, timed sections, and full-length practice tests. Score should be climbing steadily. Reading volume goes up, not down.

8th grade — September

Sharpen, don't cram. Two to three full-length practice tests under real conditions. Targeted review of error patterns. Sleep. Build a test-day routine. By now most of the heavy lifting is done.

Test day — late October

You've already done the work. Don't re-prep the night before. Walk in confident, manage time, and trust the foundation.

The August panic mistake

The single most common failure mode I see is the family that starts prep in late July or August before 8th grade. It's not that 10 weeks is "too short." It's that 10 weeks isn't long enough to finish Algebra I, and most of the math difficulty on the SHSAT lives in Algebra I.

What ends up happening: the student spends those 10 weeks half-learning Algebra and half-doing practice tests, never really mastering either. Their score on diagnostic tests plateaus around the level they walked in at. Test day arrives. They miss the Stuyvesant cutoff by 30 points, hit Brooklyn Tech, and the family is disappointed.

You can't cram Algebra I in eight weeks. You can sharpen it in eight weeks if it's already there.

The fix is to start the math content piece in 6th grade spring or 7th grade fall — quietly, not as "test prep," just as math — and only call it SHSAT prep once the foundation is finished.

Reading is the hidden lever

Half the SHSAT is ELA, and the kids who fall short on specialized high school cutoffs almost always fall short on ELA, not math. Why? Because ELA is the section you can't cram. The students who score well are students who have been reading actual books for years.

If your 6th grader reads 30 minutes a night, in three years they will have read roughly 250 to 400 hours of text. That's the lever. Not the practice tests. The reading.

One last thing — the specialized high school is not the only finish line

I want to say this clearly because the Upper West Side rumor mill can make it feel like Stuyvesant or bust. It isn't. Some of my favorite former students went to LaGuardia for the arts, to Beacon, to Bard High School Early College, to excellent private schools, to their zoned high school with a strong honors track. They went to MIT, Stanford, Harvard, Princeton from all of those places.

The SHSAT is one door. Prepare for it well if your child is interested. But the goal isn't a school. The goal is a kid who actually understands math and reads carefully. That kid does well wherever they go.
