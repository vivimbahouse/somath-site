Theory refresher · What R&W Module 1 tests

The Digital SAT Reading & Writing section has two 32-minute modules, each with 27 scored questions plus embedded pretest questions (33 total questions appear in this module). Module 1 is not adaptive — every student sees the same items. Every question is a short, self-contained passage (roughly 25–150 words) followed by one question and four answer choices — there are no multi-question passage sets. The four content domains are:

1 · Craft & Structure (roughly 13–15% of the section)

Words in Context. Pick the word or phrase that most precisely fits the logic of the sentence. Watch for signal words like “although,” “however,” and “therefore” that tell you whether the blank should agree with or contrast the surrounding idea.

Text Structure & Purpose. Identify how a text (or a single sentence within it) is organized — e.g., does it move from description to reflection, from criticism to ambition, from a claim to a supporting example?

Cross-Text Connections. Two short texts are given on the same topic. You must determine how the author/idea of Text 2 would most likely respond to, or relate to, a claim made in Text 1.

2 · Information & Ideas (roughly 26–28% of the section)

Central Ideas & Details. Identify the main point of a text as a whole — not a detail, not a partial truth, but the idea every sentence in the passage supports.

Command of Evidence (Textual). Choose the quotation (or piece of the text) that best supports, illustrates, or exemplifies a given claim.

Command of Evidence (Quantitative). Use a table or graph to complete a statement logically — the correct choice must be numerically accurate and serve the logical role the sentence needs (e.g., completing a pattern or contrast).

Inferences. Draw the most logically supported conclusion from the given information — usually phrased as which finding would most support a hypothesis, or what a passage most strongly suggests.

3 · Standard English Conventions (roughly 11–15% of the section)

Boundaries. Punctuation between and within sentences — periods, semicolons, commas, colons, and dashes. A period or semicolon joins two independent clauses; a comma alone between two independent clauses is a comma splice (incorrect); no punctuation at all between two independent clauses is a run-on (incorrect).

Form, Structure & Sense. Grammar and usage — subject-verb agreement, verb tense consistency, pronoun-antecedent agreement, plural vs. possessive nouns, and modifier placement (a modifying phrase must sit next to the noun it logically describes, or you get a dangling modifier).

4 · Expression of Ideas (roughly 12–19% of the section)

Transitions. Choose the logical connector (however, therefore, in addition, finally, etc.) that correctly signals the relationship between two ideas — contrast, cause-and-effect, addition, or sequence/conclusion.

Rhetorical Synthesis. You are given bulleted notes and a specific rhetorical goal (e.g., “describe X to an audience unfamiliar with Y” or “emphasize a difference between X and Y”). Pick the single sentence that best accomplishes that exact goal using only the given notes — accuracy alone isn’t enough; the sentence must match the stated purpose.

Question 1 · Words in Context

The following text is from the 1913 story “The King’s Coin” by Emily Pauline Johnson, a Kanienkehá:ka (Mohawk) writer also known as Tekahionwake. Fox-Foot, a young Ojibwe man, is guiding a group of fur traders who are traveling by canoe and suspects that they are being followed.

At supper time, Fox-Foot would allow no fire to be built, no landing to be made, no trace of their passing to be left. They ate canned meat and marmalade, drank again of the stream and pushed on, until just at dusk they reached the edge of a long, still lake, with shores of granite and dense fir forest.

As used in the text, what does the word “trace” most nearly mean?

  • A) Evidence
  • B) Blemish
  • C) Amount
  • D) Sketch

Correct answer: A) Evidence

✓ A) Evidence
“Trace” here means evidence or sign. Fox-Foot refuses to build a fire or make a landing precisely because he doesn’t want to leave behind any evidence of the group’s movements — he’s trying to avoid detection by whoever might be following them.

✗ B) Blemish
“Blemish” means a flaw or mark that spoils something’s appearance. Fox-Foot isn’t worried about disfiguring the landscape; he’s worried about being tracked, which is about leaving evidence, not causing damage.

✗ C) Amount
“Amount” doesn’t fit — it’s unclear what an “amount” of the group’s passing would even mean. The passage is about avoiding any sign of the group at all, not limiting a quantity of something left behind.

✗ D) Sketch
“Sketch” means a rough drawing. Nothing in the text suggests the travelers are leaving behind actual drawings; the concern is about physical evidence of their route, like a fire or a canoe landing.

Question 2 · Words in Context

Many ancient sculptures of people’s heads are missing their noses. This is because the nose is the most _______ part of a sculpture of a person’s head. It is delicate and sticks out from the rest of the sculpture, making it especially easy to break.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

  • A) recognizable
  • B) fragile
  • C) common
  • D) sophisticated

Correct answer: B) fragile

✗ A) recognizable
“Recognizable” means identifiable. If noses keep breaking off, they can’t be the most identifiable part of a sculpture’s head — this contradicts the point of the sentence.

✓ B) fragile
“Fragile” means delicate or easily broken. The text says the nose is “delicate” and “especially easy to break” because it sticks out — that's a direct restatement of fragility, which explains why noses are so often missing from ancient sculptures.

✗ C) common
“Common” means frequent or widespread. Since noses are so often missing, they are actually the least common surviving feature, not the most common one.

✗ D) sophisticated
“Sophisticated” means refined or complex. Nothing about sticking out and breaking easily relates to refinement or complexity.

Question 3 · Words in Context

K.D. Leka and colleagues found that the Sun’s corona provides an advance indication of solar flares—intense eruptions of electromagnetic radiation that emanate from active regions in the Sun’s photosphere and can interfere with telecommunications on Earth. Preceding a flare, the corona temporarily exhibits increased brightness above the region where the flare is _______.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

  • A) antecedent
  • B) impending
  • C) innocuous
  • D) perpetual

Correct answer: B) impending

✗ A) antecedent
“Antecedent” means coming before. That would flip the logic: the flare would have to precede the brightness, but the text says the brightness is the advance signal that comes before the flare.

✓ B) impending
“Impending” means approaching or about to happen. The corona’s increased brightness is described as an “advance indication” that comes before the flare — so the flare itself is impending, or on its way, when the brightness appears.

✗ C) innocuous
“Innocuous” means harmless. Solar flares are described as interfering with telecommunications, so calling them harmless contradicts the passage.

✗ D) perpetual
“Perpetual” means never-ending or constantly present. If the corona gives advance warning of a flare, the flare must not yet exist at that moment — so it can’t be described as perpetual, or always present.

Question 4 · Words in Context

To demonstrate that the integrity of underground metal pipes can be assessed without unearthing the pipes, engineer Aroba Saleem and colleagues _______ the tendency of some metals’ internal magnetic fields to alter under stress: the team showed that such alterations can be measured from a distance and can reveal concentrations of stress in the pipes.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

  • A) hypothesized
  • B) discounted
  • C) redefined
  • D) exploited

Correct answer: D) exploited

✗ A) hypothesized
“Hypothesized” would mean they merely proposed the tendency as an untested idea. But the text treats the magnetic-field behavior as a known fact the team used as a tool, not a guess they were testing.

✗ B) discounted
“Discounted” means downplayed or dismissed. The team didn’t ignore this tendency — they built their entire measurement method around it.

✗ C) redefined
“Redefined” means reformulated or given new meaning. Nothing in the text suggests the team changed the definition of how metals behave under stress; they simply used that known behavior.

✓ D) exploited
“Exploited” means made productive use of. Saleem’s team took the known tendency of certain metals’ magnetic fields to change under stress and put it to work, using it to measure pipe stress from a distance without digging up the pipe.

Question 5 · Words in Context

Despite the generalizations about human behavior they have produced, many studies of behavioral psychology have used highly unrepresentative subject pools: students at the colleges and universities where the researchers are employed. To _______ this situation, it is necessary to actively recruit subjects from diverse backgrounds and locations.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

  • A) sanction
  • B) ameliorate
  • C) rationalize
  • D) postulate

Correct answer: B) ameliorate

✗ A) sanction
“Sanction” means to officially approve or to penalize. Neither meaning fits: the text isn’t about giving approval to the problem or punishing it, but about fixing it.

✓ B) ameliorate
“Ameliorate” means to improve or remedy. The text identifies a problem — unrepresentative subject pools — and then proposes a fix (recruiting diverse subjects), which is exactly what it means to ameliorate a bad situation.

✗ C) rationalize
“Rationalize” means to explain away or justify. The text isn’t trying to justify unrepresentative subject pools; it’s proposing a solution to the problem.

✗ D) postulate
“Postulate” means to put forward a claim or theory. The sentence is about remedying a known issue, not proposing a new theoretical claim.

Question 6 · Text Structure & Purpose

The following text is adapted from Jean Webster’s 1912 novel Daddy-Long-Legs. The narrator is a young college student writing letters detailing her weekly experiences.

[The college is] organizing the Freshman basket-ball team and there’s just a chance that I shall make it. I’m little of course, but terribly quick and wiry and tough. While the others are hopping about in the air, I can dodge under their feet and grab the ball.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

  • A) To compare basketball with other sports
  • B) To provide details of how to play basketball
  • C) To state how players will be chosen for the basketball team
  • D) To explain why the narrator thinks she might make the basketball team

Correct answer: D) To explain why the narrator thinks she might make the basketball team

✗ A) To compare basketball with other sports
The text never mentions any sport besides basketball, so there’s no comparison being made.

✗ B) To provide details of how to play basketball
The text isn’t a how-to guide; it’s the narrator’s personal reasoning about her own chances, not general basketball instruction.

✗ C) To state how players will be chosen for the basketball team
The passage doesn’t describe the tryout process or selection criteria in general — it’s specifically about the narrator’s own qualities and her personal odds.

✓ D) To explain why the narrator thinks she might make the basketball team
The narrator opens by saying there’s “just a chance” she’ll make the team, then backs that up with reasons: she’s “quick and wiry and tough” and can “dodge under” other players. The whole passage is her case for why she has a shot at the team.

Question 7 · Text Structure & Purpose

In the late 1800s, Spanish-language newspapers flourished in cities across Texas. San Antonio alone produced eleven newspapers in Spanish between 1890 and 1900. But El Paso surpassed all other cities in the state. This city produced twenty-two newspapers in Spanish during that period. El Paso is located on the border with Mexico and has always had a large population of Spanish speakers. Thus, it is unsurprising that this city became such a rich site for Spanish-language journalism.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

  • A) To compare Spanish-language newspapers published in Texas today with ones published there during the late 1800s
  • B) To explain that Spanish-language newspapers thrived in Texas and especially in El Paso during the late 1800s
  • C) To argue that Spanish-language newspapers published in El Paso influenced the ones published in San Antonio during the late 1800s
  • D) To explain why Spanish-language newspapers published in Texas were so popular in Mexico during the late 1800s

Correct answer: B) To explain that Spanish-language newspapers thrived in Texas and especially in El Paso during the late 1800s

✗ A) To compare Spanish-language newspapers published in Texas today with ones published there during the late 1800s
The text is entirely about the late 1800s; it makes no comparison to newspapers published today.

✓ B) To explain that Spanish-language newspapers thrived in Texas and especially in El Paso during the late 1800s
The text opens with the general claim that Spanish-language newspapers flourished across Texas, gives San Antonio’s count as one example, then shows El Paso surpassed everyone else, and closes by explaining why El Paso in particular was such fertile ground. The text discusses popularity within Texas, not whether these newspapers were read or popular in Mexico.

Question 8 · Text Structure & Purpose

Chile’s Atacama Desert is one of the driest places on Earth. Mary Beth Wilhelm and other astrobiologists search for life, or its remains, in this harsh place because the desert closely mirrors the extreme environment on Mars. The algae and bacteria found in Atacama’s driest regions may offer clues about Martian life. By studying how these and other microorganisms survive such extreme conditions on Earth, Wilhelm’s team hopes to determine whether similar life might have existed on Mars and to develop the best tools to look for evidence of it.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?

  • A) To contrast the conditions in the Atacama Desert with those on Mars
  • B) To explain why many life-forms cannot survive in the Atacama Desert
  • C) To indicate why astrobiologists choose to conduct research in the Atacama Desert
  • D) To describe certain limitations to conducting scientific study in the Atacama Desert

Correct answer: C) To indicate why astrobiologists choose to conduct research in the Atacama Desert

✗ A) To contrast the conditions in the Atacama Desert with those on Mars
“Mirrors” signals similarity, not contrast — the sentence emphasizes how alike Atacama and Mars are, which is exactly why the desert is useful for this research.

✗ B) To explain why many life-forms cannot survive in the Atacama Desert
The sentence isn’t about organisms failing to survive; algae and bacteria are said to survive Atacama’s extreme conditions, which is the whole point of studying them.

✓ C) To indicate why astrobiologists choose to conduct research in the Atacama Desert
The underlined sentence gives the reason astrobiologists study Atacama at all: it “closely mirrors” the harsh Martian environment. The rest of the passage builds on this by explaining what they hope to learn from that similarity, confirming that the sentence justifies the choice of research site.

✗ D) To describe certain limitations to conducting scientific study in the Atacama Desert
Nothing in the sentence suggests any obstacle or limitation to research; it explains a reason researchers are drawn to the location, not a constraint on their work there.

✗ A) It presents an objection to the argument of Guerra et al. about transportation mode choice in urban areas of Mexico.
The underlined portion agrees with and restates Guerra et al.’s finding rather than objecting to it — it reinforces that context matters, echoing their research.

✓ B) It explains why it is challenging to influence transit ridership solely by altering characteristics of a transit system.

Question 10 · Text Structure & Purpose

Changes to vegetation cover and other human activities influence carbon and nitrogen levels in soil, though how deep these effects extend is unclear. Hypothesizing that differences in land use lead to differences in carbon and nitrogen levels that are not restricted to the topsoil layer (0–30 cm deep), Chukwuebuka Okolo and colleagues sampled soils across multiple land-use types (e.g., grazing land, cropland, forest) within each of several Ethiopian locations. They found, though, that across land-use types, carbon and nitrogen decreased to comparably low levels beyond depths of 30 cm.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

  • A) It describes a phenomenon that scientists do not fully understand, explains a research team’s hypothesis about that phenomenon, and then describes a finding that led the team to refine the hypothesis.
  • B) It introduces an unresolved scientific question, presents a research team’s hypothesis pertaining to that question, and then describes an observation made by the team that conflicts with that hypothesis.
  • C) It discusses a process that scientists are somewhat unclear about, introduces competing hypotheses about that process, and then explains how a research team concluded that one of those hypotheses is likely correct.
  • D) It explains a hypothesis that has been the subject of scientific debate, discusses how a research team tested that hypothesis, and then presents data the team collected that validate the hypothesis.

Correct answer: B) It introduces an unresolved scientific question, presents a research team’s hypothesis pertaining to that question, and then describes an observation made by the team that conflicts with that hypothesis.

✗ A) It describes a phenomenon that scientists do not fully understand, explains a research team’s hypothesis about that phenomenon, and then describes a finding that led the team to refine the hypothesis.
The text never describes the team refining or revising their hypothesis; it simply reports a finding that contradicts it and stops there.

✓ B) It introduces an unresolved scientific question, presents a research team’s hypothesis pertaining to that question, and then describes an observation made by the team that conflicts with that hypothesis.
The text opens with an unresolved question (how deep do land-use effects on soil go), states Okolo’s hypothesis (differences should extend below the topsoil), and then reports a finding — comparably low carbon and nitrogen at all depths below 30 cm across land uses — that conflicts with that hypothesis rather than confirming it.

✗ D) It explains a hypothesis that has been the subject of scientific debate, discusses how a research team tested that hypothesis, and then presents data the team collected that validate the hypothesis.

Question 11 · Central Ideas & Details

The data show that the marsquakes all started from the same location on the planet. This discovery was surprising to scientists, as they expected that the marsquakes would originate from all over the planet because of the cooling of the planet’s surface. ✗ A) The surface temperature of Mars has been rising.
The text mentions cooling of the surface (implying falling, not rising, temperature) only as a reason scientists expected widespread quake origins — it says nothing about surprising temperature data.

✗ B) There were different types of seismic waves causing marsquakes.
The passage never discusses different types of seismic waves; it discusses where the marsquakes originated.

✗ C) NASA’s InSight lander collected less data than scientists had expected.
There is no mention of the amount of data collected being more or less than expected; the surprise was about the quakes’ shared location, not data volume.

✓ D) All the marsquakes started from the same location on the planet.
The text says directly that the marsquakes “all started from the same location,” and that “this discovery was surprising to scientists” because they had expected quakes scattered across the planet due to surface cooling.

Question 13 · Command of Evidence (Textual)

“The Bet” is an 1889 short story by Anton Chekhov. In the story, a banker is described as being very upset about something: _______

Which quotation from “The Bet” most effectively illustrates the claim?

  • A) “Then the banker cautiously broke the seals off the door and put the key in the keyhole.”
  • B) “It struck three o’clock, the banker listened; everyone was asleep in the house and nothing could be heard outside but the rustling of the chilled trees.”
  • C) “The banker, spoilt and frivolous, with millions beyond his reckoning, was delighted at the bet.”
  • D) “When [the banker] got home he lay on his bed, but his tears and emotion kept him for hours from sleeping.”

Correct answer: D) “When [the banker] got home he lay on his bed, but his tears and emotion kept him for hours from sleeping.”

✗ A) “Then the banker cautiously broke the seals off the door and put the key in the keyhole.”
This line just describes the physical action of unlocking a door; it gives no indication of the banker’s emotional state.

✗ B) “It struck three o’clock, the banker listened; everyone was asleep in the house and nothing could be heard outside but the rustling of the chilled trees.”
This describes a quiet, still setting at three o’clock, not any feeling the banker has — it’s about atmosphere, not emotion.

✗ C) “The banker, spoilt and frivolous, with millions beyond his reckoning, was delighted at the bet.”
This says the banker was “delighted,” which is the opposite of upset, so it can’t illustrate the claim.

✓ D) “When [the banker] got home he lay on his bed, but his tears and emotion kept him for hours from sleeping.”
This quotation shows the banker in tears and too emotionally overwhelmed to sleep for hours — direct, unmistakable evidence that he was very upset about something.

Question 33 · Rhetorical Synthesis

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • The US Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) keeps a list of all at-risk species.
  • Species on the list are classified as either endangered or threatened.
  • A species that is in danger of extinction throughout most or all of its range is classified as endangered.
  • A species that is likely to soon become endangered is classified as threatened.
  • The California red-legged frog (Rana draytonii) is likely to soon become endangered, according to the FWS.

The student wants to indicate the California red-legged frog’s FWS classification category. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

  • A) Species on the FWS list, which includes the California red-legged frog (Rana draytonii), are classified as either endangered or threatened.
  • B) The California red-legged frog (Rana draytonii) appears on the FWS list of at-risk species.
  • C) According to the FWS, the California red-legged frog is in the endangered category, in danger of extinction throughout most or all of its range.
  • D) Likely to soon become endangered, the California red-legged frog is classified as threatened by the FWS.

Correct answer: D) Likely to soon become endangered, the California red-legged frog is classified as threatened by the FWS.

✗ A) Species on the FWS list, which includes the California red-legged frog (Rana draytonii), are classified as either endangered or threatened.
This states the two possible categories on the FWS list but never says which one applies to the California red-legged frog specifically.

✗ B) The California red-legged frog (Rana draytonii) appears on the FWS list of at-risk species.
This only says the frog is on the at-risk list; it doesn’t specify which of the two categories (endangered or threatened) it falls into.

✗ C) According to the FWS, the California red-legged frog is in the endangered category, in danger of extinction throughout most or all of its range.
This is factually wrong given the notes: a species “likely to soon become endangered” is classified as threatened, not endangered, so this choice misapplies the definitions.

✓ D) Likely to soon become endangered, the California red-legged frog is classified as threatened by the FWS.
This correctly applies the notes’ definition — a species “likely to soon become endangered” is classified as “threatened” — directly to the California red-legged frog, giving its specific FWS classification.

Digital SAT FAQ

What is on Module 1 of the Digital SAT Reading & Writing section?

Module 1 is a 32-minute, 27 scored + embedded pretest question module (33 total questions) that every student sees in the same fixed form. It draws from four content domains: Craft & Structure, Information & Ideas, Standard English Conventions, and Expression of Ideas. Each question is a short, self-contained passage with one question and four answer choices.

How is the Digital SAT Reading & Writing section scored?

The Reading & Writing section is scored from 200 to 800. Module 1 is the same for every student; Module 2 is stage-adaptive — students who perform well on Module 1 are routed to a harder Module 2 with access to the full 800, and students who did not are routed to an easier Module 2 that typically caps in the mid-600s.

How long is the Digital SAT Reading & Writing section?

64 minutes total, split into two 32-minute modules. Together with the Math section (70 minutes), the full Digital SAT is 2 hours 14 minutes.

What skills are tested on Digital SAT Reading & Writing Module 1?

Craft & Structure (Words in Context, Text Structure & Purpose, Cross-Text Connections), Information & Ideas (Central Ideas & Details, Command of Evidence, Inferences), Standard English Conventions (Boundaries, Form/Structure/Sense), and Expression of Ideas (Transitions, Rhetorical Synthesis).

What is a good Digital SAT Reading & Writing score?

As a general reference: 600 ≈ 70th percentile, 700 ≈ 90th percentile, 750+ ≈ top 5%. For MIT, Stanford, Harvard, and the Ivy League, competitive applicants score 750+ on Reading & Writing. For SUNY Binghamton or UNC, 700+. For most CUNY campuses, 580–660.
