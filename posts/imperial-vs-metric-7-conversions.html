Imperial System vs Metric System: 7 Conversions Every Student Should Know

Why this chart matters

The United States is one of only three countries in the world that hasn’t officially adopted the metric system for everyday life. The other two are Liberia and Myanmar. Everywhere else — and inside every science classroom, every SAT and ACT math section, every doctor’s office lab report — you’ll see meters, liters, grams, and Celsius.

Students who are fluent in both systems can read a road sign in Europe, a nutrition label at home, a chemistry problem on a test, and a physics lab from anywhere in the world without getting stuck on the units. That fluency starts with the seven conversions on this chart.

1 inch = 2.54 cm · Length

An inch is the smallest length on the poster. It’s roughly the width of an adult’s thumb. In the U.S., inches show up on rulers, TV screens (a “55-inch TV”), and clothing sizes.

The exact conversion is 1 inch = 2.54 cm — that number is not a rounding, it’s a definition set by international agreement in 1959.

  • A 12-inch ruler = 12 × 2.54 = 30.48 cm. (That’s also 1 foot — see the next row.)
  • A 6-inch pencil ≈ 15.24 cm.
  • A 55-inch TV ≈ 140 cm along the diagonal.

1 foot = 30.48 cm · Length

A foot is 12 inches. It’s the everyday U.S. unit for a person’s height (“I’m 5 feet 8″”), room dimensions, and ceiling heights.

1 foot = 30.48 cm = 0.3048 m exactly. A quick classroom estimate: 1 foot ≈ 30 cm.

  • A 5'8″ person ≈ 173 cm.
  • An 8-foot ceiling ≈ 2.44 m.
  • A 100-foot football play ≈ 30.5 m.

1 yard = 0.914 m · Length

A yard is 3 feet, roughly the distance from one hand to the other with arms stretched out (which is why the poster shows a person with arms wide). Yards are the standard for American football fields and for fabric at the store.

1 yard = 0.9144 m ≈ 91.4 cm. A meter is slightly longer than a yard — useful when you’re converting: a metric 100 m race is a few steps longer than the American 100-yard dash.

  • A 100-yard football field ≈ 91.4 m.
  • 3 yards of fabric ≈ 2.74 m.
  • A 10-yard first down ≈ 9.14 m.

1 mile = 1.609 km · Length

A mile is 5,280 feet — the U.S. unit for road distance and speed (“miles per hour”). The rest of the world drives in kilometers, so this is one of the most useful conversions for anyone traveling or reading the news.

1 mile = 1.609 km. The reverse is just as useful: 1 km ≈ 0.621 miles.

  • 60 mph ≈ 97 km/h.
  • A 5K race = 5 km ≈ 3.1 miles.
  • A 26.2-mile marathon ≈ 42.2 km.

Quick mental math: multiply miles by 1.6 to get kilometers. Divide kilometers by 1.6 to get miles.

1 gallon = 3.7851 L · Volume

A U.S. gallon is the standard container for milk, gasoline, and paint. It splits into 4 quarts, 8 pints, or 128 fluid ounces.

1 U.S. gallon = 3.7854 L (the poster rounds to 3.7851 L). Watch out: the UK Imperial gallon is different — about 4.546 L — so when you see “gallon” on a British recipe or fuel-economy chart, it’s not the same unit.

  • A 1-gallon milk jug ≈ 3.79 L.
  • A 12-gallon gas tank ≈ 45.4 L.
  • “30 mpg” is roughly 7.8 L per 100 km.

1 ounce = 28.35 g · Weight

An ounce (oz) is the small U.S. weight unit — used for food portions (“a 4-oz burger”), letters, and cosmetics. There are 16 ounces in a pound.

1 oz = 28.3495 g ≈ 28.35 g. A quick classroom shortcut: 1 oz ≈ 28 g.

  • A 1-oz slice of cheese ≈ 28 g.
  • A 4-oz burger patty ≈ 113 g.
  • An 8-oz cup of coffee ≈ 227 g (by weight).
Watch out: in cooking you’ll also see fluid ounces (fl oz), which measure volume, not weight. 1 fl oz ≈ 29.57 mL. This poster shows the weight ounce.

1 pound = 453.6 g · Weight

The pound (lb) is the everyday U.S. unit for body weight, grocery items, and shipping. 1 pound = 16 ounces.

1 lb = 453.592 g ≈ 0.454 kg. Going the other way: 1 kg ≈ 2.205 lb.

  • A 150-lb person ≈ 68 kg.
  • A 5-lb bag of flour ≈ 2.27 kg.
  • A 100-lb dog ≈ 45.4 kg.

Quick mental math: to go from pounds to kilograms, divide by 2.2. From kilograms to pounds, multiply by 2.2.

Mental-math shortcuts worth memorizing

  • Miles → km: multiply by 1.6. km → miles: divide by 1.6 (or multiply by 0.6).
  • Feet → meters: multiply by 0.3. Meters → feet: multiply by 3.3.
  • Pounds → kilograms: divide by 2.2. kg → lb: multiply by 2.2.
  • Gallons → liters: multiply by 3.8. Liters → gallons: divide by 3.8.
  • Ounces → grams: multiply by 28. (Weight ounces, not fluid.)
  • Inches → centimeters: multiply by 2.5 (exact is 2.54).
  • Fahrenheit → Celsius: subtract 32, then divide by 1.8. (Bonus — not on the poster, but the one temperature students always ask about.)

Two pitfalls this chart doesn’t warn you about

  1. U.S. gallon ≠ U.K. gallon. The U.S. gallon is 3.785 L. The U.K. (Imperial) gallon is 4.546 L — about 20% larger. When a British car ad says “50 mpg,” the U.S. equivalent is roughly 42 mpg.
  2. Weight ounces (oz) ≠ fluid ounces (fl oz). The poster’s 1 oz = 28.35 g is a weight. If a recipe says “8 fl oz of milk,” that’s a volume — 8 fl oz ≈ 237 mL. They’re close for water, but never assume they’re the same.

Frequently asked questions

What is the imperial system?
The imperial system (called the U.S. customary system in the U.S.) is the family of units that includes inches, feet, yards, miles, ounces, pounds, and gallons. It comes from British measurement traditions and is used mainly in the United States for everyday life. Most other countries use the metric system (meters, liters, grams).
Why is 1 inch exactly 2.54 cm?
It wasn’t always exact. In 1959 the U.S., U.K., Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa agreed on the “international inch,” defined as exactly 2.54 centimeters. That’s why every other length conversion on the poster follows from this one: 1 foot = 12 × 2.54 = 30.48 cm, 1 yard = 36 × 2.54 = 91.44 cm, and so on.
How do you convert miles to kilometers quickly?
Multiply miles by 1.609. For fast mental math, use 1.6: a 60 mph speed limit ≈ 60 × 1.6 = 96 km/h (actual: 96.6). Going the other way, divide kilometers by 1.6 (or multiply by 0.621). A 5K ≈ 3.1 miles; a marathon (26.2 mi) ≈ 42.2 km.
Are the U.S. gallon and the U.K. gallon the same?
No. A U.S. gallon = 3.785 L, but a U.K. (Imperial) gallon = 4.546 L — about 20% larger. That’s why British fuel economy in “miles per gallon” looks better than U.S. mpg for the same car. The poster uses the U.S. gallon.
What’s the difference between a fluid ounce and a weight ounce?
A weight ounce (oz) measures mass — 1 oz ≈ 28.35 g. A fluid ounce (fl oz) measures volume — 1 fl oz ≈ 29.57 mL. They’re close in size for water (which was the historical intent), but for anything denser or lighter than water they are different. This poster shows the weight ounce.
Why do U.S. students still need to learn the metric system?
Because the rest of the world uses it, and so does all of science. Every SAT and ACT math section, every AP science course, every college physics and chemistry class works in meters, liters, grams, and Celsius. Fluency in both systems is what letting kids read a recipe, a road sign, a nutrition label, or a lab manual anywhere in the world — and it’s a core middle-school math standard.
When do U.S. students learn imperial-to-metric conversion?
Formal conversion between systems typically starts in grades 4–6 as part of measurement and ratio units, and it’s revisited in grade 6 under the Common Core ratio and unit-rate standards. At SOMATH we cover it in the pre-algebra and middle-school tracks — and use it again in SAT/ACT word problems.
