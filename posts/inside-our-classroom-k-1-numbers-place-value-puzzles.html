1. Numbers, digits, and writing the names

We started where every strong math foundation has to start: with the distinction between a digit, a symbol, and a name. The number five is a quantity. The symbol 5 is one way to write it. The word five is another. Six- and seven-year-olds can hold all three at once, but only if you slow down and let them. So the first thing we did was write the names of the numbers from one to nine in the notebook: O-N-E, T-W-O, T-H-R-E-E, and so on through N-I-N-E. The students sounded out every letter together. This is not handwriting practice. It is a deliberate move to anchor the symbol to the word and the word to the quantity, so that later, when they read a word problem that says “nine cookies,” the connection is automatic.

2. The number line and what zero really means

Next we drew a number line on the whiteboard. We placed one through nine on the line, drew an arrow on the right end, and asked the students why the arrow was there. The answer is the first piece of mathematical maturity any child needs: the arrow shows that the numbers keep increasing forever. Then we asked about zero.

Almost every child says zero means nothing. That is wrong, and we said so clearly. Zero is not nothing. Zero is something. Zero degrees Fahrenheit is very cold. On the number line, zero is the origin — the place where everything starts. Distinguishing zero-as-origin from zero-as-absence is one of those quiet conceptual shifts that pays dividends every time the student later meets negative numbers, the coordinate plane, or the y-intercept.

3. Why we count in tens: the decimal system

Then we asked: why do we count up to ten and then start over? Why not eight, or twelve? The students held up their hands and counted to ten on their fingers. That is the answer. The decimal system — the base-ten counting system we use every day — exists because human beings have ten fingers. Some ancient cultures used base-twelve, base-twenty, or base-sixty (we still see traces of base-sixty in the way we count minutes and seconds). But base-ten won because it matched the body of the person doing the counting. Teaching this in Kindergarten removes a question children carry around silently for years.

4. Place value: ones, tens, hundreds, thousands

Once the students understood that we group by tens, place value is the natural next step. We drew a four-column chart on the board, labeled ones · tens · hundreds · thousands, and the students copied it into their notebooks. We deliberately ask the children to write — not just listen — because handwriting cements the structure in memory the way listening alone cannot.

Then we built the columns up visually:

  • Ones — a single point. Zero through nine.
  • Tens — ten ones lined up. That is a line.
  • Hundreds — ten tens stacked. That is a square (a plane). 10 × 10.
  • Thousands — ten hundreds stacked. That is a cube. 10 × 10 × 10.

We then compared multi-digit numbers using the chart. Which is bigger, 23 or 45? You check the tens digit first. Which is bigger, 4,215 or 3,678? You check the thousands digit first. The students caught on inside two minutes. That is the entire idea of comparing whole numbers, taught early enough that no one ever has to memorize it as a rule.

Why we plant place value early. In New York City public schools, place value to the thousands becomes a formal topic in 2nd and 3rd grade. By introducing it visually now — as a chart the child has already drawn and used — we make sure the topic feels familiar, not new, when it arrives. Children who have already “seen the column structure” almost never struggle with carrying or regrouping later.

5. Addition with carrying: a real word problem

Now we move from concept to computation, but inside a story. We posed a word problem children can picture:

Word problem

Ria has 27 stickers. Sam has 15 stickers. How many stickers do they have together?

Set up the columns: Tens digit and ones digit, lined up vertically.
Ones column: 7 + 5. Count up on fingers: 8, 9, 10, 11, 12. We have 12, which is more than 9, so we cannot write 12 in the ones column. We write the 2 and carry the 1 into the tens column.
Tens column: 2 + 1 + 1 (the carried one) = 4.
Answer: 42 stickers together.

The whole point of using a real word problem — rather than a bare 27 + 15 — is that the children remember the story. Six months from now they will not remember the digits, but they will remember Ria, Sam, and the stickers, and that memory pulls the procedure right back into their heads.

6. The four-digit number enigma

To finish the class we did a real puzzle. We told the students they were going to be detectives, like Sherlock Holmes. Then we put the following on the board:

Number enigma

Can you find the four-digit number?

Clue 1. The thousands digit is 2.
Clue 2. The hundreds digit is three more than the thousands digit.
Clue 3. The tens digit is the same as the thousands digit.
Clue 4. The ones digit is zero.

How the children solved it. They drew the place-value chart, dropped 2 into the thousands column, added 3 to get 5 in the hundreds column, copied the 2 into the tens column, and wrote 0 in the ones column. The answer revealed itself: 2,520.

This is what age-appropriate math puzzles look like at age 6: real reasoning, no tricks, and an answer the child arrives at by combining the morning’s lesson with a small piece of new logic. There is no “math gene” required to do this. There is only practice with the right structure.

7. Why this matters at age 5, 6, and 7

If you have a child going into 1st grade in September on the Upper West Side, this is the level of math thinking they are capable of right now — not in 3rd grade, not in 4th grade, not “eventually.” The instinct in most American math classrooms is to wait. Wait until 2nd grade to introduce place value to thousands. Wait until 3rd grade to do real puzzles. Wait until 4th grade to ask conceptual questions about why we count in tens.

That waiting is the single biggest reason American students fall behind their peers in countries where place value, the decimal system, and arithmetic structure are introduced earlier and more concretely. Our position at SOMATH is simple: if you can teach the idea in a way a 6-year-old can hold, you should. Children do not need to be protected from real mathematics. They need to be invited into it.

8. How to practice this week at home

If you watched the video with your child and want to make this stick, do these five things this week. They take about ten minutes a day, no flashcards required.

  • Have your child write the words for the numbers one through nine in their notebook (or chalk on the sidewalk). Then ten, eleven, twelve, twenty, fifty, one hundred. Saying and writing together is the trick.
  • Draw a number line on a sheet of paper. Mark zero clearly. Ask: “What does zero mean?” Listen to the answer. Then explain: zero is the origin, not nothing.
  • Build a place-value chart — just four columns: ones, tens, hundreds, thousands. Read license plates, addresses (we are at 226 W 79th St — what is the hundreds digit?), and prices into the chart.
  • Pose one word problem a day that requires carrying. Use real things in the house: cookies, stickers, crayons, LEGO bricks. The names matter more than the numbers.
  • Play a number enigma game. Pick a four-digit number, then give your child clues one at a time. Let them solve it like a detective. You can copy our puzzle structure from section 6.
