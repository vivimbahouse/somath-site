Statistics and variation: learning from data

Statistics is the science of learning from data. The image asks one question of five students, “How many minutes does it take you to get to school?”, and gets five different answers: 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30 minutes. Same question, different answers. That difference is the raw material of the whole course.

Variation means that values differ from one individual to another. Commute times vary because students live at different distances, travel in different ways and leave at different times. Even one student’s commute varies from day to day. If every value were identical, there would be nothing to analyze; one measurement would tell the whole story.

Statistics gives us three jobs to do with variation. First, describe it: what values occur, which are typical, and how spread out they are (here, from 10 to 30 minutes, a 20-minute spread). Second, look for patterns: do students who take the bus have longer commutes? Third, draw conclusions that go beyond the five values, carefully and with an honest statement of uncertainty.

AP habit: whenever you see data, ask “How much do these values vary, and why might they vary?” before computing anything.

Practice: try these five questions

Q1

Twelve students answer “How many hours did you sleep last night?”: 6, 7.5, 8, 6.5, 9, 7, 8, 5.5, 7, 8.5, 6, 7. Do these data show variation? Explain what variation means in this context.

Answer

Yes. Sleep times vary from 5.5 to 9 hours.

  1. Variation means the values differ from one individual (student) to another.
  2. The smallest value is 5.5 hours and the largest is 9 hours, so the answers are not all the same.
  3. The spread is 9 − 5.5 = 3.5 hours, so sleep time varies across these twelve students.

Q2

Which situation would show no variation?

  1. Shoe sizes of the students in a class
  2. Number of wheels on the standard bicycles in a bike rack
  3. Daily high temperatures in New York City during March
  4. Time each student needs to finish a math quiz
Answer

B. Every standard bicycle has 2 wheels.

  1. Shoe sizes, March high temperatures and quiz-finishing times all differ from one individual to another.
  2. Every standard bicycle has exactly 2 wheels, so the value is the same for every individual.
  3. With no variation there is nothing to describe or explain, so choice B is the answer.

Q3

A coffee shop times five lattes (in seconds): 95, 110, 102, 130, 98. Apply the three jobs of statistics (describe, look for patterns, draw conclusions) to these data.

Answer

Describe: 95 to 130 s, most near 95–110 s. Pattern: one unusually slow latte (130 s). Conclusion: lattes usually take under 2 minutes, but five drinks give limited evidence.

  1. Describe: the times range from 95 to 130 seconds; four of the five are between 95 and 110 seconds.
  2. Look for patterns: 130 seconds stands apart from the others. It could reflect a rush, a new barista or a complicated order.
  3. Draw conclusions: a typical latte here takes a little under 2 minutes (120 s). With only five drinks, the conclusion should be stated cautiously.

Q4

A runner times the same one-mile route on five days: 8:12, 8:05, 8:31, 8:09 and 8:18 (minutes:seconds). Is this variation? If so, what are the individuals, and why might the values differ?

Answer

Yes. The individuals are the five runs; the times differ from run to run.

  1. Here each run is an individual, and its time is the recorded value.
  2. The times differ, from 8:05 to 8:31, a spread of 26 seconds, so the data show variation.
  3. Possible reasons: weather, wind, sleep, energy, traffic lights or small timing errors. Variation can occur within one person across repeated measurements.

Q5

Explain why answering “How many minutes do students in our class spend on homework each night?” requires statistics, but answering “How many days are in a week?” does not.

Answer

The homework question has answers that vary across students; the week question has one fixed answer.

  1. Different students spend different amounts of time on homework, so we must collect data and describe the variation.
  2. Every week has 7 days. There is no variation, so no data analysis is needed.
  3. Statistics is needed when we expect the values to differ from one individual to another.

Individuals and variables

Every data set answers two questions: who or what are we studying, and which characteristics are we recording? The answers name the individuals and the variables.

Individuals are the people, animals or objects described by a data set. They do not have to be people: they can be dogs, phones, trails, restaurants or days. In the image, the individuals are three students: Maya, Leo and Ava.

Variables are the characteristics recorded for each individual. In the table, three variables are recorded: commute time (minutes), travel method and number of siblings. A variable takes different values for different individuals; for example, commute time is 15 for Maya, 25 for Leo and 10 for Ava.

In a data table, each row describes one individual and each characteristic column records one variable. The name column identifies the individuals; it is a label, not a characteristic we analyze. So this table has 3 individuals and 3 variables.

A common error is to call the individuals “the variable.” In “a survey of households about the number of cars they own,” the households are the individuals and number of cars is the variable.

Categorical variables

A categorical variable places each individual into one of several groups, or categories. Its values identify group membership; they do not measure an amount.

The image shows three examples. Travel method sorts students into walk, bus or subway. Blood type sorts people into A, B, AB or O. Preferred school subject sorts students into math, English or science. For the question “How do you usually get to school?”, Maya is in the Walk group, Leo is in the Bus group and Ava is in the Subway group.

Category values may be words (Bus), symbols (AB) or numerical labels (a code such as 2 = Bus). A yes/no variable, such as “Has a library card?”, is categorical with two categories.

Some categories have a natural order, such as shirt sizes S, M, L. The variable is still categorical: “L minus M” has no numerical meaning. AP Statistics sometimes calls these ordinal categorical variables.

How to summarize: for a categorical variable we count how many individuals fall in each category and report counts or percents. An “average category” does not make sense.

Q12

A survey asks students, “Which sport do you play most often?” Name the variable, state its type and give three possible categories.

Answer

Variable: sport played most often. Type: categorical. Categories, for example: soccer, basketball, tennis.

  1. The characteristic recorded for each student is the sport he or she plays most often.
  2. Each answer puts the student into a group; it does not measure an amount, so the variable is categorical.
  3. Possible categories: soccer, basketball, tennis (also swimming, none, and so on).

Q13

A team records each player’s shirt size: S, M, L or XL. The sizes have a natural order. Is shirt size categorical or quantitative? Explain.

Answer

Categorical (with ordered categories).

  1. Each value places a player into a size group.
  2. The order S < M < L < XL is real, but differences such as “L minus M” have no numerical meaning.
  3. Ordered groups are still categories, so shirt size is a categorical (ordinal) variable.

Q14

A café records the drink type of eight orders: latte, tea, latte, mocha, tea, latte, latte, tea. Does it make sense to compute an “average drink type”? What summary makes sense instead?

Answer

No average. Use counts or percents: latte 4 (50%), tea 3 (37.5%), mocha 1 (12.5%).

  1. Drink type is categorical, so the values cannot be added or averaged.
  2. Count each category: latte 4, tea 3, mocha 1. Check: 4 + 3 + 1 = 8 orders.
  3. Percents: 4/8 = 50%, 3/8 = 37.5%, 1/8 = 12.5%. The most common drink is the latte.

Q15

Age in years is quantitative. Show how to turn age into a categorical variable.

Answer

Group ages into categories, for example: under 13, 13–17, 18 and older.

  1. Create non-overlapping groups that cover every possible age, such as “under 13,” “13–17” and “18 and older.”
  2. Each person is now placed into exactly one age group.
  3. The new variable, age group, is categorical because its values name groups instead of amounts.

Quantitative variables: counts and measurements

A quantitative variable records a numerical value that represents an amount. The amount is either a count (how many?) or a measurement (how much?).

In the image, height (168 cm) and study time (45 minutes) are measurements; number of siblings (2) is a count. For commute time, Maya’s 15 minutes, Leo’s 25 minutes and Ava’s 10 minutes are values of one quantitative variable whose unit is minutes.

Quantitative values usually come with units (minutes, centimeters, dollars, people). Always state the unit; “25” alone is not a complete answer.

The key test: differences between values have numerical meaning. Leo’s commute is 25 − 10 = 15 minutes longer than Ava’s. Averages also make sense: the mean of 15, 25 and 10 is 50 ÷ 3 ≈ 16.7 minutes.

Ask “How many?” or “How much?” If the question makes sense and the arithmetic has meaning, the variable is quantitative. Numbers used only as labels are categorical (Topic 6).

Q18

Three dogs weigh 18 lb, 42 lb and 65 lb. Does 65 − 18 = 47 have a meaning? What does this tell you about the variable?

Answer

Yes: the heaviest dog weighs 47 lb more than the lightest. Weight is quantitative.

  1. Subtract the lightest weight from the heaviest: 65 − 18 = 47 pounds.
  2. The difference is a real amount of weight with a unit, pounds.
  3. Because differences have numerical meaning, weight is a quantitative variable.

  3. Points are counted, so quiz score is a quantitative count.

Discrete and continuous quantitative variables

Quantitative variables come in two types.

A discrete variable has a set of separate, countable possible values, usually from counting. Number of siblings can be 0, 1, 2, 3, …, with gaps between the possible values: you cannot have 2.5 siblings.

A continuous variable can take any value within an interval, usually from measuring. A height could be 168 cm, 168.4 cm or 168.42 cm. Between any two possible heights there is another possible height. How many decimals we see depends on the precision of the measuring tool, not on the variable.

In the image, number of books in a backpack is discrete (count), while time spent studying and mass of a backpack are continuous (measurements).

Watch the rounding trap: a continuous measurement may be rounded when recorded. Commute times written as whole minutes are still continuous because time itself flows continuously. Ask what the variable can be in principle, not how it happens to be written down. (Money is technically counted in cents, but amounts with many possible values, such as prices or incomes, are often treated as continuous.)

Q22

Can “number of students absent today” equal 3.5? What does your answer tell you about the variable?

Answer

No. The variable is discrete.

  1. Students are counted as whole people: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, …
  2. Values between counts, such as 3.5, are impossible.
  3. Separate, countable possible values make the variable discrete.

Q23

A bathroom scale shows a cat’s weight as 9 lb, a kitchen scale shows 9.2 lb and a laboratory scale shows 9.17 lb. Is the cat’s weight discrete or continuous? Explain.

Answer

Continuous.

  1. All three readings describe the same cat; they differ only in precision.
  2. A more precise tool can show more decimal places, so weight can take any value in an interval.
  3. Precision depends on the measuring tool; the variable itself is continuous.

Q24

Race times are recorded to the nearest second (for example, 1,382 seconds). A classmate says, “The recorded values are whole numbers, so race time is discrete.” Respond to the classmate.

Answer

Race time is continuous; the values were only rounded when recorded.

  1. Time can be 1,382.4 or 1,382.47 seconds; any value in an interval is possible.
  2. Recording to the nearest second rounds the measurement but does not change the nature of the variable.
  3. Classify by what the variable can be, not by how it is written: race time is continuous.

Q25

Name one discrete variable and one continuous variable that could be recorded about a car.

Answer

For example: discrete, number of doors; continuous, fuel used on a trip. Other correct answers are possible.

  1. Discrete: something counted, such as number of doors, seats or previous owners.
  2. Continuous: something measured, such as speed, fuel used, weight or length of the car.

Numbers as categories

A number can be a label rather than an amount. Numerical labels identify groups or individuals; they do not measure quantity.

In the image, ZIP code 10024 identifies a postal area, jersey number 23 identifies a player and the code 1 = Walk, 2 = Bus, 3 = Subway identifies a travel method. All three are categorical variables even though their values are written with digits.

Same format, different meaning: jersey number 23 is categorical (a label for a player), while points scored 23 is quantitative (a count of points).

Two quick tests: Does a difference mean anything? Does an average describe an amount? Jersey 23 minus jersey 10 is not “13 of anything,” and the average of category codes does not describe an amount of travel. If the arithmetic is meaningless, the numbers are labels.

Rule to remember: ask what the number means, not just how it looks.

Q27

A survey codes eye color as 1 = brown, 2 = blue, 3 = green, 4 = hazel. Eight responses are 1, 1, 2, 4, 3, 1, 2, 1. A student reports “the mean eye color is 1.875.” What is wrong, and what summary should be reported?

The codes are labels, so a mean is meaningless. Report counts: brown 4, blue 2, green 1, hazel 1.

Reading a data table

Reading a data table puts every idea from this class together. Identify three things: the individuals (rows), the variables (columns) and the recorded values (cells).

In the image, each row describes one student: Maya, Leo and Ava. Student names serve as identifiers. Each characteristic column holds one variable: travel method, commute time and number of siblings. A single cell gives one value for one individual; for example, Maya’s commute time is 15 minutes.

Then classify each variable: travel method is categorical; commute time is quantitative, continuous (recorded times are rounded to whole minutes); number of siblings is quantitative, discrete.

Two reading directions: read across a row to learn about one individual (“Leo takes the bus, his commute is 25 minutes and he has 2 siblings”); read down a column to compare one variable across individuals (“commute times range from 10 to 25 minutes”).

Investigating variation and statistical questions

Statistics starts with a good question. A statistical question anticipates variation in the data and is answered by collecting data. “How long are the commutes of students in our class?” is statistical: we expect different answers from different students.

A question about one individual, such as “How long was Maya’s commute today?”, has a single answer. It is not a statistical question, even though its answer is a number.

Once we see variation, we ask why it happens. Commute times of 15, 25 and 10 minutes might differ because of distance from school, travel method, or traffic and delays. These are possible explanations to investigate, not conclusions. To test one, record the explanatory characteristic as another variable (for example, distance) and compare.

Habit for the whole course: expect differences, ask questions and learn from data.

Rewrite “How many books did Jonah read this summer?” as a statistical question.

Answer

For example: “How many books did students in our grade read this summer?”

  1. The original asks about one individual, Jonah, so it has one answer.
  2. Widen the question to a group so that variation is expected.
  3. Answering it requires collecting data from many students.

Q38

Students’ times to finish the same crossword puzzle vary from 4 to 19 minutes. Give three possible explanations for this variation. Are they conclusions?

Answer

For example: puzzle experience, vocabulary, distractions. They are possible explanations to investigate, not conclusions.

  1. Experience: students who solve puzzles often may be faster.
  2. Vocabulary or reading speed, and distractions or effort, may also matter.
  3. None is proven by the times alone; each would require recording another variable and comparing.

Q39

Six tomato plants have heights of 42, 51, 38, 47, 55 and 44 cm after six weeks. A student concludes, “The plants that get more sun are taller.” Is this conclusion supported by these data? What would you need to record?

Answer

Not supported. Record each plant’s amount of sunlight and compare it with height.

  1. The data show variation in height (38 to 55 cm) but contain no information about sunlight.
  2. Sunlight is a possible explanation to investigate, not a conclusion.
  3. Record sunlight (for example, hours per day) for each plant as a second variable, then compare heights across sunlight levels.

Individuals: 50 gym members. Membership type: categorical. Visits: quantitative, discrete. Workout length: quantitative, continuous.

A data set describes 200 Manhattan apartments: neighborhood, monthly rent ($), number of bedrooms, floor area (square feet) and whether pets are allowed. Classify each variable as categorical or quantitative. Which quantitative variable is discrete?

Categorical: neighborhood, pets allowed. Quantitative: rent, bedrooms, floor area. Discrete: number of bedrooms.

  1. Neighborhood and pets allowed (yes/no) place apartments into groups.

A survey codes favorite season as 1 = winter, 2 = spring, 3 = summer, 4 = fall. The 20 responses are: 3, 3, 1, 2, 4, 3, 2, 3, 4, 1, 3, 2, 3, 4, 3, 1, 2, 3, 4, 3. A student reports, “The average season is 2.7, between spring and summer.” Explain the error and give an appropriate summary.

W11

A café owner thinks wait times are longer on weekends. Weekday waits (minutes): 3.5, 4.0, 2.5, 5.0, 3.0. Weekend waits: 6.5, 4.5, 8.0, 5.5, 7.0. (a) Name and classify the two variables. (b) Find the average wait for each. (c) Do the data support the owner’s idea? Mention variation in your answer.

Answer

(a) Wait time: quantitative, continuous; day type: categorical. (b) Weekday 3.6 min; weekend 6.3 min. (c) Yes, the weekend average is 2.7 minutes longer, but the times overlap and this is only 10 orders.

  1. (a) Wait time is measured; day type (weekday or weekend) places each order in a group.
  2. (b) Weekday: 3.5 + 4.0 + 2.5 + 5.0 + 3.0 = 18.0, and 18.0 ÷ 5 = 3.6. Weekend: 6.5 + 4.5 + 8.0 + 5.5 + 7.0 = 31.5, and 31.5 ÷ 5 = 6.3.
  3. (c) 6.3 − 3.6 = 2.7 minutes longer on weekends. Waits still vary within each group, and one weekday wait (5.0) exceeds one weekend wait (4.5). More customers on weekends is a possible explanation to investigate.

Quick questions and answers

What is variation in statistics?

Variation means that values differ from one individual to another. When students answer “How many minutes does it take you to get to school?” with 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30 minutes, the commute times vary. Statistics describes that variation, looks for patterns and draws conclusions.

What is the difference between individuals and variables?

Individuals are the people or objects a data set describes; each row of a data table is one individual. Variables are the characteristics recorded for each individual; each characteristic column is one variable.

How do you tell a categorical variable from a quantitative variable?

A categorical variable places each individual into a group, such as travel method or blood type. A quantitative variable records a count or a measurement, such as number of siblings or height, and differences between its values have numerical meaning. Ask “How many?” or “How much?”

What is the difference between discrete and continuous variables?

A discrete variable has separate, countable possible values, such as number of siblings (0, 1, 2, …). A continuous variable can take any value within an interval, such as height or time. A continuous measurement may be rounded when it is recorded, but it is still continuous.

Can a number be a categorical variable?

Yes. ZIP codes, jersey numbers, room numbers and survey codes are numerical labels. They identify groups or individuals and do not measure quantity, so they are categorical. Averaging them does not describe an amount.

What makes a question statistical?

A statistical question anticipates variation in the data and is answered by collecting data. “How long are the commutes of students in our class?” is statistical; “How long was Maya’s commute today?” is a question about one individual.
