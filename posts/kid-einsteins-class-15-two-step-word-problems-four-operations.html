Understanding the problem

Read the final question first, then read the whole story. Identify what you know, what you need to find, and the unit your answer should use. A two-step problem needs an intermediate result: something useful you must find before you can answer the final question.

In a shopping story, “How much change?” is not the same as “How much did it cost?” First find the total cost, then subtract it from the amount paid. Label your two results so you do not accidentally stop after the first calculation. Information that does not help answer the question can be left unused.

Try it yourself: five questions

Q1 Practice 1 of 5

A family buys 3 planetarium tickets for $7 each and pays with $25. How much change should the family receive?

Answer

$4 in change.

  1. Find the cost of all three tickets. 3 × 7 = 21.
  2. Subtract the cost from the amount paid. 25 − 21 = 4.

Q2 Practice 2 of 5

A school shop has 5 packs with 4 notebooks in each pack. It sells 7 notebooks. How many notebooks remain?

Answer

13 notebooks remain.

  1. Find the starting number of notebooks. 5 × 4 = 20.
  2. Remove the notebooks sold. 20 − 7 = 13.

Q3 Practice 3 of 5

Thirty-six crayons are shared equally among 6 children. One child receives 3 extra crayons. How many crayons does that child have now?

Answer

9 crayons.

  1. Find one child’s equal share. 36 ÷ 6 = 6.
  2. Add the extras to that child’s share. 6 + 3 = 9.

Q4 Practice 4 of 5

A museum opens at 9 a.m. A class buys 5 tickets at $4 each and pays with $30. How much change does the class receive?

Answer

$10 in change.

  1. Find the ticket cost. The opening time is not needed. 5 × 4 = 20.
  2. Subtract the ticket cost from the amount paid. 30 − 20 = 10.

Q5 Practice 5 of 5

A reading corner has 32 books. A teacher adds 14 books, and children borrow 9. How many books are there now?

Answer

37 books.

  1. Find the total after the books are added. 32 + 14 = 46.
  2. Subtract the borrowed books. 46 − 9 = 37.

Planning two steps

Say your plan in words before calculating: “First I will find ____. Then I will use that answer to find ____.” The second step must use the information found in the first step, not a number chosen just because it appears in the story.

A quick picture or bar model can show what is missing. Draw equal groups when every group has the same number, or draw a whole split into parts when sharing. Sometimes you must combine two amounts before dividing. Choose the order from the story, not from the order in which the numbers are written.

Choosing the operations

Addition combines amounts. Subtraction can take away an amount, compare two amounts, or find a missing part. Multiplication finds the total in equal groups. Division can find the size of each equal share or the number of equal groups.

Do not choose an operation from one keyword alone. “More” might describe a comparison whose missing amount requires subtraction. Ask what each quantity represents, then explain why the operation fits. Use two separate number sentences while you are learning to plan.

Choose an operation from the situation
OperationWhat it can help you find
AddA combined amount or an amount after more arrives
SubtractWhat remains, a difference, or a missing part
MultiplyThe total in equal groups
DivideOne equal share or the number of equal groups

Q14 Practice 4 of 5

A shop has 27 orange balloons and 18 green balloons. It puts all the balloons into bunches of 5. How many bunches can it make? Name both operations.

Answer

Q15 Practice 5 of 5

Liam has 4 bags with 6 marbles in each bag. Ava has 31 marbles. How many more marbles does Ava have than Liam?

Answer

7 more marbles.

  1. Multiply to find Liam’s total. 4 × 6 = 24.
  2. Compare Ava’s total with Liam’s total by subtracting. 31 − 24 = 7.

Addition and subtraction

When a story adds items and then removes some, first find the new total and then subtract what leaves. If items leave first and more arrive later, follow that sequence. The result of the first calculation becomes the starting amount for the second.

Keep track of the same kind of object through both steps. Check your result by reversing the changes: undo a subtraction with addition and undo an addition with subtraction. You should arrive back at the original amount.

Try it yourself: five questions

Q16 Practice 1 of 5

A library cart has 37 books. A librarian adds 26 books, and students borrow 18. How many books remain on the cart?

Answer

45 books remain.

  1. Find the total after the addition. 37 + 26 = 63.
  2. Remove the borrowed books. 63 − 18 = 45.
  3. Check by undoing the steps in reverse order: 45 + 18 = 63; then 63 − 26 = 37. Both original quantities are recovered.

  2. Add those who get on. 28 + 9 = 37.
  3. Check by undoing the steps in reverse order: 37 − 9 = 28; then 28 + 17 = 45. Both original quantities are recovered.

Q20 Practice 5 of 5

A class collects 125 cans on Monday and 48 on Tuesday. It sends 67 cans for recycling. How many cans are waiting to be sent?

Answer

106 cans.

  1. Combine the cans from both days. 125 + 48 = 173.
  2. Subtract the cans already sent. 173 − 67 = 106.
  3. Check by undoing the steps in reverse order: 106 + 67 = 173; then 173 − 48 = 125. Both original quantities are recovered.

Multiply, then add or subtract

When there are equal groups, multiply the number of groups by the number in each group to find the total. Then add extra items or subtract items that are taken away. Check that the groups really are equal before multiplying.

Be precise about what changes. “Add three chairs altogether” is different from “add three chairs to every row.” In these practice problems, the extra or removed amount changes the whole total once. If you pay for several identical items, find their total cost before calculating change.

Divide, then adjust one share

Equal sharing starts with division: total amount divided by number of recipients gives the amount for one recipient. After that, an addition or subtraction can change one share. The second calculation uses one share, not the original total.

Read the final question carefully. If one student receives extra pencils, you are finding that student’s new amount, not the number of pencils everywhere. Label the first result “per student,” “per table,” or “per bag” to keep its meaning clear.

More practice with equal shares

This section revisits division followed by addition or subtraction. Keep the original total and one share separate in your thinking. A bar model split into equal parts can show why changing one part is not the same as changing the whole.

Check in reverse order: first undo the change to the single share, then multiply that original share by the number of recipients. The result should match the starting total. The repeated poster supports a second round of new problems.

Writing and checking the answer

Show both calculations, label the intermediate amount, and finish with an answer to the actual question. “Thirty-two” alone is less clear than “Thirty-two badges remain.” If you found a cost but the question asks for change, you still have a step to do.

Use inverse operations to check in reverse order. Addition and subtraction undo each other; multiplication and division undo each other. Also check the story: a total should increase when more items arrive and decrease when items leave. An estimate can catch a large error, but it does not replace checking the exact calculation.

Quick questions and answers

How do you solve a two-step word problem?

Identify the final question, plan what to find first, calculate that intermediate result, and use it in the second calculation. Label the answer and check that it fits the story.

Does a two-step problem use all four operations at once?

No. A two-step problem uses two calculation steps. This lesson offers practice choosing among addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division; a single problem does not need all four.

How do I choose between multiplication and division?

Multiply to find the total in equal groups when you know the number of groups and the amount in each. Divide to find an equal share or the number of groups when you know the total.

Should I always calculate in the order the numbers appear?

No. Choose the order from what each quantity means and what the question asks. You may need to find a cost before change, or combine amounts before sharing them equally.

Why does it matter whether one share or the whole total changes?

After equal sharing, an extra amount given to one child changes that child’s share. It should be added after division. Adding it to the original total and dividing would spread the extra amount among everyone instead.
