1. What’s actually on the SHSAT math section

The SHSAT math section has 57 questions in 57 minutes, no calculator allowed. Roughly 5 of the 57 questions are grid-in (student-produced response); the rest are 4-choice multiple choice. The math content maps closely to the standard grade 7–8 New York State curriculum plus a few early grade-9 topics.

The tested content bands are:

  • Arithmetic and number properties — divisibility, primes, GCD/LCM, order of operations, absolute value.
  • Fractions, decimals, percents — conversions, operations, percent change, discount and markup, percent word problems.
  • Ratios and proportions — scaling, unit rates, similar figures.
  • Exponents and roots — laws of exponents, square roots, scientific notation.
  • Linear equations and inequalities — solving in one variable, translating word problems, systems of two equations.
  • Coordinate geometry — slope, intercepts, distance, midpoint, graphing linear equations.
  • Plane geometry — angles, triangles (including similar and right), quadrilaterals, circles (arcs, sectors), area, perimeter, volume of prisms and cylinders.
  • Probability and statistics — mean, median, mode, range, simple probability, counting.
  • Multi-step word problems — the piece that separates a 550 from a 700 more than any other single skill.

The exam does not test calculus, trigonometry, or advanced algebra 2. But it does test all of the above in 57 seconds per question on average, without a calculator, with a testmaker who is deliberately writing questions that penalize sloppy arithmetic. That’s the piece parents underestimate.

2. Enrichment vs. test prep — why the difference matters

Math enrichment and SHSAT test prep are not substitutes. They do different jobs.

Dimension Math enrichment SHSAT test prep
Job Build underlying math fluency Teach the exam — format, pacing, strategy
Timeline Multi-year (grades 3–7) 3–9 months (grades 7–8)
Content Fractions, ratios, algebra, geometry — done deeply Same content, but at exam speed and under time pressure
What it fixes “My child doesn’t understand this material.” “My child understands it, but not fast enough.”
Typical outcome alone Strong math foundation, 550–600 SHSAT diagnostic +100–150 point gain from starting score
Combined outcome Enrichment first, prep on top — typical 650–700+ math score.

The mistake most NYC families make is not doing enrichment or not doing prep — it’s doing only prep. A student who sits through a 12-week prep course without a solid grade 6–7 math foundation ends up learning strategy for content they can’t actually solve. The strategy compounds nothing.

3. When to start: grade-by-grade timeline

Grade 5 (age 10) — ideal for Stuyvesant-track families

Verdict: start enrichment now. You have 30+ months before test day.

Grade 5 is the ideal starting point for families with a Stuyvesant, Bronx Science, or specialized-track goal. At this age, enrichment is still about fraction and decimal fluency, multi-digit operations, ratios, and word-problem structure — the algebra prerequisites. Two full years of building this foundation before Young Fermats means your child enters pre-algebra already confident, and enters SHSAT prep already scoring above a 600 diagnostic. This is the Kid Einsteins track.

Grade 6 (age 11) — the “on-time” start

Verdict: start now. This is when most successful SHSAT students begin.

Grade 6 is the modal starting age for serious NYC SHSAT families. The child has 18–24 months to the October grade 8 exam. Enrichment shifts from arithmetic fluency to pre-algebra: negative numbers, exponents, roots, ratios and proportions, linear equations, basic geometry. This is the material the SHSAT tests directly. Students who start enrichment in grade 6 typically test above 550 on a first diagnostic without any test-prep work at all.

Grade 7 (age 12) — enrichment + light prep

Verdict: start enrichment now. Layer in some SHSAT-specific work in the spring.

Grade 7 is still an excellent starting age. The child has about 12 months to the exam. This is the last full year to build algebra fluency — linear equations, systems, exponents, geometry — before test prep needs to become the primary activity. A grade 7 student in Young Fermats through the school year, then a spring/summer SHSAT prep intensive, is a realistic path to a top-tier score.

Grade 8 (age 13) — test prep is now the main activity

Verdict: run enrichment and prep in parallel. Realistic goal-setting matters.

Grade 8 families come in two shapes. Shape one: the child has already done enrichment in grades 5–7, is scoring above 550 on a diagnostic, and needs a targeted prep course to add 100–150 points. That’s the standard path and it works. Shape two: the child has done no enrichment, is scoring below 500, and the family is starting from zero in July. That’s the hard path — see section 7 below.

5. A sample SHSAT-style problem, worked

Here’s a problem that’s representative of the middle of the SHSAT math section — the kind of two-step ratio problem that separates a 550 from a 650.

SHSAT-style problem

A recipe calls for flour and sugar in the ratio 5 : 3. If a baker uses 12 cups of sugar, how many cups of flour and sugar are used in total?

Show worked solution

Step 1 — set up the ratio. The ratio of flour to sugar is 5 : 3. Let flour = 5k and sugar = 3k for some scale factor k.

Step 2 — use the given sugar amount. We’re told sugar = 12, so 3k = 12, which gives k = 4.

Step 3 — find flour. Flour = 5k = 5 · 4 = 20 cups.

Step 4 — answer the question that was asked. The question asks for the total, not for flour. Total = 20 + 12 = 32 cups.

Why students miss it. The most common wrong answer is 20 — the amount of flour — because the student solved the ratio correctly and then didn’t re-read the question. On the SHSAT, this pattern — solve, then miss the final translation step — is worth 4–6 points across the whole math section. Enrichment fixes it; test prep alone usually doesn’t.

Students who’ve done Young Fermats solve this problem in under 60 seconds without hesitation because they’ve seen the ratio-scaling pattern 40–60 times across the year. Students who’ve only done test prep often get the ratio right, then answer 20 instead of 32 because the last translation step is unfamiliar under time pressure. That’s the whole difference between enrichment and prep in one problem.

6. Score targets for Stuyvesant, Bronx Science, Brooklyn Tech

SHSAT is scored out of a combined 800 (400 math + 400 English/reading, on a scale that’s not a straight 1:1 with the raw questions). Cutoffs vary year to year with cohort strength, but the working targets are:

Bronx Science ~530+ 620–680 Grade 5 or 6
Brooklyn Tech ~500+ 570–640 Grade 6 or 7
Staten Island Tech ~530+ 620–680 Grade 5 or 6
HSAS at Lehman, HSMSE at CCNY, Queens HSSE, Brooklyn Latin ~460–510 540–620 Grade 6 or 7

How to read this table. Cutoffs are approximate and change with each year’s cohort. Aim to score comfortably above the cutoff, not exactly at it — a 570 in a strong year is a rejection, and a 555 in a soft year can be an acceptance. Building a 20–40 point cushion is the point of starting enrichment early.

7. If your child is grade 8 and you’re starting now

This is the “we’re calling in July for the October exam” scenario. It’s hard but not hopeless. Here’s an honest plan.

  1. Diagnostic first. Take a full timed SHSAT practice test in the first week. Don’t skip this — without a starting score, you don’t know what’s realistic. This is what our free 30-minute evaluation is for.
  2. Run enrichment and prep in parallel. A grade 8 student without enrichment background needs both simultaneously. That typically means 2–4 hours per week of Young Fermats (algebra + geometry fill-in) plus 2–4 hours per week of SHSAT Prep (strategy and timed practice), through July, August, and September.
  3. Weight the schedule toward the math section. A grade 8 with a weak math foundation should spend 60–70% of prep time on math, not the standard 50/50 with English.
  4. Set a realistic school target. If the grade 8 starting diagnostic is 400–450, Brooklyn Tech and Brooklyn Latin are realistic targets; Stuyvesant usually isn’t, and pretending otherwise sets up disappointment. If the diagnostic is 500–550, Bronx Science becomes reachable with a strong summer. Above 550, Stuyvesant is on the table.
  5. Take three full timed mock exams before test day. The single highest-leverage thing a late-starting student can do is finish three full 3-hour mocks. Pacing is what breaks under real conditions, not knowledge.
