Understanding the story

A word problem is a little story with a math question inside it. Before choosing plus or minus, read the whole story and picture it. In the image, Mia has 14 stickers and gives 6 stickers to Leo, and the question asks how many stickers Mia has left.

Ask three questions every time: What do we know? (Mia starts with 14 and gives away 6.) What happens? (Some stickers leave Mia’s group.) What do we need to find? (How many stickers Mia has left.) Then predict: if a group gets smaller, the answer will be less than the starting number; if it grows, the answer will be greater.

Practice: try these five questions

Q1

Ben has 12 apples. He eats 3 of them. What do we know, what happens, and what do we need to find? Then solve.

Answer

9 apples

We know: Ben starts with 12 apples and eats 3. What happens: some apples leave his group. We need to find how many apples are left. The group gets smaller, so subtract: 12 − 3 = 9. Ben has 9 apples left.

Q2

Sofia has 9 shells. She finds 5 more shells at the beach. Will the answer be greater or less than 9? Why? Then solve.

Answer

Greater; 14 shells

Finding more shells makes Sofia’s group bigger, so the answer must be greater than 9. Add: 9 + 5. Make a ten: 9 + 1 = 10, and 10 + 4 = 14. Sofia has 14 shells.

Q3

There are 15 birds on a fence. Then 7 birds fly away. Will the answer be greater or less than 15? Find how many birds are still on the fence.

Answer

Less; 8 birds

Birds fly away, so the group gets smaller and the answer must be less than 15. Subtract: 15 − 7. Subtract to ten: 15 − 5 = 10, then 10 − 2 = 8. There are 8 birds still on the fence.

Q4

Read the story: “Tia has 11 pencils. Her dad gives her 6 more pencils. How many pencils does Tia have now?” Which sentence is the question? What do we need to find?

Answer

“How many pencils does Tia have now?”; 17 pencils

The question is the sentence that ends with a question mark. We need to find Tia’s total number of pencils after her dad gives her more. Her group grows, so add: 11 + 6 = 17. Tia has 17 pencils now.

Q5

Max says: “Jada has 18 grapes. She eats 4 grapes. So she has 22 grapes.” Without solving, how can you tell Max is wrong? What is the correct answer?

Answer

Eating makes fewer, so the answer must be less than 18; 14 grapes

When Jada eats grapes, her group gets smaller, so the answer must be less than 18. Max’s answer, 22, is greater than 18, so he added by mistake. Subtract: 18 − 4 = 14. Jada has 14 grapes.

Putting groups together

Some stories have two parts that are already there, and the question asks about both of them together. Words like altogether, in all, and total often point to this kind of story. The image shows 13 books on one shelf and 8 books on another shelf. The two parts make one whole.

We add because we are joining two groups: 13 + 8 = 21 books. A quick way to add is to make a ten: split 8 into 7 and 1, so 13 + 7 = 20 and 20 + 1 = 21. Nothing is added to or taken away from either shelf; we are only counting both groups together.

Practice: try these five questions

Q6

There are 9 red cars and 6 blue cars in a parking lot. How many cars are there altogether?

Answer

15 cars

The two parts are 9 red cars and 6 blue cars. We want the whole, so add: 9 + 6. Make a ten: 9 + 1 = 10, and 10 + 5 = 15. There are 15 cars altogether.

Q7

A class has 14 girls and 7 boys. How many children are in the class in all?

Answer

21 children

Join the two groups: 14 + 7. Make a ten: split 7 into 6 and 1. 14 + 6 = 20, and 20 + 1 = 21. There are 21 children in the class.

Q8

A farmer has 8 cows and 12 sheep. How many animals does the farmer have in all?

Answer

20 animals

The parts are 8 cows and 12 sheep. Add to find the whole: 12 + 8. Since 2 + 8 = 10, we get 10 + 10 = 20. The farmer has 20 animals.

Q9

One bag has 17 oranges. Another bag has 5 oranges. Name the two parts and the whole.

Answer

Parts: 17 and 5; whole: 22 oranges

The parts are the two bags: 17 oranges and 5 oranges. The whole is both bags together: 17 + 5. Make a ten: 17 + 3 = 20, then 20 + 2 = 22. The whole is 22 oranges.

Q10

Use make a ten to solve 18 + 6. Show each step.

Answer

24

Split 6 into 2 and 4, because 18 needs 2 more to make 20. 18 + 2 = 20. Then 20 + 4 = 24. So 18 + 6 = 24.

Adding more to a group

In a joining story, there is a starting group, then something happens that makes the group bigger. The image starts with 16 children on the playground; then 7 more children arrive. Words like then, more, join, arrive, and get on often describe this change.

Find the start, notice the change, and choose addition: 16 + 7 = 23 children. To add quickly, make a ten: 16 + 4 = 20, and the 3 left over make 23. We add because the group gets bigger, so the answer is greater than the starting number.

16 ducks

23 stickers

Q13

A bus has 19 people on it. At the next stop, 4 more people get on. How many people are on the bus now?

Answer

23 people

People get on, so the group grows: 19 + 4. Make a ten: 19 + 1 = 20, then 20 + 3 = 23. There are 23 people on the bus.

Q14

“Ella read 13 pages in the morning. In the evening, she read 9 more pages.” Find the start, the change, and the total number of pages she read.

Answer

Start 13, change 9 more, total 22 pages

Start: 13 pages. Change: 9 more pages. Add: 13 + 9. Make a ten: 13 + 7 = 20, then 20 + 2 = 22. Ella read 22 pages.

Q15

There were 27 balloons at a party. Then 6 more balloons were blown up. Lee writes 27 − 6. Is he right? Explain and solve.

Answer

No; 27 + 6 = 33 balloons

More balloons are blown up, so the group gets bigger and we must add, not subtract. 27 + 6: make a ten with 27 + 3 = 30, then 30 + 3 = 33. There are 33 balloons.

Taking away from a group

In a taking-away story, there is a starting group and then some of it leaves. The image shows Mia starting with 23 crayons and giving 8 crayons to Leo. Words like gives away, eats, uses, goes home, flies away, and left often describe this change.

Find the start, notice the change, and choose subtraction: 23 − 8 = 15 crayons. To subtract quickly, subtract to ten: take away 3 to reach 20, then take away the other 5 to reach 15. We subtract because some crayons are taken away, so the answer is less than the start.

Practice: try these five questions

Q16

There are 13 cookies on a plate. Sam eats 5 cookies. How many cookies are left?

Answer

8 cookies

Start: 13. Change: 5 are eaten, so subtract. Subtract to ten: 13 − 3 = 10, then 10 − 2 = 8. There are 8 cookies left.

Q17

There are 21 children on the playground. Then 6 children go home. How many children are still on the playground?

Answer

15 children

Children leave, so subtract: 21 − 6. Subtract to ten: 21 − 1 = 20, then 20 − 5 = 15. There are 15 children still on the playground.

Q18

Nora has 24 beads. She uses 7 beads to make a bracelet. How many beads does she have left?

Answer

17 beads

Using beads takes them away: 24 − 7. Subtract to ten: 24 − 4 = 20, then 20 − 3 = 17. Nora has 17 beads left.

Q19

A branch has 30 leaves. Then 9 leaves fall off. How many leaves are still on the branch?

Answer

21 leaves

Leaves fall off, so subtract: 30 − 9. Take away 10 instead: 30 − 10 = 20. We took away 1 too many, so add 1 back: 20 + 1 = 21. There are 21 leaves on the branch.

Q20

Leo has 22 toy cars. He gives 8 cars to his cousin. Explain why this story uses subtraction, then solve.

Answer

Cars leave Leo’s group; 14 cars

Leo’s group gets smaller because he gives cars away, so we subtract. 22 − 8: subtract to ten with 22 − 2 = 20, then 20 − 6 = 14. Leo has 14 cars left.

Comparing two groups

In a comparing story, nothing joins and nothing leaves. There are two separate groups, and the question asks how many more or how many fewer one group has. The image compares Mia’s 24 stickers with Leo’s 17 stickers.

To find the difference, subtract the smaller number from the larger number: 24 − 17 = 7. You can also count up from the smaller number: 17 + 3 = 20, 20 + 4 = 24, and 3 + 4 = 7. The same difference answers both questions: Mia has 7 more stickers than Leo, and Leo has 7 fewer stickers than Mia.

Practice: try these five questions

Q21

Ava has 15 stickers. Ben has 9 stickers. How many more stickers does Ava have than Ben?

Answer

6 more stickers

Compare by subtracting: 15 − 9. Count up from 9: 9 + 1 = 10, 10 + 5 = 15, and 1 + 5 = 6. Ava has 6 more stickers than Ben.

Q22

A red tower has 12 blocks. A blue tower has 20 blocks. How many fewer blocks does the red tower have?

Answer

8 fewer blocks

Find the difference: 20 − 12. Count up from 12 to 20: 12 + 8 = 20. The red tower has 8 fewer blocks than the blue tower.

Q23

Jon has 26 cards. Maya has 19 cards. How many more cards does Jon have than Maya?

Answer

7 more cards

Subtract to compare: 26 − 19. Count up: 19 + 1 = 20, 20 + 6 = 26, and 1 + 6 = 7. Jon has 7 more cards than Maya.

Q24

Box A has 18 crayons. Box B has 25 crayons. How many fewer crayons are in Box A?

Answer

7 fewer crayons

Compare: 25 − 18. Count up: 18 + 2 = 20, 20 + 5 = 25, and 2 + 5 = 7. Box A has 7 fewer crayons than Box B.

Q25

Zoe has 14 shells and Tim has 21 shells. Tim says: “The question says how many more, so I add: 14 + 21 = 35.” What is wrong? How many more shells does Tim have?

Answer

Comparing needs subtraction; 7 more shells

“How many more” here compares two groups that are already there; nobody gets new shells. To compare, subtract: 21 − 14. Count up: 14 + 6 = 20, 20 + 1 = 21, and 6 + 1 = 7. Tim has 7 more shells than Zoe.

Finding a missing part

Sometimes we know the whole and one part, and we need the other part. The image shows a box of 25 crayons. 17 crayons are blue, and the rest are red. A part-part-whole diagram shows the whole on top and the two parts below it.

To find the missing part, subtract: whole − known part = missing part. 25 − 17 = 8 red crayons. Then check with addition: the two parts must make the whole, and 17 + 8 = 25. Words like the rest and some are often clues that a part is missing.

Q27

Answer

7 striped fish

Whole − known part: 22 − 15. Count up: 15 + 5 = 20, 20 + 2 = 22, and 5 + 2 = 7. Check: 15 + 7 = 22. There are 7 striped fish.

Q28

A bag holds 30 marbles. 12 marbles are blue, and the rest are red. How many marbles are red?

Answer

18 red marbles

Whole: 30. Known part: 12. Missing part: 30 − 12 = 18. Check: 12 + 18 = 30. There are 18 red marbles.

Q29

Ana had 16 grapes. She ate some grapes. Now she has 9 grapes. How many grapes did she eat?

Answer

7 grapes

The whole is 16 grapes. One part is the 9 grapes left; the missing part is the grapes she ate. 16 − 9: count up from 9, 9 + 1 = 10, 10 + 6 = 16, and 1 + 6 = 7. Check: 9 + 7 = 16. Ana ate 7 grapes.

Q30

Fill in the blank: 14 + ___ = 23. Then write the subtraction that finds the missing part.

Answer

9; 23 − 14 = 9

The whole is 23 and one part is 14. Missing part: 23 − 14. Count up: 14 + 6 = 20, 20 + 3 = 23, and 6 + 3 = 9. Check: 14 + 9 = 23.

Choosing the operation

A single word cannot always tell us what to do. Both stories in the image use the word more. In Story A, Leo has 18 marbles and gets 7 more, so his group grows: 18 + 7 = 25. In Story B, Mia has 25 marbles and Leo has 18, and the question asks how many more Mia has, so we compare: 25 − 18 = 7.

Instead of hunting for one key word, explain what the numbers mean. Is a group growing, shrinking, being compared, or missing a part? Then finish the sentence: “I choose ___ because ___.” For example: I choose addition because 7 marbles join Leo’s group.

Answer

Addition; 22 cars

Subtraction; 9 more cars

Subtraction; 17 children

Q34

Q35

Which story needs subtraction? A: 14 frogs sit on a log, and 6 more frogs hop on. B: 14 frogs sit on a log, and 6 frogs hop off. C: 14 green frogs and 6 brown frogs sit on a log; how many frogs are there in all? Solve the subtraction story.

Answer

Story B; 8 frogs

In Story A, frogs join, so we add (14 + 6 = 20). In Story C, two parts are put together, so we add (14 + 6 = 20). In Story B, frogs leave, so we subtract: 14 − 6. Subtract to ten: 14 − 4 = 10, then 10 − 2 = 8. There are 8 frogs left on the log.

Solving mixed word problems

Mixed practice means the stories come in any order, so each one needs its own thinking. Use four steps: Model the story with a bar or a part-part-whole drawing, Choose the operation and say why, Solve, and Check.

The image shows both kinds. A class reads 16 books on Monday and 9 on Tuesday: put the groups together, 16 + 9 = 25, and check that 25 is greater than either part. A basket has 32 apples and 7 are used: find what remains, 32 − 7 = 25, and check with addition, 25 + 7 = 32. Always answer with a sentence and a label.

Practice: try these five questions

Q36

A library cart has 34 books. A helper puts 8 books on the shelves. How many books are still on the cart? Model, choose, solve, and check.

Answer

26 books

Model: whole 34, part used 8, part left ?. Choose subtraction because books leave the cart. Solve: 34 − 4 = 30, 30 − 4 = 26. Check: 26 + 8 = 34. There are 26 books still on the cart.

Q37

A class planted 17 flowers on Monday and 8 flowers on Tuesday. How many flowers did they plant in all? Model, choose, solve, and check.

Answer

25 flowers

Model: two parts, 17 and 8, make the whole. Choose addition because we join two groups. Solve: 17 + 3 = 20, 20 + 5 = 25. Check: 25 is greater than both parts, and 25 − 8 = 17. They planted 25 flowers.

Q38

Ruby has 31 beads. Kim has 24 beads. How many fewer beads does Kim have than Ruby?

Answer

7 fewer beads

Model: two bars, 31 and 24, and the difference between them. Choose subtraction to compare: 31 − 24. Count up: 24 + 6 = 30, 30 + 1 = 31, and 6 + 1 = 7. Check: 24 + 7 = 31. Kim has 7 fewer beads than Ruby.

Q39

A jar had some cookies. After 9 cookies were eaten, 15 cookies were left. How many cookies were in the jar at the start?

Answer

24 cookies

Model: the start is the whole; its parts are 9 eaten and 15 left. Even though cookies were eaten, we need the whole, so we add the parts: 15 + 9. Make a ten: 15 + 5 = 20, 20 + 4 = 24. Check: 24 − 9 = 15. There were 24 cookies at the start.

Q40

Dev has 19 stamps. He buys 6 more stamps, then gives 5 stamps to his sister. How many stamps does Dev have now?

Answer

20 stamps

Step 1: buying makes the group grow, so add: 19 + 6 = 25. Step 2: giving away makes it smaller, so subtract: 25 − 5 = 20. Check: 20 + 5 = 25 and 25 − 6 = 19. Dev has 20 stamps now.

Quick questions and answers

How does a child know whether to add or subtract in a word problem?

Read the whole story, picture it, and ask what happens. If two groups are put together or a group gets bigger, add. If some of a group is taken away, two groups are compared, or a missing part must be found, subtract. Then explain the choice with “I choose ___ because ___.”

Are key words like “more” and “left” reliable?

They are helpful clues but not rules. “He gets 7 more” means add, while “How many more does Mia have?” means compare and subtract. Children should explain what the numbers mean instead of reacting to one word.

What is a part-part-whole model?

It is a simple drawing with the whole on top and two parts below. If both parts are known, add to find the whole. If the whole and one part are known, subtract to find the missing part: whole − known part = missing part.

How can my child check a word problem answer?

First check that the answer makes sense: a growing group should get bigger, and a shrinking group should get smaller. Then check subtraction with addition, for example 32 − 7 = 25 because 25 + 7 = 32.

What do “make a ten” and “subtract to ten” mean?

They are mental math strategies that use 10 or 20 as a stepping stone. For 16 + 7, add 4 to reach 20, then add 3 more to get 23. For 23 − 8, take away 3 to reach 20, then take away 5 more to get 15.

Does a student need to attend an earlier class first?

No. This is a self-contained Little Newtons lesson in a rolling rotation. It reviews the addition and subtraction strategies used here; class numbers identify topics, not attendance prerequisites.
