1. The data: what we actually know about kids and AI for math

The Education Week June 2026 survey is the most detailed look yet at how K–12 students interact with AI for homework. The headline — 55% of math-struggling students using AI weekly — matters less than what sits underneath it. These students are using AI as a first resort, not a fallback: they reach for ChatGPT or Photomath before attempting the problem, before asking a classmate, before texting a parent.

The 46% figure for confident math students matters too. Even kids doing fine are using AI regularly. The difference is how: confident students use it to check reasoning or explore alternate approaches; struggling students use it to get through an assignment they do not understand. Both produce completed homework. The outcome gap is enormous and almost invisible from the outside.

Education Week’s companion piece on teachers and AI found that most K–12 teachers report seeing students who cannot explain their own work when asked in class. This is happening in classrooms at PS 87, West End Secondary School, MS 54 (Booker T. Washington), and private schools like Calhoun and Trevor Day right now.

2. What AI tools your child is most likely using right now

Parents often talk about “AI” as if it is a single thing. It is not. The tools range from educational platforms with guardrails to general-purpose chatbots that will solve an entire problem set without a second thought.

ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini (general-purpose chatbots)

These have no built-in educational guardrails. Ask ChatGPT to solve a system of equations and it produces a complete step-by-step solution with no friction — no question about whether you tried first. For students genuinely trying to understand, these tools can be valuable. For students who want to finish homework without thinking, they are the fastest shortcut available.

Photomath, Microsoft Math Solver, Symbolab (image-based math solvers)

Photomath is particularly common among middle schoolers. Point your phone camera at a problem, get an immediate solution with steps. These tools were designed as checking tools — you do the work, then verify. In practice, that workflow has been reversed: photograph the problem first, then “check” the AI-generated answer. The distinction between checking and copying disappears quickly.

Khanmigo, Khan Academy’s AI tutor (educational guardrails)

Khanmigo is the most educationally sound option for K–12 math. Designed to avoid giving answers directly, it asks guiding questions and prompts students to explain their reasoning rather than simply solving on demand. This is the right design for learning. The limitation is that Khanmigo requires more effort than Photomath or ChatGPT — exactly why it is less popular among students who want fast answers.

Tools NYC schools quietly allow vs. block

Policies are inconsistent and largely unenforced for homework. After initially blocking ChatGPT in 2023, NYC DOE now encourages “thoughtful use” of AI in learning. Selective schools like Stuyvesant, Bronx Science, Collegiate, and Brearley have more explicit policies for assessed work — but those policies cannot govern what a student does at home on a Tuesday night. Your child’s school policy tells you very little about what actually happens during homework time.

3. What AI is actually good at for math learning

AI tools have genuine value for math learners when used thoughtfully. Dismissing them entirely would be wrong, and parents who try to ban them outright often drive the use underground. Here is where the value is real.

Explaining a concept three different ways until one clicks. Ask ChatGPT to explain why dividing by a fraction means multiplying by its reciprocal, and it will give you a visual explanation, an algebraic proof, and a real-world analogy — and another angle if you ask again. For a student who missed the teacher’s explanation in a class of 28, this flexibility is genuinely valuable.

Generating practice problems at the right level. “Give me ten two-step equation problems, slightly harder than my last quiz” is a prompt that works. AI generates custom sets on demand, producing problems that do not appear in the textbook your child already has the answer key for.

Catching algebra mistakes in worked solutions. “Here is my solution — is my reasoning correct at each step?” turns AI into a checker rather than a replacement for thinking. Completing a problem first, then asking AI to audit it, is using it correctly.

Vocabulary and word-problem translation. AI is excellent at rephrasing problems in simpler language, identifying the operation embedded in a verbal description, and explaining notation. This is a legitimate support that does not bypass the mathematical thinking itself.

4. Where AI ruins math learning

These are not hypothetical risks. I see them in students who come to SOMATH after a year or more of heavy AI use. The patterns are consistent.

Skipping the productive struggle that builds memory

Learning is stronger when retrieval is effortful. When a student works through a problem they do not immediately know how to solve — trying an approach, getting stuck, rerouting — the neural pathway for that procedure is built durably. AI eliminates this process. The student who photographs a problem and reads a solution witnesses the math rather than learning it. The consequence shows up on tests: a vague memory of having “done this type before” but no ability to reconstruct the steps under pressure.

Wrong answers presented with total confidence (calculation errors, misread problems)

A student recently brought in a homework problem: “Simplify: (3x² − 12) ÷ (x − 2).” ChatGPT had factored the numerator as (3x + 6)(x − 2) and cancelled to arrive at 3x + 6. The factoring was wrong: 3x² − 12 factors as 3(x² − 4) = 3(x + 2)(x − 2), giving 3(x + 2). The final answer happened to match numerically — but in a slightly different problem, that error in the middle step would have propagated to a wrong answer. ChatGPT displayed the incorrect factoring with the same formatting and tone of authority as a correct solution. The student had no way to know. This happens regularly with multi-step algebra, fraction arithmetic, and any problem involving signs and exponents. These tools make mistakes confidently.

No transfer to closed-book tests (SHSAT, ISEE, SAT, AP)

Every high-stakes test NYC students face is closed-book and AI-free. The SHSAT is taken on paper in a 3-hour sitting without any tools. The ISEE, required by Brearley, Spence, Dalton, and Trinity, is similarly paper-based. The College Board AP exam policies explicitly prohibit AI in the testing room. A student who has used AI as a shortcut for two years is preparing for a swimming test by watching swimming videos. Our SHSAT math 12-month roadmap and ISEE prep guide are both built on the premise that every problem must be worked independently from day one.

The “I followed the steps but can’t reproduce them” trap

This is the version parents often miss because it looks like understanding. The student can narrate the solution: “First you factor, then you cancel, then you simplify.” What they cannot do is start from scratch on a novel problem of the same type. They built recognition, not the generative skill. This gap is invisible on homework and wide open on tests — it is the most common thing SOMATH tutors surface in the diagnostic.

5. The signal your child has crossed the line from tool to crutch

  • Homework is done faster than it should be. A 20-problem algebra assignment that takes 10 minutes is a red flag. Genuine mathematical thinking takes time.
  • They cannot explain how they solved a problem. Ask your child to walk you through one homework problem they just completed. If they cannot explain the steps in their own words, they did not do the thinking.
  • Quiz and test scores are significantly lower than homework scores. If homework is consistently 90%+ but tests are 60–70%, the homework is not representative of what they can actually do.
  • They become frustrated or resistant when asked to work without a device. A student who has internalized a process is not threatened by working on paper. One who has outsourced the process is.
  • Their solution formatting does not match what they were taught in class. AI solutions often use different notation or vocabulary than a given teacher’s approach. Homework that looks unusually polished or stylistically foreign is worth a second look.
  • They reach for a device before attempting a problem. Watch the sequence when your child sits down to do math. Do they try the problem first, or open an app first?
A practical test: Ask your child to redo one problem from last night’s completed homework — on paper, in front of you. Not to be punitive: just to see what they can do independently. If they can reproduce their work with reasonable fluency, they probably understood it. If they are essentially starting from scratch, the homework was not really theirs.

6. A SOMATH-tested family AI policy for math

Generic advice is useless here. “Be thoughtful about AI use” means nothing to a 7th grader with a Photomath subscription. Here is what actually works, broken out by grade band.

For grades K–5

No AI use during math homework. If your child is stuck, they should ask you. If there is a concept they consistently do not understand, that is a signal to talk to the teacher or explore whether enrichment or tutoring makes sense — not a reason to let AI fill the gap. Our guide on when to start math enrichment covers what to look for at each elementary grade.

For middle school (grades 6–8)

Attempt first, AI second — and only for explanation, never for solution. The rule: try every problem yourself first. If genuinely stuck, use AI to explain the concept, then close it and do the problem yourself. For SHSAT and ISEE prep, this is non-negotiable: every problem must be worked independently. Our SHSAT math 12-month roadmap and ISEE prep guide for NYC private schools are both built on that premise.

For high school (grades 9–12)

AI as concept explainer and practice generator: fine. AI as homework solution machine: not fine. Complete your work first, then use AI to audit it: “Here is my solution — where did I go wrong?” is far better than “solve this.” For SAT prep context, see how SAT math differs from school math and our Digital SAT guide. For AP context, the post-AP-scores guide makes clear what AI-dependent gaps look like when results arrive.

For test prep specifically (SHSAT, ISEE, SAT, AP — closed-book skills must be built without AI)

No AI during prep work. Every test here is closed-book under timed, proctored conditions. Doing SHSAT prep with Photomath is like training for a marathon by riding a bus. The College Board AP exam policies are unambiguous: no AI in the testing room, and the exam assesses exactly what AI use prevents students from building.

7. How NYC teachers and tutors are actually adapting in 2026

The teaching profession is genuinely mid-adaptation, and the range of approaches across NYC schools is wide.

In-class no-tech work sessions, oral assessments, observed problem-solving. The most common adaptation we hear about from UWS families is a shift toward more in-class assessment. Teachers at schools like PS 84, West End Secondary, and Columbia Prep have increased the share of work done under observation. Oral assessments — asking a student to explain their solution process at the board — distinguish genuine understanding from AI-assisted output in a way take-home work cannot. Some teachers assign the same problem set twice: once at home and once in class a few days later, to see whether performance holds.

The shift in how SOMATH teaches: more whiteboards, fewer worksheets. Our sessions at 226 W 79th Street are structured around the whiteboard, not the worksheet. Students think out loud and explain their reasoning before writing anything down. We have also shifted toward oral problem-posing: the tutor describes a problem verbally, the student sets it up from scratch — no printed version to photograph. A student who has outsourced their thinking cannot narrate it. Frequently asked questions

Should I let my 4th grader use ChatGPT for math?

Generally no, not unsupervised. K–5 homework builds the foundational number sense that everything else in math is built on — skills you only develop by doing problems yourself, making mistakes, and correcting them. A workable rule: try first, ask a parent if stuck, and use AI only to get a second explanation of a concept you already attempted — never as the first step.

Will using AI hurt my child’s SHSAT score?

Yes, if it becomes a crutch. The SHSAT is taken on paper without any tools in a 3-hour sitting. Students who have consistently offloaded thinking to AI struggle with retrieval under pressure: they know they “saw” how to do it, but cannot reconstruct the steps independently. SHSAT prep must be done entirely without AI from day one. Our SHSAT math 12-month roadmap covers how to build the skills that transfer to the test room.

Is Photomath cheating?

It depends on how it is used. Using Photomath to check a problem you already worked is fine. Using it to photograph a problem set and copy steps without thinking is both dishonest and counterproductive — your child is not learning the material. The learning damage matters more than the honor code question.

What math AI tool is best for a struggling middle-schooler?

Khanmigo is the most educationally sound option. Unlike Photomath or general chatbots, it asks guiding questions rather than giving answers directly — it scaffolds thinking instead of bypassing it. A parent-supervised session with ChatGPT or Gemini can also work for concept explanation, provided the goal is understanding rather than completing an assignment. The core principle: AI should explain, not do.

How do I tell if my child is using AI to skip the learning?

Watch for a disconnect between homework performance and what they can do without support: correct homework but inability to explain solutions, quiz scores far below homework scores, and resistance to working without a device. The practical test: ask them to redo one completed homework problem on paper, in front of you. What happens in those five minutes is the truth.

What does SOMATH do about AI use in tutoring sessions?

We run sessions without AI: no Photomath, no ChatGPT during a problem. We teach on whiteboards, ask students to reason aloud, and use productive struggle as the teaching moment rather than bypassing it. For older students, we do teach how AI can be used well as a study resource — practice generation, concept explanation, solution auditing — but that use is introduced deliberately, never as a shortcut to finished homework.

Can AI help with SAT or ACT math prep?

AI can generate practice problems and explain concepts. What it cannot do is build the timed, pressure-tested fluency the Digital SAT and ACT actually measure. Using AI for concept review is fine; using it as a substitute for timed, unassisted practice sets is counterproductive. See our guide to Digital SAT structure, dates, and score strategy for how the 2026 test is structured.

Are NYC public schools letting students use AI?

Policies vary by school, and official NYCPS guidance is still evolving. After initially blocking ChatGPT in 2023, NYC DOE now encourages “thoughtful use” of AI in learning. In practice, many schools quietly tolerate it for homework while prohibiting it on assessments. Ask your child’s specific school directly rather than assuming any citywide standard applies.
