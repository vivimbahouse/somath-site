Concept 1 of 8

What is money?

Money is what people use to trade for the things they need and want. Before money existed, people had to trade one thing for another — that is called barter. If you had eggs and needed shoes, you had to find someone who had shoes and wanted eggs. That was hard.

Money makes trading easier because everyone agrees on its value. In the United States, money comes in two forms children see every day:

  • Coins — penny (1¢), nickel (5¢), dime (10¢), quarter (25¢).
  • Bills — $1, $5, $10, $20 (and more).

Grown-ups earn money by working. Kids can also earn money — for example by doing chores, helping neighbors, or selling lemonade. Money that is earned is money that can be used to buy, save, or share. In this class we will learn how to decide what to do with the money we have.

Key idea: money is a tool that helps people trade fairly for things they need and want.

Practice — 5 questions

  1. Q1. Before money existed, how did people get things they did not have?
    Show solution
    They used barter — trading one thing directly for another (for example, eggs for shoes).
  2. Q2. Name two forms of money you see in the United States.
    Show solution
    Coins (penny, nickel, dime, quarter) and bills ($1, $5, $10, $20).
  3. Q3. How do most grown-ups get money?
    Show solution
    They earn it by working at a job.
  4. Q4. Name one way a child might earn a little money.
    Show solution
    Any reasonable answer, for example: doing chores at home, helping a neighbor, or selling lemonade.
  5. Q5. Complete the sentence: Money is a tool that helps people ______ fairly for things they need and want.
    Show solution
    Trade (or exchange / buy). Money makes trading easier because everyone agrees on its value.
Concept 2 of 8

Needs vs. wants

Every day we ask ourselves, “Do I really need this, or do I just want it?” Learning the difference is one of the most important money skills there is.

  • A need is something we must have to live and stay healthy.
  • A want is something that would be nice to have but we can live without.

Here are examples that most 1st and 2nd graders can recognize:

NeedsWants
Healthy food (bread, milk, fruit)Candy, soda, ice cream
Warm clothes and shoesA new toy or a fancy sticker pack
A safe home to sleep inA bigger TV
Medicine when we are sickA birthday balloon

Wants are not bad. Ice cream and toys make life fun. But when there is not enough money for both, needs come first.

Key idea: needs keep us alive and healthy. Wants are extra and fun.

Practice — 5 questions

  1. Q1. Is a warm winter coat a need or a want? Why?
    Show solution
    A need. A warm coat keeps our body healthy and safe when it is cold.
  2. Q2. Is a new toy race car a need or a want?
    Show solution
    A want. It is fun to have, but we can live without it.
  3. Q3. You have $5. You are hungry and haven’t eaten lunch. You see candy for $5 and a sandwich for $5. Which should you pick and why?
    Show solution
    The sandwich. Food that keeps you full is a need. The candy is a want.
  4. Q4. Sort these into needs and wants: medicine, ice cream, shoes, video game.
    Show solution
    Needs: medicine, shoes. Wants: ice cream, video game.
  5. Q5. True or false: wants are bad and we should never buy them.
    Show solution
    False. Wants are fine and fun — we just take care of our needs first.
Concept 3 of 8

Earn, spend, save, share

When money comes into our hands, we always do one of four things with it. Learning these four words gives us a plan for every dollar.

  1. Earn — we get money by working (chores, helping, a job).
  2. Spend — we use money now to buy something we need or want.
  3. Save — we keep money for later, so we can buy something bigger.
  4. Share — we give some money to help other people (a friend, a family member, a charity, or animals in a shelter).

Every dollar you get can be split into these four buckets. You do not have to put everything in one bucket. For example, if Grandma gives you $10 for your birthday, you might spend $4 on a book, save $5 for a bigger toy, and share $1 by putting it in a charity jar.

Key idea: Earn → then decide how much to Spend, Save, and Share.

Practice — 5 questions

  1. Q1. Which of the four money words means “get money by working”?
    Show solution
    Earn.
  2. Q2. Which word means “keep money for later”?
    Show solution
    Save.
  3. Q3. Which word means “give some money to help others”?
    Show solution
    Share.
  4. Q4. Grandma gives you $10. You spend $4 on a book, save $5 for a bigger toy, and share $1. How many dollars did you use in total?
    Show solution
    $4 + $5 + $1 = $10. Every dollar you got has a job.
  5. Q5. You do chores and earn $2. Which of the four words did you just do?
    Show solution
    Earn. You worked and got money for it.
Concept 4 of 8

What is a piggy bank?

A piggy bank is a safe place to keep the money you want to save. It can be a real pig-shaped bank, a glass jar, a small box, or even an envelope. What matters is that:

  • The money stays in one place instead of being lost.
  • You can see the money grow over time.
  • It is a little bit of work to open it, so you are less tempted to spend right away.

Real banks (like the ones grown-ups use) work the same way. When money goes into a bank, it is protected, it grows a little bit over time (that extra money is called interest), and you take it out only when you really want to use it.

Money you put in the piggy bank is called your savings. Money you take out of the piggy bank is called a withdrawal. Money you put in is called a deposit.

Key idea: a piggy bank holds your savings safely so they can grow over time.

Practice — 5 questions

  1. Q1. What is the word for money you put into the piggy bank?
    Show solution
    Deposit.
  2. Q2. What is the word for money you take out of the piggy bank?
    Show solution
    Withdrawal.
  3. Q3. What is the word for all the money that is in the piggy bank?
    Show solution
    Your savings.
  4. Q4. Name two things (besides a real pig-shaped bank) that could work as a piggy bank.
    Show solution
    Any two of: a glass jar, a small box, an envelope.
  5. Q5. Why is it a good thing that a piggy bank is a little bit of work to open?
    Show solution
    It makes you stop and think before spending — so you are less tempted to use the money too fast.
Concept 5 of 8

Saving over time — the goal

Saving means waiting. You do not spend money right away because you are working toward a bigger goal. A goal is the thing you are saving for.

To make a saving goal you need three things:

  1. The thing you want. Draw it or write it down.
  2. How much it costs. Ask a grown-up or check the price tag.
  3. How much you already have vs. how much more you need.

Example: Maria wants a Lego set that costs $20. She already has $8 in her piggy bank. She needs $20 − $8 = $12 more. If she saves $2 each week from her allowance, in 6 weeks she will have enough (because 6 × $2 = $12).

Saving takes patience, but the feeling of buying something with your own saved money is much better than buying something on a whim.

Key idea: a saving goal = what you want + how much it costs − what you already have.

Practice — 5 questions

  1. Q1. You want a book that costs $10 and you already have $4. How much more do you need to save?
    Show solution
    $10 − $4 = $6.
  2. Q2. You want a toy that costs $15 and you already have $6. How much more do you need to save?
    Show solution
    $15 − $6 = $9.
  3. Q3. Maria needs $12 more and she saves $2 every week. How many weeks until she has enough?
    Show solution
    $12 ÷ $2 = 6 weeks.
  4. Q4. Leo saves $1 every week. How many weeks will it take him to save $10?
    Show solution
    $10 ÷ $1 = 10 weeks.
  5. Q5. Why does saving take patience?
    Show solution
    Because you have to wait and not spend right away, even when the money is in your piggy bank.
Concept 6 of 8

Counting your savings — coins and dollars

To know how much is in your piggy bank you have to count your money. It is easiest when you sort first.

Coin values to remember:

CoinValue
Penny1¢
Nickel5¢
Dime10¢
Quarter25¢

How to count:

  1. Sort the coins into piles (all quarters together, all dimes together, and so on).
  2. Count each pile using skip counting: quarters by 25, dimes by 10, nickels by 5, pennies by 1.
  3. Add the piles together to get the total.
  4. Every 100¢ = $1. So 4 quarters = $1, 10 dimes = $1, and 20 nickels = $1.

Example: 3 quarters + 2 dimes + 1 nickel = 75¢ + 20¢ + 5¢ = $1.00.

Key idea: sort first, skip-count each pile, then add. 100¢ = $1.

Practice — 5 questions

  1. Q1. What is the value of a quarter?
    Show solution
    25¢.
  2. Q2. What is the value of a dime?
    Show solution
    10¢.
  3. Q3. How many pennies make $1?
    Show solution
    100 pennies (because 100¢ = $1).
  4. Q4. How much money is 2 quarters + 3 dimes?
    Show solution
    2 × 25¢ + 3 × 10¢ = 50¢ + 30¢ = 80¢.
  5. Q5. How much money is 4 quarters + 1 nickel?
    Show solution
    4 × 25¢ + 5¢ = $1.00 + 5¢ = $1.05.
Concept 7 of 8

The 3-jar system — Save · Spend · Share

The 3-jar system is a simple way for kids to manage every dollar they earn. You use three jars (or three sections of a piggy bank) labeled:

  • SAVE — money for a bigger goal in the future.
  • SPEND — money you can use now on small things you want.
  • SHARE — money to help someone else or give to a cause you care about.

Every time you get money, you decide how to split it. Many families use an easy rule that is simple to remember:

The half-and-half rule: half in SAVE, one quarter in SPEND, one quarter in SHARE. So $4 becomes: $2 save, $1 spend, $1 share.

You can pick any split that works for you. The important part is that every dollar has a job before you get to the store.

Example: Grandma gives you $8 for helping her.

  • Half in SAVE = $4
  • One quarter in SPEND = $2
  • One quarter in SHARE = $2

Total: $4 + $2 + $2 = $8. Every dollar has a job.

Practice — 5 questions

  1. Q1. What are the three jars in the 3-jar system?
    Show solution
    SAVE, SPEND, and SHARE.
  2. Q2. Using the half-and-half rule, split $4 into the three jars.
    Show solution
    Half in SAVE = $2. One quarter in SPEND = $1. One quarter in SHARE = $1. Total $4.
  3. Q3. Using the half-and-half rule, split $8 into the three jars.
    Show solution
    SAVE = $4, SPEND = $2, SHARE = $2. Total $8.
  4. Q4. If your SAVE jar holds $6 and your SPEND jar holds $3, how much money do you have in those two jars together?
    Show solution
    $6 + $3 = $9.
  5. Q5. Which jar is for money you use on small things right now?
    Show solution
    SPEND.
Concept 8 of 8

Everyday money decisions

Every day you will have small chances to make good money decisions. Here are four habits that grow strong money skills:

  1. Wait a day before buying. If you still want it tomorrow, it is probably a good purchase. If you forget about it, it was probably just a moment’s want.
  2. Compare prices. The same toy can cost different amounts at different stores. Ask a grown-up to help you check.
  3. Check the price and count your money before the register. Never assume you have enough.
  4. Say thank you when you buy or receive. Gratitude is part of being good with money and good with people.

Real-life example: You are at the store and see a $5 sticker book. You have $6 in your SPEND jar. You could buy it. But you also remember that in 2 weeks you want to buy your friend a birthday card that costs $4. If you buy the stickers now, you will not have enough for the card. A grown-up decision is to skip the stickers and keep the money for the card.

Key idea: good money decisions come from thinking ahead, waiting, and knowing what your money is for.

Practice — 5 questions

  1. Q1. Why is it a good idea to wait a day before buying something you want?
    Show solution
    If you still want it tomorrow, it’s probably a good purchase. If you forget about it, it was just a moment’s want.
  2. Q2. You see a toy for $9 at Store A and the same toy for $7 at Store B. Which is the better price? By how much?
    Show solution
    Store B is better by $9 − $7 = $2.
  3. Q3. You have $6 in your SPEND jar. You want a $5 sticker book, but in 2 weeks you also want a $4 birthday card for a friend. Can you buy both? Why or why not?
    Show solution
    No. $5 + $4 = $9, and you only have $6. If you buy the stickers, you won’t have enough for the card. A grown-up decision is to skip the stickers and save the money for the card.
  4. Q4. You buy a $3 juice with a $5 bill. How much change should you get back?
    Show solution
    $5 − $3 = $2 in change.
  5. Q5. Name one habit from this lesson that helps you be smart with money.
    Show solution
    Any of: wait a day before buying, compare prices, count your money before the register, or say thank you.
FAQ

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a need and a want?
A need is something we must have to live and stay healthy — food, water, a warm coat, a safe home, medicine. A want is something that would be nice to have but we can live without — candy, a new toy, video games. When money is limited, needs come first. Wants are fine when needs are already covered.

Why does the syllabus say “piggy bank” instead of just teaching coins?
Coin identification and coin arithmetic (making change, combining coins and bills) are covered separately in Class 30 — Money combinations. Class 11 is the decision-making foundation: needs vs. wants, saving over time, and the 3-jar system. Research from the Council for Economic Education and Jump$tart Coalition places these decision-making concepts before coin identification in K–2 personal-finance standards.
What is the 3-jar system?
The 3-jar system is a simple way for kids to give every dollar a job. Three jars (or three sections of a piggy bank) are labeled SAVE (money for a bigger future goal), SPEND (money for small things now), and SHARE (money to help others). A common starter rule is half in Save, one quarter in Spend, one quarter in Share, so $4 splits into $2 / $1 / $1.
How do I use these practice questions with my child?
Read the theory panel out loud with your child, then work through the 5 questions under each section together. Ask your child to guess before clicking “Show solution”. The last question in most sections is a real-world decision — use it as a conversation starter about your own family’s money choices.
Does SOMATH teach financial literacy in every grade?
Yes. In Little Newtons (grades 1–2), students meet needs vs. wants, saving, and coin/bill arithmetic (Class 11 and Class 30). Older SOMATH courses introduce percents, tax and tips, simple interest, and eventually compound interest and investing concepts. Financial literacy is a lifelong thread in the SOMATH curriculum, not a one-time unit.
