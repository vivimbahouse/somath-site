Grade 3 Regents Math: Reading a Water Container in Liters

A Grade 3 Regents-style measurement question marks a container from 2 L to 12 L. The water reaches the 8 L line. Read the scale, estimate to the nearest liter, and you have the answer — but the technique matters more than this one problem. Here's how to teach the skill that earns easy points on every NY state math exam.

School of Math May 12, 2026 6 min read

The short answer: the correct answer is C — 8 liters. The water level on the container reaches right at the 8 L mark, and rounded to the nearest liter, the total is 8 liters. The reason this question matters is not that one Grade 3 student needs to know this single fact — it is that reading a marked scale is one of the most reliably tested skills on the NY State Math Test, and students who lose points here are almost always losing them for the same two reasons. We'll show you both.

The question, restated cleanly

A container is marked with horizontal lines at 2 L, 4 L, 6 L, 8 L, 10 L, and 12 L. The container is partially filled with water. The water reaches just at the 8 L line — the line is the top of the shaded water region. The question asks: "What is the total amount of water, to the nearest liter, in the container?" The answer choices are A) 4, B) 6, C) 8, D) 12.

If your child has practiced reading a scale, this is a five-second question. If they have not, it is a coin flip between the four labeled values. That gap — between five seconds and a guess — is the entire point of this article.

How to read the answer in three steps

  1. Identify the unit. Every tick on the container is labeled in liters. Not milliliters, not "marks on a ruler," not "cups." The question asks for the answer in liters, and the scale is already in liters. Match what is asked to what is shown.
  2. Find the water line. The top of the shaded region is where the water ends. On this container, the top of the water sits exactly on the line labeled 8 L.
  3. Round to the nearest unit. The question says "to the nearest liter." The water is at 8 L exactly, so 8 is already the nearest whole liter. Answer: 8.

That is the entire procedure. It is simple — and it is exactly the procedure that students do not follow when they get this question wrong.

The two mistakes that lose this question

Mistake 1: reading the closest label, not the closest tick

The tick marks on the container are at 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 — every 2 liters, not every 1 liter. Students who are not paying attention may notice that 8 has a number next to it and pick 8 by default, which happens to be right here. But on the next test they see, the water might sit between 6 L and 8 L — say, at 7 L — and the answer might be 7 even though there is no tick labeled 7. Students who memorized "find the closest labeled number" will be wrong.

The fix is to teach the child to read the position of the water line first, in their own words ("a little above six" or "right at the eight line"), and only then check it against the answer choices. Reading the visual before looking at the answer options is the single biggest score booster for any question with a chart, graph, or scale.

Mistake 2: confusing the unit

Liters and milliliters live next to each other in the Grade 3 curriculum, and students mix them up. A child who is anxious sees a container, panics, and writes 8000 or 800. The Regents and the NY State Math Test will sometimes deliberately put a milliliters question right before or after a liters question to catch this. The fix: before reading the value, the child should say out loud (or whisper) the unit on the scale. "This is in liters." That one-second habit eliminates the mistake.

Why measurement questions matter on state tests

Visual measurement and estimation questions are not rare — they are a regular feature of:

  • NY State Math Test (Grades 3–8): volume, mass, length, and time scales each appear, usually with at least one question per grade level.
  • Regents-aligned elementary practice tests: measurement is one of the four major domains assessed in the elementary grades.
  • SHSAT elementary preparation: the SHSAT itself is a middle-school exam, but the foundational skills tested in Grade 3 measurement — careful reading, estimation, interpreting visuals — show up again on the SHSAT in disguised form.
  • Common Core 3.MD standards: measurement and data is one of five Grade 3 content domains.
  • Classroom benchmark exams used by most NYC public and independent schools to track progress through the year.

The reason teachers and tutors emphasize this skill is not the question itself. It is that careful scale-reading transfers cleanly into more advanced topics: reading a thermometer in a science class, reading a number line in a fractions unit, reading the y-axis of a coordinate plane in middle school algebra, and reading a confidence interval on a statistics graph in high school. The student who learns this skill cleanly in Grade 3 is set up to extract information from visual data for the rest of their academic career.

What "to the nearest liter" actually means

The phrase "to the nearest liter" is worth slowing down on, because Grade 3 is the moment many students meet rounding for the first time on a state exam. "Nearest" means the closest whole-number value — not the lowest, not the highest, not the next labeled tick. If the water sat halfway between the 6 L and 8 L lines, the nearest liter would be 7. If it sat just above the 8 L line, the nearest liter would still be 8, because the water is closer to 8 than to 9. The water in this question is sitting exactly on 8 L, which is the cleanest possible case — and that is on purpose. Grade 3 questions are written this way deliberately, so the student does not also have to estimate. They just have to read.

One way we teach this at SOMATH: ask the student, "Is the water above the line or below the line?" If above, round up to the next labeled value. If below, round down. If on, you have your answer. Three short questions, every time. The procedure is simple enough that a Grade 3 student can verbalize it during the test, which is exactly what we want.

How to build the skill at home

Worksheets help, but the fastest gains come from physical measurement. Three things to try this week:

  1. Measuring cup in the kitchen. Pour water to a target level — "fill this to 250 milliliters" — and let your child read the scale to confirm. Then ask them to estimate to the nearest 50 mL before checking.
  2. Ruler and a real object. Hand your child a ruler and a paperback book. Have them measure the long side to the nearest inch, then to the nearest half-inch, then to the nearest centimeter. Reading the same object at three different precisions builds the habit of matching the answer to the units asked.
  3. Thermometer on a window. A simple indoor/outdoor thermometer is one of the best measurement tools a kid can have. Reading the temperature every morning for a week, and rounding to the nearest five degrees, builds the same scale-reading muscle that solves the water-container question.

These are not test-prep activities. They are just careful, repeated practice with real instruments. The Regents skill is a byproduct.
