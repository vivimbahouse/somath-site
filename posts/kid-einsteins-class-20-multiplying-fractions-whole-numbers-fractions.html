Multiplying Fractions by Whole Numbers and by Fractions

Multiplying a fraction counts equal groups or takes part of an amount. Multiplication as repeated addition

A note on the example in the image: the sentence says each ribbon is “1/3 meter,” but the ribbons and the number sentence show 1/5 meter. Read the example as 1/5 meter per ribbon: 4 × \(\frac{1}{5}\) = \(\frac{1}{5}\) + \(\frac{1}{5}\) + \(\frac{1}{5}\) + \(\frac{1}{5}\) = \(\frac{4}{5}\) meter.

Multiplication means equal groups. Just as 3 × 2 means three groups of 2, the product 3 × \(\frac{1}{4}\) means three groups of one-fourth: \(\frac{1}{4}\) + \(\frac{1}{4}\) + \(\frac{1}{4}\). Count the fourths: one fourth, two fourths, three fourths. So 3 × \(\frac{1}{4}\) = \(\frac{3}{4}\).

The denominator names the size of each piece, and it does not change when we add equal pieces. Only the number of pieces grows. The whole number tells how many groups we have, so we multiply it by the numerator: 3 groups of 1 fourth is 3 fourths.

Multiplying is a fast way to add the same fraction again and again. Instead of writing \(\frac{1}{6}\) six times, write 6 × \(\frac{1}{6}\) = \(\frac{6}{6}\) = 1 whole.

Q1

Write 5 × \(\dfrac{1}{6}\) as a repeated addition. Then find the product.

Answer

\(\dfrac{5}{6}\)

  1. 5 × \(\frac{1}{6}\) means 5 groups of one-sixth.
  2. Repeated addition: \(\frac{1}{6}\) + \(\frac{1}{6}\) + \(\frac{1}{6}\) + \(\frac{1}{6}\) + \(\frac{1}{6}\).
  3. Count the sixths: 5 sixths, so the product is \(\frac{5}{6}\).

Q2

Write \(\dfrac{2}{9}\) + \(\dfrac{2}{9}\) + \(\dfrac{2}{9}\) + \(\dfrac{2}{9}\) as a multiplication. Then find the total.

Answer

4 × \(\dfrac{2}{9}\) = \(\dfrac{8}{9}\)

  1. The same fraction, \(\frac{2}{9}\), is added 4 times, so the multiplication is 4 × \(\frac{2}{9}\).
  2. Each group has 2 ninths. Four groups have 4 × 2 = 8 ninths.
  3. The total is \(\frac{8}{9}\).

Q3

A small glass holds \(\dfrac{1}{8}\) liter of juice. How much juice is in 7 glasses?

Answer

\(\dfrac{7}{8}\) liter

  1. There are 7 equal groups of \(\frac{1}{8}\) liter, so multiply: 7 × \(\frac{1}{8}\).
  2. 7 groups of 1 eighth is 7 eighths: 7 × \(\frac{1}{8}\) = \(\frac{7}{8}\).
  3. The 7 glasses hold \(\frac{7}{8}\) liter of juice.

Q4

Is 6 × \(\dfrac{1}{6}\) more than, less than, or equal to 1 whole? Explain using equal groups.

Answer

Equal to 1 whole

  1. 6 × \(\frac{1}{6}\) means 6 groups of one-sixth: \(\frac{1}{6}\) + \(\frac{1}{6}\) + \(\frac{1}{6}\) + \(\frac{1}{6}\) + \(\frac{1}{6}\) + \(\frac{1}{6}\).
  2. That is 6 sixths, or \(\frac{6}{6}\).
  3. A whole cut into 6 equal parts has exactly 6 sixths, so \(\frac{6}{6}\) = 1. The product is equal to 1 whole.

Q5

One lap around a small track is \(\dfrac{1}{10}\) mile. Mia runs 9 laps. Write a repeated addition and a multiplication, then find how far she runs.

Answer

\(\dfrac{9}{10}\) mile

  1. Repeated addition: \(\frac{1}{10}\) added 9 times.
  2. Multiplication: 9 × \(\frac{1}{10}\).
  3. 9 groups of 1 tenth is 9 tenths, so Mia runs \(\frac{9}{10}\) mile.

Multiplying a fraction by a whole number

To multiply a whole number by a fraction, multiply the whole number by the numerator and keep the same denominator: 3 × \(\frac{2}{5}\) = \(\frac{3 \times 2}{5}\) = \(\frac{6}{5}\). Three groups of 2 fifths make 6 fifths. The pieces are still fifths, so the denominator stays 5.

When the numerator becomes larger than the denominator, the product is more than 1 whole. Five fifths make one whole, so \(\frac{6}{5}\) is 1 whole and 1 fifth: \(\frac{6}{5}\) = \(1\frac{1}{5}\). To change an improper fraction to a mixed number, divide the numerator by the denominator: 6 ÷ 5 = 1 remainder 1.

Always check whether the answer can be simplified. In 4 × \(\frac{3}{8}\) = \(\frac{12}{8}\), both 12 and 8 can be divided by 4, so \(\frac{12}{8}\) = \(\frac{3}{2}\) = \(1\frac{1}{2}\). A common mistake is to multiply the denominator too. Remember: the denominator tells the size of each piece, and the size does not change.

Q6

Find 4 × \(\dfrac{2}{9}\).

Answer

\(\dfrac{8}{9}\)

  1. Multiply the whole number by the numerator: 4 × 2 = 8.
  2. Keep the denominator 9: 4 × \(\frac{2}{9}\) = \(\frac{4 \times 2}{9}\) = \(\frac{8}{9}\).

Find 5 × \(\dfrac{3}{7}\). Write the answer as a mixed number.

Answer

\(2\dfrac{1}{7}\)

  1. Multiply the numerator: 5 × 3 = 15. Keep the denominator: \(\frac{15}{7}\).
  2. Divide 15 by 7: 15 ÷ 7 = 2 remainder 1.
  3. So \(\frac{15}{7}\) = \(2\frac{1}{7}\).

Q8

Find 6 × \(\dfrac{5}{8}\). Simplify and write the answer as a mixed number.

\(3\dfrac{3}{4}\)

  1. Multiply the numerator: 6 × 5 = 30. Keep the denominator: \(\frac{30}{8}\).
  2. Simplify by dividing the numerator and denominator by 2: \(\frac{30}{8}\) = \(\frac{15}{4}\).
  3. Divide: 15 ÷ 4 = 3 remainder 3, so \(\frac{15}{4}\) = \(3\frac{3}{4}\).

Q9

Find 3 × \(\dfrac{4}{5}\). Write the answer as a mixed number.

Answer

\(2\dfrac{2}{5}\)

  1. Multiply the numerator: 3 × 4 = 12. Keep the denominator: \(\frac{12}{5}\).
  2. Divide: 12 ÷ 5 = 2 remainder 2.
  3. So 3 × \(\frac{4}{5}\) = \(\frac{12}{5}\) = \(2\frac{2}{5}\).

Q10

Ben says 2 × \(\dfrac{3}{5}\) = \(\dfrac{6}{10}\). Explain his mistake and find the correct product.

Answer

\(\dfrac{6}{5}\) = \(1\dfrac{1}{5}\)

  1. Ben multiplied both the numerator and the denominator by 2.
  2. The denominator tells the size of the pieces. Two groups of 3 fifths are still fifths, so the denominator stays 5.
  3. Correct work: 2 × \(\frac{3}{5}\) = \(\frac{2 \times 3}{5}\) = \(\frac{6}{5}\) = \(1\frac{1}{5}\). (Ben's \(\frac{6}{10}\) equals \(\frac{3}{5}\), which is only one group.)

Using visual models

A picture helps us see why multiplying by the numerator works. Three models show the same product 3 × \(\frac{1}{4}\) = \(\frac{3}{4}\).

Fraction strips: draw one strip for each group. Each strip is cut into fourths with 1 part shaded. Slide the shaded parts together onto one strip: 3 shaded fourths, or \(\frac{3}{4}\). Shaded shapes: a circle cut into 4 equal slices with 3 slices shaded shows three one-fourth pieces. Number line: cut the space from 0 to 1 into 4 equal jumps. Start at 0 and make 3 jumps of \(\frac{1}{4}\): you land at \(\frac{3}{4}\).

On a number line, the whole number tells how many jumps to make and the fraction tells how long each jump is. If the jumps go past 1, the product is greater than 1 whole: 3 jumps of \(\frac{2}{5}\) land at \(\frac{6}{5}\) = \(1\frac{1}{5}\). Different models give the same product.

Q11

The model shows equal strips. Each strip is cut into sixths. Write the multiplication that the model shows, then find the product.

Answer

3 × \(\dfrac{2}{6}\) = \(\dfrac{6}{6}\) = 1

  1. There are 3 strips, so there are 3 groups.
  2. Each strip has 2 of 6 equal parts shaded, so each group is \(\frac{2}{6}\).
  3. Multiply: 3 × \(\frac{2}{6}\) = \(\frac{6}{6}\). Six sixths fill one whole strip, so the product is 1.

Q12

The number line from 0 to 1 is cut into eighths. Start at 0 and make 5 jumps of \(\dfrac{1}{8}\). Where do you land? Write the multiplication.

Answer

5 × \(\dfrac{1}{8}\) = \(\dfrac{5}{8}\)

  1. Each jump is \(\frac{1}{8}\), the length of one space on the number line.
  2. After 5 jumps: \(\frac{1}{8}\), \(\frac{2}{8}\), \(\frac{3}{8}\), \(\frac{4}{8}\), \(\frac{5}{8}\).
  3. You land at \(\frac{5}{8}\), so 5 × \(\frac{1}{8}\) = \(\frac{5}{8}\).

Q13

A circle is cut into 6 equal slices. You shade \(\dfrac{1}{6}\) of the circle five times, one slice at a time. What fraction of the circle is shaded, and what fraction is not shaded?

Answer

\(\dfrac{5}{6}\) shaded; \(\dfrac{1}{6}\) not shaded

  1. Shading \(\frac{1}{6}\) five times is 5 × \(\frac{1}{6}\) = \(\frac{5}{6}\).
  2. The whole circle is \(\frac{6}{6}\). Unshaded: \(\frac{6}{6}\) − \(\frac{5}{6}\) = \(\frac{1}{6}\).
  3. Check: \(\frac{5}{6}\) + \(\frac{1}{6}\) = \(\frac{6}{6}\) = 1 whole circle.

Q14

Use the number line from 0 to 2, cut into fifths, to show 3 × \(\dfrac{2}{5}\). How long is each jump, and where do you land?

Answer

Each jump is \(\dfrac{2}{5}\); you land at \(\dfrac{6}{5}\) = \(1\dfrac{1}{5}\)

  1. The fraction tells the jump length: each jump covers 2 fifths, or \(\frac{2}{5}\).
  2. Three jumps: \(\frac{2}{5}\), then \(\frac{4}{5}\), then \(\frac{6}{5}\).
  3. \(\frac{6}{5}\) is past 1: it is 1 whole and 1 more fifth, so 3 × \(\frac{2}{5}\) = \(\frac{6}{5}\) = \(1\frac{1}{5}\).

Q15

Draw strips to show 4 × \(\dfrac{1}{3}\). How many whole strips can you fill, and what is the product?

Answer

\(\dfrac{4}{3}\) = \(1\dfrac{1}{3}\): one full strip and one third of another

  1. Draw 4 strips cut into thirds, each with 1 part shaded: 4 × \(\frac{1}{3}\) = \(\frac{4}{3}\).
  2. Slide the shaded thirds together. Three thirds fill one whole strip.
  3. One shaded third is left over, so the product is \(\frac{4}{3}\) = \(1\frac{1}{3}\).

Multiplying fractions by fractions

To multiply two fractions, multiply across the top and across the bottom: \(\frac{a}{b}\) × \(\frac{c}{d}\) = \(\frac{a \times c}{b \times d}\). The denominators b and d must not be zero, because we can never cut a whole into zero parts.

Step 1: multiply the numerators. Step 2: multiply the denominators. Step 3: simplify. For example, \(\frac{2}{3}\) × \(\frac{3}{5}\): the numerators give 2 × 3 = 6, the denominators give 3 × 5 = 15, so the product is \(\frac{6}{15}\). Dividing 6 and 15 by their common factor 3 gives \(\frac{2}{5}\).

Unlike adding, multiplying fractions does not need a common denominator. Never add the numerators or the denominators when you multiply. The “Your turn” in the image works the same way: \(\frac{3}{4}\) × \(\frac{2}{5}\) = \(\frac{6}{20}\) = \(\frac{3}{10}\).

Q19

Find \(\dfrac{3}{5}\) × \(\dfrac{5}{8}\). Simplify your answer.

Answer

\(\dfrac{3}{8}\)

  1. Numerators: 3 × 5 = 15. Denominators: 5 × 8 = 40. Product: \(\frac{15}{40}\).

  3. \(\frac{15}{40}\) = \(\frac{3}{8}\).

Q20

Ana writes \(\dfrac{2}{5}\) × \(\dfrac{1}{3}\) = \(\dfrac{3}{8}\). What did she do wrong? Find the correct product.

Answer

\(\dfrac{2}{15}\)

  1. Ana added the numerators (2 + 1 = 3) and added the denominators (5 + 3 = 8). That is not the rule for multiplying.
  2. Multiply across: numerators 2 × 1 = 2, denominators 5 × 3 = 15.
  3. The correct product is \(\frac{2}{15}\). It makes sense that it is smaller than \(\frac{2}{5}\), because we are taking one third of \(\frac{2}{5}\).

Finding a fraction of a fraction

The word “of” means multiply. “Half of two-thirds” means \(\frac{1}{2}\) × \(\frac{2}{3}\).

An area model shows why. Step 1: draw a rectangle, cut it into 3 equal columns, and shade 2 columns to show \(\frac{2}{3}\). Step 2: cut the rectangle into 2 equal rows and take half of the shaded part, the top row of the two shaded columns. Step 3: compare with the whole. The rectangle now has 2 × 3 = 6 equal parts, and 2 of them are in the double-shaded part, so \(\frac{1}{2}\) of \(\frac{2}{3}\) = \(\frac{2}{6}\) = \(\frac{1}{3}\).

The area model also shows why we multiply the denominators: cutting thirds into 2 rows makes sixths. Multiplying the numerators counts the double-shaded parts. In a recipe that needs \(\frac{2}{3}\) cup of milk, half the recipe needs \(\frac{1}{2}\) × \(\frac{2}{3}\) = \(\frac{1}{3}\) cup.

Q21

Find \(\dfrac{1}{2}\) of \(\dfrac{3}{4}\).

Answer

\(\dfrac{3}{8}\)

  1. “Of” means multiply: \(\frac{1}{2}\) × \(\frac{3}{4}\).
  2. Numerators: 1 × 3 = 3. Denominators: 2 × 4 = 8.
  3. Half of three-fourths is \(\frac{3}{8}\).

Q22

Find \(\dfrac{1}{3}\) of \(\dfrac{3}{5}\). Simplify your answer.

Answer

\(\dfrac{1}{5}\)

  1. Write it as multiplication: \(\frac{1}{3}\) × \(\frac{3}{5}\).
  2. Numerators: 1 × 3 = 3. Denominators: 3 × 5 = 15. Product: \(\frac{3}{15}\).
  3. Divide by 3: \(\frac{3}{15}\) = \(\frac{1}{5}\). One third of 3 fifths is 1 fifth.

Q23

A rectangle is cut into 4 equal columns, and 3 columns are lightly shaded to show \(\dfrac{3}{4}\). It is also cut into 3 equal rows, and 2 rows of the shaded part are shaded dark. What fraction of the whole rectangle is dark? Write the multiplication.

Answer

\(\dfrac{2}{3}\) × \(\dfrac{3}{4}\) = \(\dfrac{6}{12}\) = \(\dfrac{1}{2}\)

  1. The dark part is \(\frac{2}{3}\) of the shaded \(\frac{3}{4}\), so multiply \(\frac{2}{3}\) × \(\frac{3}{4}\).
  2. The rectangle has 3 rows × 4 columns = 12 equal parts. The dark part has 2 rows × 3 columns = 6 parts, so it is \(\frac{6}{12}\).
  3. Simplify: \(\frac{6}{12}\) = \(\frac{1}{2}\). The dark part is half of the whole rectangle.

Q24

A garden is \(\dfrac{4}{5}\) planted. Carrots grow in \(\dfrac{1}{4}\) of the planted part. What fraction of the whole garden has carrots?

Answer

\(\dfrac{1}{5}\) of the garden

  1. “\(\frac{1}{4}\) of the planted part” means \(\frac{1}{4}\) × \(\frac{4}{5}\).
  2. Numerators: 1 × 4 = 4. Denominators: 4 × 5 = 20. Product: \(\frac{4}{20}\).
  3. Simplify by dividing by 4: \(\frac{4}{20}\) = \(\frac{1}{5}\) of the whole garden.

Q25

There is \(\dfrac{3}{4}\) of a tray of brownies left. Sam eats \(\dfrac{1}{3}\) of what is left. What fraction of the whole tray does Sam eat?

Answer

\(\dfrac{1}{4}\) of the tray

  1. Sam eats \(\frac{1}{3}\) of \(\frac{3}{4}\), so multiply \(\frac{1}{3}\) × \(\frac{3}{4}\).
  2. Numerators: 1 × 3 = 3. Denominators: 3 × 4 = 12. Product: \(\frac{3}{12}\).
  3. Simplify: \(\frac{3}{12}\) = \(\frac{1}{4}\). Sam eats one fourth of the whole tray.

Simplifying products two ways

There are two ways to reach a product in simplest form. Method 1: multiply, then simplify. \(\frac{2}{3}\) × \(\frac{3}{4}\) = \(\frac{6}{12}\), and dividing 6 and 12 by their common factor 6 gives \(\frac{1}{2}\).

Method 2: simplify before multiplying. Any numerator can be paired with any denominator, even across the × sign, and divided by a common factor. In \(\frac{2}{3}\) × \(\frac{3}{4}\), divide the numerator 3 and the denominator 3 by 3, and divide the numerator 2 and the denominator 4 by 2. The problem becomes \(\frac{1}{1}\) × \(\frac{1}{2}\) = \(\frac{1}{2}\): the same answer with smaller numbers.

With a whole number, first write it over 1. For 6 × \(\frac{5}{9}\), write \(\frac{6}{1}\) × \(\frac{5}{9}\), divide 6 and 9 by 3 to get \(\frac{2}{1}\) × \(\frac{5}{3}\) = \(\frac{10}{3}\) = \(3\frac{1}{3}\). Simplify only a numerator with a denominator, never two numerators or two denominators. The “Your turn” in the image: \(\frac{3}{8}\) × \(\frac{4}{9}\) becomes \(\frac{1}{2}\) × \(\frac{1}{3}\) = \(\frac{1}{6}\).

Q26

Find \(\dfrac{4}{9}\) × \(\dfrac{3}{8}\) two ways: multiply then simplify, and simplify before multiplying.

Answer

\(\dfrac{1}{6}\)

  1. Method 1: \(\frac{4 \times 3}{9 \times 8}\) = \(\frac{12}{72}\). The greatest common factor of 12 and 72 is 12, so \(\frac{12}{72}\) = \(\frac{1}{6}\).
  2. Method 2: divide the numerator 4 and the denominator 8 by 4 to get 1 and 2. Divide the numerator 3 and the denominator 9 by 3 to get 1 and 3.
  3. Now multiply: \(\frac{1}{3}\) × \(\frac{1}{2}\) = \(\frac{1}{6}\). Both methods give \(\frac{1}{6}\).

  2. Divide the numerator 6 and the denominator 12 by 6 to get 1 and 2.

Understanding the size of the product

Before multiplying, predict the size of the answer. Start with a positive number and look at the other factor.

If the factor is between 0 and 1 (a fraction smaller than 1, like \(\frac{1}{2}\)), the product is smaller than the starting number: 8 × \(\frac{1}{2}\) = 4, and 4 < 8. We are taking only part of 8. If the factor is exactly 1 (including fractions like \(\frac{5}{5}\)), the product stays the same: 8 × 1 = 8. If the factor is greater than 1 (like \(\frac{3}{2}\)), the product is larger: 8 × \(\frac{3}{2}\) = 12, and 12 > 8.

This is called scaling. It is a quick way to catch mistakes. For example, \(\frac{3}{4}\) × \(\frac{1}{2}\) must be less than \(\frac{3}{4}\), because taking half of something makes it smaller. Indeed, \(\frac{3}{4}\) × \(\frac{1}{2}\) = \(\frac{3}{8}\).

Q31

Without multiplying, decide whether 9 × \(\dfrac{2}{3}\) is greater than or less than 9. Then find the product to check.

Answer

Less than 9; the product is 6

  1. The factor \(\frac{2}{3}\) is between 0 and 1, so the product is smaller than 9.

Q32

Is 6 × \(\dfrac{5}{4}\) greater than or less than 6? Explain, then find the product as a mixed number.

Answer

Greater than 6; the product is \(7\dfrac{1}{2}\)

  1. \(\frac{5}{4}\) is greater than 1 because the numerator is larger than the denominator. Multiplying by a number greater than 1 makes the product larger.
  2. Multiply: 6 × \(\frac{5}{4}\) = \(\frac{30}{4}\) = \(\frac{15}{2}\).
  3. 15 ÷ 2 = 7 remainder 1, so \(\frac{15}{2}\) = \(7\frac{1}{2}\), and \(7\frac{1}{2}\) > 6.

Q33

Order from least to greatest without finding exact answers: 10 × \(\dfrac{7}{5}\), 10 × 1, 10 × \(\dfrac{3}{5}\). Then check by multiplying.

Answer

10 × \(\dfrac{3}{5}\), 10 × 1, 10 × \(\dfrac{7}{5}\) (6, 10, 14)

  1. \(\frac{3}{5}\) is less than 1, so 10 × \(\frac{3}{5}\) is less than 10. Multiplying by 1 gives exactly 10. \(\frac{7}{5}\) is greater than 1, so 10 × \(\frac{7}{5}\) is more than 10.
  2. Check: 10 × \(\frac{3}{5}\) = \(\frac{30}{5}\) = 6; 10 × 1 = 10; 10 × \(\frac{7}{5}\) = \(\frac{70}{5}\) = 14.
  3. Order: 6, 10, 14.

Q34

Is \(\dfrac{2}{3}\) × \(\dfrac{4}{5}\) greater than or less than \(\dfrac{4}{5}\)? Explain, then check.

Answer

Less than \(\dfrac{4}{5}\); the product is \(\dfrac{8}{15}\)

  1. We take \(\frac{2}{3}\) of \(\frac{4}{5}\). Since \(\frac{2}{3}\) is less than 1, the product is less than \(\frac{4}{5}\).
  2. Multiply: \(\frac{2}{3}\) × \(\frac{4}{5}\) = \(\frac{8}{15}\).
  3. Compare with fifteenths: \(\frac{4}{5}\) = \(\frac{12}{15}\), and \(\frac{8}{15}\) < \(\frac{12}{15}\).

Q35

Write <, >, or = in each blank: (a) 12 × \(\dfrac{5}{5}\) ___ 12 (b) 12 × \(\dfrac{4}{3}\) ___ 12

Answer

(a) = (b) >

  1. (a) \(\frac{5}{5}\) = 1, and multiplying by 1 keeps the number the same: 12 × \(\frac{5}{5}\) = 12, so the sign is =.
  2. (b) \(\frac{4}{3}\) is greater than 1, so the product is larger than 12: 12 × \(\frac{4}{3}\) = \(\frac{48}{3}\) = 16.
  3. 16 > 12, so the sign is >.

Solving real-world fraction problems

Solve every story problem in four steps: read, multiply, simplify, label.

Read: decide what kind of story it is. Equal groups (“each,” “every,” “how much in all”) use a whole number times a fraction: 4 ribbons of \(\frac{3}{4}\) meter each use 4 × \(\frac{3}{4}\) = \(\frac{12}{4}\) = 3 meters. Part of an amount (“half of,” “\(\frac{3}{5}\) of,” “for half the recipe”) uses fraction times fraction: half of \(\frac{2}{3}\) cup is \(\frac{1}{2}\) × \(\frac{2}{3}\) = \(\frac{1}{3}\) cup.

Multiply, simplify, and write the answer as a whole or mixed number when that is easier to understand. Label: always include the unit, such as meters, cups, or pounds. Finally, check the size: taking part of an amount gives less than the amount; several groups give more than one group.

Quick questions and answers

How do you multiply a fraction by a whole number?

Multiply the whole number by the numerator and keep the denominator. For example, 3 × 2/5 = 6/5 = 1 1/5. The denominator stays the same because the size of each piece does not change.

Why is multiplying a fraction by a whole number like repeated addition?

Multiplication counts equal groups. 4 × 1/5 means four groups of one-fifth, or 1/5 + 1/5 + 1/5 + 1/5 = 4/5.

How do you multiply two fractions?

Multiply the numerators and multiply the denominators, then simplify: 2/3 × 3/5 = 6/15 = 2/5. Unlike adding, multiplying does not need a common denominator.

What does “of” mean with fractions?

“Of” means multiply. Half of 2/3 is 1/2 × 2/3 = 2/6 = 1/3. An area model shows this as 2 of 6 equal parts of the whole.

What does simplifying before multiplying mean?

Divide any numerator and any denominator by a common factor before multiplying. In 3/8 × 4/9, divide 3 and 9 by 3 and divide 4 and 8 by 4 to get 1/2 × 1/3 = 1/6.

Does multiplying always make a number bigger?

No. Multiplying a positive number by a fraction between 0 and 1 makes it smaller, multiplying by 1 keeps it the same, and multiplying by a number greater than 1 makes it larger.
