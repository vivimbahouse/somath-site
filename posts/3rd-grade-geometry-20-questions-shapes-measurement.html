What 3rd graders learn in geometry & measurement

The Common Core Grade 3 standards break geometry and measurement into three big ideas:

1. 2D shapes and their properties. Third graders learn that shapes belong to categories based on their attributes — the number of sides, the number of vertices (corners), whether the sides are parallel or equal, and whether the angles are right, acute, or obtuse. This is where kids start to see that a square is a special rectangle, a rectangle is a special parallelogram, and a parallelogram is a special quadrilateral. Being able to say why a shape belongs to a category is a huge cognitive leap from earlier grades.

2. Area and perimeter. This is the flagship topic of 3rd grade geometry. Students learn to tile a rectangle with unit squares to find its area, connect that tiling to multiplication (length × width), and separately measure the boundary of a shape to find its perimeter. Keeping these two concepts distinct — area is "how much space fits inside," perimeter is "how far around the edge" — is a real challenge for 8- and 9-year-olds and one of the most common places we see confusion.

3. Measurement. Third graders measure lengths in inches, centimeters, and meters; they learn to pick the right unit for the right object (you'd measure a pencil in centimeters, not kilometers); and they start estimating — a hugely important habit because it teaches kids to check whether an answer makes sense in the real world.

Layered on top of all of this are foundational ideas that keep showing up later: right angles (the corners of a square), lines of symmetry (fold the shape and both halves match), and partitioning shapes into equal parts — which is really the geometric side of fractions.

20 practice questions (medium level)

Medium · 20 Questions

Question 1

Which shape has exactly 4 sides that are all the same length and 4 right angles?

  1. Rectangle
  2. Square
  3. Rhombus
  4. Trapezoid

Answer: B — Square

A rectangle has 4 right angles but its sides are not all equal. A rhombus has 4 equal sides but no right angles (unless it's a square). Only the square has both: 4 equal sides and 4 right angles. Every square is a special rectangle and a special rhombus.

Question 2

A rectangle is 6 cm long and 4 cm wide. What is its perimeter?

  1. 10 cm
  2. 20 cm
  3. 24 cm
  4. 28 cm

Answer: B — 20 cm

Perimeter = the distance all the way around: 6 + 4 + 6 + 4 = 20 cm. Shortcut: 2 × (length + width) = 2 × (6 + 4) = 20 cm. Notice 24 cm is the area (6 × 4) — a very common mix-up.

Question 3

What is the area of a rectangle that is 5 units long and 3 units wide?

  1. 8 square units
  2. 15 square units
  3. 16 square units
  4. 30 square units

Answer: B — 15 square units

Area = length × width = 5 × 3 = 15 square units. You can also count the squares in a 5-by-3 grid: 3 rows of 5 = 15. This is the tiling model of area from Grade 3 (3.MD.C.5–7).

Question 4

How many sides does a hexagon have?

  1. 5
  2. 6
  3. 7
  4. 8

Answer: B — 6

A hexagon has 6 sides — think of a honeycomb cell. A 5-sided shape is a pentagon, 7 is a heptagon, and 8 is an octagon.

Question 5

Which of these letters has exactly one line of symmetry?

  1. S
  2. H
  3. A
  4. N

Answer: C — A

A line of symmetry means you can fold the letter and both halves match perfectly. A has one vertical line of symmetry. H has two (vertical and horizontal). S and N have no lines of symmetry (they look the same only after being turned 180°, which is rotational symmetry — different concept).

Question 6

Which shape is a quadrilateral but is NOT a parallelogram?

  1. Square
  2. Rectangle
  3. Rhombus
  4. Trapezoid

Answer: D — Trapezoid

A parallelogram has two pairs of parallel sides. Squares, rectangles, and rhombuses all fit that rule. A trapezoid has only one pair of parallel sides — so it's a quadrilateral (4 sides) but not a parallelogram.

Question 7

A right angle measures how many degrees?

  1. 45°
  2. 60°
  3. 90°
  4. 180°

Answer: C — 90°

A right angle is the corner of a square — exactly 90 degrees. 180° is a straight line, 45° is half of a right angle, and 60° shows up in equilateral triangles.

Question 8

A triangle has all three sides the same length. What kind of triangle is it?

  1. Scalene
  2. Isosceles
  3. Equilateral
  4. Right

Answer: C — Equilateral

Equilateral means all three sides are equal (and all three angles are 60°). Isosceles has two equal sides. Scalene has no equal sides. Right means one angle is 90° — separate classification (by angle, not side).

Question 9

A square garden has a side length of 7 feet. What is its area?

  1. 14 sq ft
  2. 28 sq ft
  3. 49 sq ft
  4. 56 sq ft

Answer: C — 49 sq ft

Area of a square = side × side = 7 × 7 = 49 square feet. The perimeter would be 4 × 7 = 28 ft — another common mix-up between area and perimeter.

Question 10

Which figure has both a curved side and a straight side?

  1. Circle
  2. Square
  3. Semicircle
  4. Triangle

Answer: C — Semicircle

A semicircle is half a circle: it has one curved edge (the arc) and one straight edge (the diameter). A circle has only curved edges; a square and triangle have only straight edges.

Question 11

How many right angles does a rectangle have?

  1. 0
  2. 2
  3. 3
  4. 4

Answer: D — 4

Every rectangle has 4 right angles — one at each corner. That's actually the defining property of a rectangle: a quadrilateral with four 90° corners.

Question 12

Which of these units would you use to measure the length of a pencil?

  1. Meters
  2. Kilometers
  3. Centimeters
  4. Liters

Answer: C — Centimeters

A pencil is small, so centimeters are the right unit — a new pencil is about 18 cm long. Meters are for room-size things, kilometers for distances between places, and liters are for volume (not length at all).

Question 13

A rectangle has an area of 24 square inches. If its length is 8 inches, what is its width?

  1. 2 in
  2. 3 in
  3. 4 in
  4. 6 in

Answer: B — 3 in

Area = length × width, so width = area ÷ length = 24 ÷ 8 = 3 inches. Check: 8 × 3 = 24 ✓. This is the connection between area and division that Grade 3 emphasizes (3.MD.C.7.b).

Question 14

Which shape has 8 sides?

  1. Hexagon
  2. Heptagon
  3. Octagon
  4. Nonagon

Answer: C — Octagon

Octagon has 8 sides — think of a STOP sign. Hexagon = 6, heptagon = 7, nonagon = 9.

Question 15

How many lines of symmetry does a square have?

  1. 1
  2. 2
  3. 3
  4. 4

Answer: D — 4

A square has 4 lines of symmetry: one vertical, one horizontal, and two diagonals. A rectangle (that isn't a square) has only 2 lines of symmetry — no diagonals — which is a great follow-up question.

Question 16

Two rectangles have the same area but different shapes. Rectangle A is 2 × 6. Which of these has the same area?

  1. 3 × 3
  2. 3 × 4
  3. 4 × 4
  4. 4 × 3

Answer: B — 3 × 4

Rectangle A has area 2 × 6 = 12 square units. Check each: 3 × 3 = 9, 3 × 4 = 12 ✓, 4 × 4 = 16, 4 × 3 = 12 (same as B, just written differently — but the question lists only one 12). 3 × 4 = 12 matches.

Question 17

Which is the best estimate for the perimeter of a standard piece of notebook paper?

  1. Under 20 cm
  2. About 50 cm
  3. About 100 cm
  4. About 200 cm

Answer: C — About 100 cm

A standard sheet is about 22 cm wide and 28 cm tall. Perimeter ≈ 2 × (22 + 28) = about 100 cm. Estimation is a Grade 3 standard (3.MD) and helps students catch answers that don't make real-world sense.

Question 18

A shape has 4 sides. Two sides are 5 cm long and two sides are 3 cm long. Opposite sides are equal and all four angles are right angles. What is it?

  1. Square
  2. Rectangle
  3. Rhombus
  4. Trapezoid

Answer: B — Rectangle

Rectangle: opposite sides equal, all four angles right angles. It's not a square (sides aren't all equal), not a rhombus (angles aren't right — rhombuses generally don't have right angles), not a trapezoid (a trapezoid only needs one pair of parallel sides).

Question 19

A triangle has one 90° angle. What is the name for this triangle?

  1. Acute triangle
  2. Obtuse triangle
  3. Right triangle
  4. Equilateral triangle

Answer: C — Right triangle

A right triangle has exactly one 90° angle. Acute means all angles are less than 90°, obtuse means one angle is greater than 90°, and equilateral means all angles are 60°.

Question 20

Marcus is tiling a rectangular floor. The floor is 6 feet by 4 feet, and each tile is 1 square foot. How many tiles does he need?

  1. 10 tiles
  2. 20 tiles
  3. 24 tiles
  4. 30 tiles

Answer: C — 24 tiles

The floor's area is 6 × 4 = 24 square feet. Since each tile covers 1 square foot, Marcus needs 24 tiles. This is exactly why 3rd grade uses tiling to teach area — you're literally counting how many unit squares fit inside the shape.

How to coach a 3rd grader through these

Four habits from our Upper West Side classroom:

  1. Draw the shape every time. Even if the problem gives numbers, ask your child to sketch the rectangle before doing any arithmetic. Drawing makes "6 × 4" concrete — a rectangle with 6 rows of 4 boxes — and prevents the area-vs.-perimeter mix-up.
  2. Count sides and corners out loud. When naming a 2D shape, always count. "One, two, three, four, five, six sides … six corners … hexagon." This habit reinforces the defining attributes that Grade 3 asks about.
  3. Ask "does that answer make sense?" every time. If the question is about the perimeter of a piece of paper and your child says "8 centimeters," gently ask: is that a real-world answer? Grade 3 introduces estimation for exactly this reason.
  4. Use a ruler for real things at home. Measure the kitchen table, a book, a shoe. Practice picking units: "Would we measure our couch in centimeters or in meters?" Ten minutes at the dinner table is worth more than a whole workbook page.

If your child gets stuck on a question:

  • "Can you draw the shape?"
  • "Can you label the sides with the numbers from the problem?"
  • "Are we looking for area (inside) or perimeter (around)?"
  • "What unit should the answer be in?"

Those four questions unlock almost every 3rd grade geometry problem.
