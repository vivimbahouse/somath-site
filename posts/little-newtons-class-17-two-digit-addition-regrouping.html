Understanding tens and ones

A tens rod represents ten ones, and a small cube represents one. The number of rods tells us how many tens we have; the number of loose cubes tells us how many ones we have. For example, 43 is 4 tens and 3 ones.

Different arrangements can have the same value. If there are ten or more loose cubes, exchange a group of ten cubes for a rod. Nothing is added or taken away: only the way we show the number changes.

Practice: try these five questions

Q1

A model has 4 tens rods and 3 ones cubes. What number does it show?

Answer

43

Four tens are 40. Add the 3 ones: 40 + 3 = 43.

Q2

Build 60 with tens rods and ones cubes. How many of each do you need?

Answer

6 tens rods and 0 ones cubes

Six tens make 60. There are no loose ones, so write 0 in the ones place.

Q3

Draw a base-10 model for 76. Explain what each digit means.

Answer

7 rods and 6 cubes

The 7 is in the tens place, so it represents 70. The 6 is in the ones place, so it represents 6. Together, 70 + 6 = 76.

Q4

A model has 5 tens rods and 13 loose cubes. What number is it, and how can you show it with fewer loose cubes?

Answer

63; 6 rods and 3 cubes

Five tens and 13 ones make 50 + 13 = 63. Exchange 10 of the cubes for one rod. You then have 6 rods and 3 loose cubes.

Q5

You use exactly 7 pieces: 5 tens rods and the rest ones cubes. What number have you built?

Answer

52

There are 7 − 5 = 2 cubes. Five rods are worth 50, not 5, so the value is 50 + 2 = 52.

Building addition problems

Build each addend in its own row. Put rods in the tens column and cubes in the ones column. In written addition, align tens under tens and ones under ones.

The plus sign asks us to combine the two amounts. Keeping the place values lined up helps us avoid treating tens as ones. You may prepare the model first and then combine the cubes and rods.

Practice: try these five questions

Q6

Build 21 + 34 in two rows. Tell how many rods and cubes belong in each row.

Answer

21: 2 rods and 1 cube; 34: 3 rods and 4 cubes

Split each number by place value: 21 = 20 + 1 and 34 = 30 + 4. Put both sets of rods in the tens column and both sets of cubes in the ones column.

Q7

Set up 32 + 45 in tens and ones columns, then find the total.

Answer

77

Add the ones: 2 + 5 = 7. Keep the 7 ones; no exchange is needed. Add the tens: 3 + 4 = 7 tens. The result is 7 tens and 7 ones, or 77. Check by counting the rods as tens and the cubes as ones.

Q8

For 44 + 28, how many rods and loose cubes are there altogether before you exchange any cubes?

Answer

6 rods and 12 cubes

Combine rods: 4 + 2 = 6. Combine cubes: 4 + 8 = 12. This is a correct unregrouped model; it represents 60 + 12 = 72.

Q9

Draw the two rows for 56 + 17, then combine and regroup.

Answer

73

Add the ones: 6 + 7 = 13. Exchange 10 ones for 1 ten, leaving 3 ones. Add the tens: 5 + 1 + 1 = 7 tens. The result is 7 tens and 3 ones, or 73. Check by counting the rods as tens and the cubes as ones.

Q10

For 61 + 18, a student puts the 8 underneath the 6 and the 1 underneath the 1. What is wrong? Show the correct setup and total.

Answer

The digits of 18 are reversed; the total is 79

The 1 in 18 means one ten, so it belongs under the 6 tens. The 8 ones belong under the 1 one. Then 1 + 8 = 9 ones and 6 + 1 = 7 tens, giving 79.

Adding the ones first

In this written method, start in the ones column. Combine only the ones cubes, not the tens rods. A ones total from 0 through 9 can stay in the ones column.

A ones total of 10 or more needs regrouping. Make one group of ten, move its value into the tens column, and leave the remaining loose cubes in the ones column. Adding the ones first helps us know whether a new ten is coming.

Practice: try these five questions

Q11

In 24 + 13, add only the ones. Will you need to regroup?

Answer

7 ones; no regrouping

The ones are 4 and 3. Since 4 + 3 = 7, fewer than ten cubes are present. Keep all 7 in the ones column.

Q12

In 25 + 16, add only the ones. Can they all remain as a single ones digit?

Answer

11 ones; no

The ones total is 5 + 6 = 11. A place-value digit cannot be 11. Exchange 10 ones for one ten and leave 1 one.

Q13

In 48 + 23, how many ones are there before regrouping, and how many remain afterward?

Answer

11 before; 1 afterward

Add 8 + 3 = 11. Ten cubes become a tens rod, so 11 − 10 = 1 cube remains.

Q14

In 57 + 26, explain why the ones digit of the answer will be 3 rather than 13.

Answer

Because 13 ones become 1 ten and 3 ones

Add 7 + 6 = 13. Record the 3 loose ones in the ones place and include the new ten when adding the tens. The value of 13 has not disappeared.

Q15

In 34 + 2□, which digit makes the ones add to exactly 10? What stays in the ones column?

Answer

□ = 6; 0 ones remain

We need 4 + □ = 10, so the missing digit is 6. All 10 cubes become one rod. Write 0 in the ones place; the full sum is 34 + 26 = 60.

Exchanging ten ones for one ten

Regrouping is a fair exchange: ten ones have exactly the same value as one ten. Circle ten cubes, exchange them for one rod, and count the cubes left over.

Do not keep the ten exchanged cubes and also add a new rod. That would count the same ten twice. Keep the new rod, remove the exchanged cubes, and leave the other cubes alone.

Practice: try these five questions

Q16

Exchange 10 loose cubes for tens rods. How many rods and loose cubes do you have afterward?

Answer

1 rod and 0 loose cubes

All ten cubes are traded for one rod worth ten. No cubes are left outside the group. The value is still 10.

Q17

Regroup 13 ones into tens and ones.

Answer

1 ten and 3 ones

Make a group of 10 and exchange it for a rod. There are 13 − 10 = 3 loose cubes left. Thus 13 = 10 + 3.

Q18

Regroup 18 ones. Explain why you cannot make two tens.

Answer

1 ten and 8 ones

One group of ten leaves 8 cubes. Two tens would require 20 cubes, but there are only 18.

Q19

A model has 3 tens rods and 16 ones cubes. Regroup it and state its value.

Answer

4 tens and 6 ones; 46

Exchange 10 of the 16 cubes for one rod. Add that rod to the 3 original rods: 3 + 1 = 4. Six cubes remain, so the number is 46.

Q20

A model has 4 tens and 19 ones. A student trades 10 cubes for a rod but keeps all 19 cubes too. Explain the error and give the correct model.

Answer

Correct model: 5 tens and 9 ones, or 59

The ten cubes used in the exchange must be removed. Keeping them would count ten twice. Start with 40 + 19 = 59; after the exchange, 50 + 9 = 59.

Connecting regrouping to carrying

The small carried 1 is written above the tens column because it represents one new ten. It does not mean one extra loose cube. This is a written record of the exchange you made with the blocks.

Write the remaining ones in the answer and place the carried 1 above the tens. When you add the tens, include that carried ten exactly once. If no ones remain, write a zero in the ones place.

Q22

The ones make 8 + 7 = 15. Exchange ten ones for a rod, leaving 5 cubes. Record 1 above the tens column and 5 below the ones column.

Q23

Solve 29 + 31. Explain why the answer needs a zero.

Answer

Q24

A student says 39 + 24 = 53 after writing 3 ones. Which step was missed? What is the correct sum?

Answer

The carried ten was missed; 63

Nine plus four gives 13 ones, or 1 ten and 3 ones. Add 3 tens + 2 tens + 1 carried ten = 6 tens. The correct answer is 63, ten more than 53.

Q25

In 46 + 2□, the ones leave 3 cubes after making one new ten. Find the missing digit and the sum.

Answer

□ = 7; the sum is 73

One new ten and 3 remaining ones mean the ones total is 13. Since 6 + □ = 13, □ = 7. Then 4 + 2 + 1 = 7 tens, so 46 + 27 = 73.

Adding all the tens

After regrouping the ones, add the original tens rods and the new rod. Each rod is one ten, so the answer to this step tells you the number of tens, not the number of loose cubes.

Finish by reading the tens and ones together. For instance, 7 tens and 3 ones make 73. Check that you have included every original rod and included the exchanged rod once.

Solving and checking a complete sum

Use the whole routine: build the addends, combine the ones, exchange a group of ten when needed, and add all the tens. A model and a written calculation should tell the same story.

Check by counting the final rods in tens and then adding the loose ones. You can also break an addend into tens and ones and add those parts in a different order. If the checks disagree, look for an omitted or double-counted ten.

Quick questions and answers

What does regrouping mean?

Regrouping means showing the same amount in a different place-value form. In this lesson, ten ones are exchanged for one ten. The value stays the same.

Why do we add the ones first?

In the written carrying method, adding the ones first tells us whether a new ten needs to join the tens column. Other addition methods can start with tens, but they must still account for all the ones and any exchange.

What does the carried 1 represent?

It represents one ten, worth ten ones. It is written above the tens column because it belongs with the tens, not the loose ones.

When is regrouping needed?

Regroup the ones when their total is ten or more. If the ones total is less than ten, keep those ones and do not carry a new ten.

What if exactly ten ones are made?

Exchange all ten ones for a tens rod and write zero in the ones place. The zero shows that no loose ones remain.

No. This is a self-contained Little Newtons lesson in a rolling rotation. It introduces the tens-and-ones ideas needed here; class numbers identify topics, not attendance prerequisites.
