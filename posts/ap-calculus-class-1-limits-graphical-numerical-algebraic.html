What is a limit?

The idea

A limit describes the value that \(f(x)\) approaches as \(x\) gets closer and closer to a number \(a\). We write

\[\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L\]

and read it as “the limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches \(a\) is \(L\).” The arrow tells us which input we approach; \(L\) is the output the function is heading toward.

Reading a table numerically

To estimate a limit numerically, choose inputs on both sides of \(a\) that get closer and closer to it, then look at the outputs. In the image, \(f(x) = x + 1\): the inputs \(1.9, 1.99\) approach 2 from below and \(2.1, 2.01\) approach 2 from above. The outputs \(2.9, 2.99, 3.01, 3.1\) close in on 3, so \(\lim_{x\to 2}(x+1) = 3\).

Approach, not necessarily reach

The limit is about nearby inputs, not the input \(x = a\) itself. The function may be undefined at \(a\), or have a different value there, and the limit can still exist. This is why calculus can describe instantaneous speed and slopes of curves: the formulas break at a single point, but the nearby behaviour is perfectly clear.

Common mistakes

  • Checking only one side of \(a\). A two-sided limit needs both sides.
  • Thinking the limit is the last number in the table. A table only suggests a value; algebra (later topics) confirms it.
  • Assuming \(f(a)\) must equal the limit. Topic 6 shows why it need not.

Q4

Which statement must be true if \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 5} h(x) = 2\)?

  1. \(h(5)\) must equal 2.
  2. As \(x\) gets close to 5, \(h(x)\) gets close to 2.

(B)

Estimating limits from graphs

Follow the curve from both sides

To estimate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x)\) from a graph:

  1. Locate \(x = a\) on the horizontal axis.
  2. Trace the graph toward \(x = a\) from the left and from the right.
  3. Read the \(y\)-value that both sides approach.

In the image, the line approaches the height \(y = 3\) from both directions, so \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2} f(x) = 3\).

Holes, filled dots and corners

  • An open circle (hole) means the function has no value at that point on that piece of the graph. A hole does not prevent a limit from existing.
  • A filled dot shows the actual function value \(f(a)\). It does not affect the limit, which depends only on nearby points.
  • A sharp corner still has a limit when both sides reach the same height.

Reading carefully

Graph readings are estimates. When the curve heads to a point between grid lines, give your best reading and confirm with algebra or a table when a formula is available.

Q9

The graph has a corner at \(x = 2\). Find (a) \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1} f(x)\), (b) \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2} f(x)\), (c) \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 4} f(x)\).

Answer

(a) \(2\) (b) \(3\) (c) \(1\)

  1. (a) Near \(x = 1\) the rising piece is at height 2.
  2. (b) Both pieces meet at the corner \((2, 3)\); left and right both approach 3. A corner does not prevent a limit.
  3. (c) Near \(x = 4\) the falling piece is at height 1.

Q10

True or false: “If a graph has a hole at \(x = a\), then \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x)\) does not exist.” Explain.

Answer

False

  1. A limit depends on the \(y\)-values the graph approaches, not on the point at \(x = a\).
  2. If both sides head toward the same height, the limit exists and equals that height, hole or no hole.
  3. Example: the graph in Q6 has a hole at \(x = 1\) and its limit there is 4.

Approaching from the left and from the right

Notation for each side

The image introduces the notation used throughout calculus for the direction of approach:

  • \(x \to 2^-\) means \(x\) approaches 2 using values less than 2 (from the left): \(1.9, 1.99, 1.999, \dots\)
  • \(x \to 2^+\) means \(x\) approaches 2 using values greater than 2 (from the right): \(2.1, 2.01, 2.001, \dots\)

The small sign is a direction, not a negative or positive number. \(2^-\) is still near 2, not near \(-2\).

Look near the point

On \(y = x + 1\) with a hole at \(x = 2\), the left side heads to 3 and the right side heads to 3. Because both sides approach the same \(y\)-value, \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2} f(x) = 3\). The hole at \(x = 2\) changes nothing about the nearby heights.

Tables with both sides

A good numerical table uses inputs that get closer on each side, for example \(0.9, 0.99, 0.999\) and \(1.001, 1.01, 1.1\) when \(a = 1\). Read the left column of outputs and the right column of outputs separately, then compare.

One-sided limits: the direction of approach matters

Left-hand and right-hand limits

\[\lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) \quad\text{uses } x\text{-values less than } a, \qquad \lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) \quad\text{uses } x\text{-values greater than } a.\]

In the image, the graph approaches height 1 from the left of \(x = 2\) and height 3 from the right. So \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2^-} f(x) = 1\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2^+} f(x) = 3\).

Jumps

A graph that breaks and restarts at a different height has a jump. Each side has its own one-sided limit, but because they differ, there is no single height the graph approaches. Therefore \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2} f(x)\) does not exist (DNE).

Piecewise formulas

For a function defined by different rules on each side of \(a\), use the left rule for \(x \to a^-\) and the right rule for \(x \to a^+\). Substitute \(a\) into each rule that is a polynomial; the value at \(x = a\) itself is not needed.

\[f(x) = \begin{cases} x + 1, & x \lt 2 \\ 7 - x, & x \ge 2 \end{cases} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \lim_{x\to 2^-} f(x) = 3,\ \ \lim_{x\to 2^+} f(x) = 5.\]

Q17

Let \(f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2, & x \lt 2 \\ 3x - 1, & x \ge 2 \end{cases}\). Find both one-sided limits at \(x = 2\) and decide whether \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2} f(x)\) exists.

Answer

Left \(4\), right \(5\); the limit does not exist.

  1. Left rule: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2^-} x^2 = 4\).
  2. Right rule: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2^+}(3x - 1) = 5\).
  3. \(4 \ne 5\), so \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2} f(x)\) does not exist.

Q18

Let \(g(x) = \dfrac{|x|}{x}\). Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^-} g(x)\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+} g(x)\).

Answer

\(-1\) and \(1\)

  1. For \(x \lt 0\), \(|x| = -x\), so \(g(x) = -x/x = -1\).
  2. For \(x \gt 0\), \(|x| = x\), so \(g(x) = 1\).
  3. The one-sided limits are \(-1\) and \(1\); the two-sided limit at 0 does not exist.

Q20

The floor function \(\lfloor x \rfloor\) gives the greatest integer less than or equal to \(x\). Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2^-} \lfloor x \rfloor\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2^+} \lfloor x \rfloor\).

Answer

\(1\) and \(2\)

  1. Just left of 2 (e.g. \(1.99\)), \(\lfloor x \rfloor = 1\).
  2. Just right of 2 (e.g. \(2.01\)), \(\lfloor x \rfloor = 2\).
  3. The floor function jumps at every integer, so \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2}\lfloor x \rfloor\) does not exist.

When does a limit exist?

The existence test

A finite two-sided limit exists exactly when both one-sided limits exist and equal the same real number:

\[\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) = \lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) = L.\]

Check both sides

  1. Find the left-hand limit.
  2. Find the right-hand limit.
  3. Compare. Same value: the limit exists and equals it. Different values: the limit does not exist.

Other ways a limit can fail

Besides a jump, a limit fails to be a finite number when the outputs grow without bound (for example \(1/x^2\) near 0) or oscillate without settling. Those cases are studied with limits at infinity and continuity in later classes.

The value at the point is irrelevant

The value \(f(a)\) does not determine the limit. A piecewise function can define \(f(a)\) as anything, or leave it undefined, and the limit depends only on the two sides.

Making a limit exist

When a piecewise function contains an unknown constant, set the two one-sided limits equal and solve for the constant.

The limit vs. the function value

Two different questions

  • The limit \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x)\) asks: where are the nearby outputs heading?
  • The function value \(f(a)\) asks: what is the output exactly at \(x = a\)?

In the image, the line heads to the hole at height 3, so \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2} f(x) = 3\), but the filled dot shows \(f(2) = 1\).

Three possible situations

  1. The limit and \(f(a)\) are equal (the graph is unbroken at \(a\)).
  2. The limit exists but \(f(a)\) is a different number (a hole with a displaced dot).
  3. The limit exists but \(f(a)\) is undefined (a hole with no dot).

The limit can also fail to exist while \(f(a)\) is defined, as at a jump. When the limit equals \(f(a)\), the function is called continuous at \(a\); that idea is the subject of a later class.

Q28

Q29

True or false: “If \(f(4) = 7\), then \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 4} f(x) = 7\).” Give a reason.

False

  2. Counterexample: \(f(x) = x\) for \(x \ne 4\) and \(f(4) = 7\). Then \(f(4) = 7\) but \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 4} f(x) = 4\).
  3. The statement is true only for functions continuous at 4.

Both equal \(1\).

  2. The dot is on the line, so \(f(3) = 1\).

Limit laws and direct substitution

The limit laws

If \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} g(x) = M\), then

\[\lim_{x\to a}[f(x) \pm g(x)] = L \pm M, \qquad \lim_{x\to a}[f(x)\,g(x)] = LM,\]
\[\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{L}{M}\ \ (M \ne 0), \qquad \lim_{x\to a}[f(x)]^n = L^n\ \ (n \text{ a positive integer}).\]

A constant multiple follows from the product law: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} c\,f(x) = cL\).

Direct substitution

Polynomials, and rational functions whose denominator is not zero at \(a\), are continuous there. For them, the limit equals the value: substitute \(x = a\).

\[\lim_{x\to 2}(3x^2 - 4x + 1) = 3(2)^2 - 4(2) + 1 = 12 - 8 + 1 = 5.\]

When substitution is not enough

  • If substitution gives a real number, that number is the limit (for continuous functions).
  • If it gives \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), the form is indeterminate: factor or rationalize first (Topics 8 and 9).
  • If it gives a nonzero number over 0, the limit is not a finite number; check one-sided behaviour.

  2. Numerator: \(2^2 + 1 = 5\). The limit is \(5/5 = 1\).

Q35

Try direct substitution on each limit. State the result, or say that another method is needed: (a) \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 3}\frac{x + 5}{x - 2}\) (b) \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 4}\frac{x^2 - 16}{x - 4}\).

Answer

(a) \(8\) (b) \(\tfrac{0}{0}\): another method is needed

  1. (a) Denominator \(3 - 2 = 1 \ne 0\); the value is \(8/1 = 8\).
  2. (b) Substitution gives \(\dfrac{16 - 16}{4 - 4} = \dfrac{0}{0}\), an indeterminate form.
  3. Factoring (Topic 8) finishes (b): \(\dfrac{(x-4)(x+4)}{x-4} = x + 4 \to 8\).

Limits by factoring

Simplify first, then evaluate

  1. Try substitution. For \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 3}\frac{x^2-9}{x-3}\), substitution gives \(\tfrac{0}{0}\): indeterminate, so keep working.
  2. Factor. \(x^2 - 9 = (x-3)(x+3)\).
  3. Cancel the common factor. \(\dfrac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} = x + 3\) for \(x \ne 3\).
  4. Evaluate the simplified limit. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 3}(x + 3) = 6\).

Why cancellation is allowed

A limit only uses \(x\)-values near 3, where \(x \ne 3\), so \(x - 3 \ne 0\) and dividing it out is legal. The original expression is still undefined at \(x = 3\); its graph is the line \(y = x + 3\) with a hole.

Useful factoring patterns

\[a^2 - b^2 = (a-b)(a+b), \qquad a^3 - b^3 = (a-b)(a^2 + ab + b^2),\]
\[x^2 + bx + c = (x + p)(x + q) \text{ where } p + q = b,\ pq = c.\]

A \(\tfrac{0}{0}\) result at \(x = a\) means \((x - a)\) is a factor of both numerator and denominator, which tells you what to look for.

Limits by rationalizing

When a radical causes \(\tfrac{0}{0}\)

For \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sqrt{x+4} - 2}{x}\), substitution gives \(\dfrac{\sqrt4 - 2}{0} = \dfrac{0}{0}\). Factoring does not help directly, because the radical is a difference. Multiply by the conjugate.

The four steps

  1. Try substitution: \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), indeterminate.
  2. Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate \(\sqrt{x+4} + 2\).
  3. Simplify using \((A - B)(A + B) = A^2 - B^2\):
    \[\frac{(x + 4) - 4}{x(\sqrt{x+4} + 2)} = \frac{x}{x(\sqrt{x+4} + 2)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x+4} + 2}, \quad x \ne 0.\]
  4. Evaluate: \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt4 + 2} = \dfrac{1}{4}\).

Tips

  • Leave the conjugate factor in the denominator unexpanded; it is what you substitute into at the end.
  • The conjugate of \(\sqrt{u} - c\) is \(\sqrt{u} + c\); the conjugate of \(\sqrt{u} - \sqrt{v}\) is \(\sqrt{u} + \sqrt{v}\).
  • If the radical is in the denominator, multiply by its conjugate in the same way.

One limit, three methods

Three views of the same limit

The image studies \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2}\frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2}\) in three ways:

  1. Graphical. The graph is the line \(y = x + 2\) with a hole at \((2, 4)\). Both sides approach \(y = 4\).
  2. Numerical. The table values \(3.9, 3.99, 4.01, 4.1\) suggest a limit of 4.
  3. Algebraic. \(\dfrac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x - 2} = x + 2\) for \(x \ne 2\), so the limit is \(2 + 2 = 4\).

Which method proves the answer?

Graphs and tables suggest a limit; algebra justifies the exact value. A table can be misleading if the chosen inputs are not close enough, and a graph can hide small details. On AP free-response questions, show the algebra whenever a formula is given.

Choosing a method

  • Substitution gives a number: done (Topic 7).
  • \(\tfrac00\) with polynomials: factor (Topic 8).
  • \(\tfrac00\) with a square root: rationalize (Topic 9).
  • Piecewise or absolute value: compute each one-sided limit (Topics 4 and 5).
  • Only a graph or table given: read both sides (Topics 1 to 3).

W1

A ball dropped from a tower falls \(s(t) = 16t^2\) feet in \(t\) seconds. Its average speed from \(t = 1\) to \(t = 1 + h\) is \(\dfrac{16(1+h)^2 - 16}{h}\). Find the limit as \(h \to 0\) and interpret it.

Answer

\(32\) feet per second, the ball's speed at \(t = 1\)

  1. Expand: \(16(1 + 2h + h^2) - 16 = 32h + 16h^2\).
  2. Divide by \(h\) (\(h \ne 0\)): \(32 + 16h\).

Quick questions and answers

What is a limit in AP Calculus?

A limit describes the value that f(x) approaches as x gets closer to a number a, written lim f(x) = L as x approaches a. It depends on nearby inputs, not on the value at x = a itself.

How do you find a limit from a graph?

Locate x = a, trace the graph toward it from the left and from the right, and read the y-value both sides approach. A hole at the point does not prevent the limit from existing.

When does a limit not exist?

A two-sided limit fails to exist when the left-hand and right-hand limits are different, as at a jump. It also fails to be a finite number when the outputs grow without bound or oscillate.

Can a limit exist if f(a) is undefined?

Yes. The limit depends only on values near a. For example, (x² − 4)/(x − 2) is undefined at x = 2, but its limit there is 4.

What does 0/0 mean when evaluating a limit?

0/0 is an indeterminate form. It means direct substitution is not enough: factor and cancel, or multiply by a conjugate, then substitute into the simplified expression.

Which method should I use: graphical, numerical or algebraic?

Graphs and tables suggest a limit; algebra justifies the exact value. Use substitution when it gives a number, factoring or rationalizing for 0/0, and one-sided limits for piecewise or absolute-value functions.
