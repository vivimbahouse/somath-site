The exact 2026 AP score release timeline

The College Board follows a consistent calendar each year, and 2026 is no different. Here are the key dates you need to know, with no fluff:

Date What happens Action required?
May 4–15 & May 18–22, 2026 AP exam testing windows Exams taken
June 15, 2026 Deadline to cancel a score (before seeing it) Cancel if needed (rare)
June 20, 2026 Deadline for free score sends to colleges (one free send per exam) Already passed
July 1, 2026 Colleges begin receiving official AP score reports No action needed
July 6, 2026 — 8 AM ET Students can view scores; East Coast releases first, rolls west through the morning Log in to College Board
July 6, 2026 onward Score withholding option opens ($10 per score per college) Review scores; withhold if needed

A few things worth noting. The free score send deadline was June 20 — that window has already closed. If your child designated a college to receive their score before the exam, that report is already on its way.

If they did not send one or want to send scores to additional schools, each official report now costs $15 per score per institution, ordered through the College Board AP scores page. Colleges receive reports starting July 1 — five days before students see their own scores. That means an admissions office technically has your child’s score before your child does, if an official report was sent.

The score withholding option opens July 6. This is not a cancellation — it prevents a specific score from being sent to a specific school. If a score was already sent via the free-send option, you can still withhold it from additional schools going forward, but you cannot retroactively pull a score already delivered.

East Coast rollout: The 8 AM ET start is when the College Board’s servers open. In practice, students on the East Coast — including everyone in NYC — are typically able to see scores within a few minutes of 8 AM. West Coast students usually see their scores an hour or two later. Check the College Board AP exam calendar for any updates to the 2026 release schedule.

How to actually check your AP scores

Scores are available at apscore.collegeboard.org — not through the main collegeboard.org homepage, though links from there will redirect you correctly. Your child needs to log in with the same College Board account used when registering for the exams. If they have forgotten their login credentials, here is the fastest path:

  1. Go to collegeboard.org and click “Sign In.”
  2. Click “Forgot Username or Password” and enter the email address used when registering for the exam.
  3. If they do not remember which email they used, check any College Board confirmation emails from spring 2026, or contact the school’s AP coordinator, who can look up registration details.
  4. Once logged in, navigate to “My AP” → “Scores.” Scores for all years will appear here.

The score report shows the exam name, the year, and a single digit from 1 to 5. It does not show the number of questions you answered correctly or your raw score — only the final scaled score. For most AP math and science exams, the score report will also list a brief descriptor next to the number. You can also see your score history across all AP exams ever taken through the same account.

If a score is missing — particularly common when a student registered late or took an exam at a different school — contact the College Board directly through their student help center. Processing delays occasionally push some scores to appear a day or two after July 6.

What each score means in plain English

The College Board uses a five-point scale with official descriptors. Here is what each score means, and what it means in the context of NYC’s competitive schools:

Score College Board descriptor What it means in practice NYC competitive school context
5 Extremely well qualified Genuine mastery of the material. Earns credit or placement at most schools that have a credit policy. Among the strongest outcomes possible. Expected baseline for Stuyvesant students in core APs. Ivy applicants typically present multiple 5s in their intended field.
4 Very well qualified Strong performance. Earns credit at many selective schools; earns placement at nearly all. Nothing to be embarrassed about. A 4 in AP Calculus BC is a strong result at any NYC school. A 4 from Bronx Science or Beacon carries real weight.
3 Qualified Adequate grasp of the material. Earns credit at some schools (mostly less selective), placement at many. The most common score nationally. Fine for most purposes but below the median for Stuyvesant’s highest-performing AP cohort. Typically doesn’t earn credit at Ivies.
2 Possibly qualified Below the threshold for credit or placement at the vast majority of schools. The student passed the course but the exam was not a strength. Do not self-report unless required. Does not help an admissions file. Consider omitting from Common App.
1 No recommendation Well below the passing threshold. No credit, no placement. Does not indicate course failure, but the exam was not passed. Omit from self-reporting. Focus on understanding why and whether the course load was appropriate.

A few NYC-specific data points worth knowing. College Board AP program results data shows that nationally, roughly 20–25% of students score a 5 on most AP exams, with significant variation by subject. AP Calculus BC historically has a higher rate of 5s (around 40%) partly because students self-select into the course.

At Stuyvesant, where students routinely take 10–12 APs over four years, the effective median in competitive STEM subjects skews toward 4s and 5s. At Bronx Science and Brooklyn Tech, similar patterns hold. Private schools like Trinity, Collegiate, and Columbia Prep tend to have fewer APs per student but higher scores per exam, in part due to more preparation time and smaller class sizes.

The 7-day decision tree: what to do by score

Here is the actionable breakdown. The 7-day window matters because score withholding requests and additional score report orders need to be made while admissions season planning is still fresh. Don’t wait until August.

If your child scored a 5

Celebrate genuinely — a 5 represents real mastery. Then do three things this week. First, check whether the colleges on your list award credit or placement for a 5 on this specific exam (see the credit table below). Second, if this is a math or science AP, use the score as a launching point: the student’s foundation is strong, and the question is what challenge comes next.

Third, if there are more APs coming next year, build a plan for those now while momentum is high. A 5 in AP Calculus AB is the ideal signal to begin planning the AB-to-BC progression if BC is on the horizon.

If your child scored a 4

A 4 is a genuinely good score. Do not let the internet convince you otherwise. The main decision this week is whether to send the score to colleges and whether to pursue credit. At selective colleges (Columbia, Cornell, Penn, MIT), a 4 on most exams earns placement but often not credit — meaning they can skip the introductory course but won’t receive credit hours.

At Boston University, NYU, Northeastern, and most public universities, a 4 earns actual course credit. Check the College Board’s AP credit policy search tool for the specific school and exam. On the admissions side, a 4 is positive information — self-report it on the Common App with confidence.

If your child scored a 3

A 3 is “qualified” by the College Board’s own standard, which means the student demonstrated a grasp of the material. The practical question is twofold. First, on the credit side: most selective schools do not award credit for a 3 (see the table below for specifics).

A 3 may earn placement — the right to skip into a higher course — at some schools, which has real value if your child plans to major in that field. Second, on the admissions side: a 3 is neither exciting nor alarming. It’s fine to self-report it; it’s also fine to leave it off.

The key question is whether it’s consistent with the rest of the application. A student with a 3 in AP Chemistry and a 5 in AP Calculus BC is clearly strong in math — the 3 does not undermine anything. A student with 3s across multiple exams in their intended major should think carefully about how that looks alongside their transcript.

If your child scored a 2

A 2 is below the threshold for credit or placement at essentially all colleges. The primary action: do not self-report it on the Common App unless the school requires reporting all scores (very few do). Do not send an official score report to colleges. The score will remain in the College Board account indefinitely but is invisible to colleges unless you explicitly send it.

On the “what now” side: a 2 usually signals one of three things — the course was genuinely above the student’s current math preparation, the student was spread too thin across too many APs, or the exam format (timed, high-stakes, free-response heavy) was a problem that extra prep could solve. All three are addressable. A free 60-minute evaluation at SOMATH is a useful diagnostic here: we can tell you which gaps are causing the score and what a realistic remediation looks like.

If your child scored a 1

A 1 is a hard result to sit with, and the honest thing to say is: do not minimize it, but do not catastrophize it either. A 1 is not a course failure — your child passed the AP class at school regardless of the exam score. What a 1 tells you is that something significant is not connecting between the coursework and the College Board’s exam format, usually because the underlying math foundation has gaps that the course relied on but never addressed.

In NYC’s competitive school environment, this is more common than people admit: students at Stuyvesant and Bronx Science sometimes take 4–5 APs simultaneously and the foundational gaps in one subject don’t surface until exam day. Do not self-report. Do not order an official score report to any college. And do use the summer to genuinely address what went wrong — not to re-memorize formulas, but to rebuild the foundation the score revealed was missing.

The college credit question: which schools accept which scores

This is the most practically important section for families making decisions right now. “Credit” and “placement” are two different things, and many families confuse them.

  • Credit means the college counts the AP score as equivalent to a college course and awards actual course credit — reducing the total credits needed to graduate and potentially saving tuition money.
  • Placement means the college allows the student to skip an introductory course and enter a higher-level one, but does not give credit for the introductory course. You still pay for the same number of courses; you just start at a higher level.

The following table covers AP Calculus BC (one of the most common APs for NYC math students) as a reference point. Policies for other exams follow the same general structure. Always verify current policies using the College Board AP credit policy search tool — institutions update these annually.

NYU (CAS) 4 or 5 4 = MATH-UA 121 (Calc I); 5 = 121 + 122 (Calc I + II) 3 = placement into Calc I at NYU Courant, no credit Stern has separate policies; verify by school

The retake question: when AP retakes make sense (and when they don’t)

The retake question comes up every July, and the honest answer is: AP retakes make sense in a very narrow set of circumstances.

Why retakes are rarely the right move

The economics are unfavorable for most students. AP exams are offered once per year, in May. To retake an exam, your child would need to either re-enroll in the AP course (at most high schools, that’s the prerequisite) or self-study for an entire additional year. The time cost of that preparation is almost always better spent on new coursework, new APs, or standardized test prep.

Meanwhile, colleges do not see a “best score” from multiple AP attempts — they see all scores sent to them. If you send a retake score, the original is also visible if you sent it during the initial reporting window. If you did not send the original, the retake is the only one they see.

On the admissions side, the calculus is simple: a low AP score that was never sent to a college is invisible. You do not need to “fix” it by retaking. You can simply move on.

The exception is a student who needs a specific score for college credit at a school they are definitely attending — for example, a student who will enroll at Boston University in the fall and got a 3 on AP Calculus BC (which does not earn BU credit), but knows they need to test out of Calculus I to stay on track for a double major. That is a legitimate retake scenario.

College reporting policies

What do colleges actually see? If your child applied to a school as a rising senior and self-reported a 3 on the Common App, the college saw that 3. It is already in their file. A retake a year later does not retroactively change that. If they did not self-report the score and did not send an official report, the college has no record of it.

The Common App self-reporting section is voluntary — you choose which scores to list. For AP scores specifically, there is no obligation to report all of them.

How AP scores affect college admissions for rising juniors and seniors

This is the section for students who just finished junior year (rising seniors applying in the fall) and for students finishing sophomore year (rising juniors who still have two AP cycles ahead of them).

Rising seniors: self-reporting on the Common App

The Common App opens August 1 for the fall 2026 cycle. In the academic honors and coursework section, students self-report AP scores. The rules:

  • Report scores that help you. Any 4 or 5 in a subject relevant to your intended major or area of strength is worth reporting.
  • Omit scores that hurt you. A 1 or 2 that you did not self-report during any previous interaction with the college does not need to appear on the Common App.
  • A 3 is a judgment call. In a subject outside your intended major, a 3 is probably fine to include. In your intended major, think carefully about whether it helps or hurts your narrative.
  • Don’t lie. If you did report a 3 on a supplement or earlier school form, do not omit it from the Common App now. Consistency matters.
  • Official score reports are sent separately. Self-reported scores on the Common App are not verified until after admission. Most schools only require official AP score reports after enrollment, not as part of the application.

Score withholding

The score withholding option, which opens July 6, allows you to prevent a specific AP score from being sent to a specific institution. This is useful when you requested a free score send before the exam (deadline: June 20) and then received a score you would rather that school not see.

It costs $10 per score per institution to withhold. Withheld scores are never deleted — they remain in the College Board account and can be sent to other institutions at any time. Score cancellation (permanent deletion from your record) is a separate, more drastic option that is almost never advisable.

Course load decisions

One of the biggest AP performance mistakes we see at SOMATH is course-load inflation. Students at Stuyvesant routinely attempt 12+ APs over four years; students at Bronx Science often carry 5–6 per year by junior and senior year. For math-focused APs specifically, the sequencing matters more than the quantity.

A student who has genuinely mastered pre-calculus will perform dramatically better in AP Calculus AB than a student who was “moved along” through pre-calc without filling the gaps. If this July’s score revealed a gap, the honest response is to address the gap over the summer rather than race to the next level.

The most effective AP Calculus preparation starts in the summer before the course, not in October when the homework pile is already full. If your child attends a specific NYC school

AP culture varies significantly across NYC schools. Here is what July 6 means at each of the schools we see most often at SOMATH:

Stuyvesant High School

Stuyvesant students take more APs than almost anyone in the country. A 3 at Stuy in a core STEM subject is genuinely below the school median and worth addressing, not minimizing. A 4 or 5 is strong confirmation.

If a student got a 3 on AP Calculus BC — one of the most important APs for Stuy’s competitive college applicants — use this summer to rebuild the foundation, not to cram the same material faster. The course structure at Stuyvesant is demanding; the students who excel are those who arrive in September already fluent in the prerequisite material.

Bronx Science and Brooklyn Tech

Similar to Stuyvesant but with slightly more variation in AP load across the student body. A 4 or 5 in AP Calculus AB from Bronx Science is a very strong result for college applications. The jump from AB to BC is significant, and students who got a 3 on AB should think carefully about whether BC is the right next step without summer foundation work.

Beacon High School and LaGuardia High School

Both schools have strong AP programs but with different cultures around math. Beacon students often take fewer math APs but benefit from strong humanities APs. LaGuardia’s arts-intensive environment means math APs are often taken by a self-selecting motivated group. A 4 or 5 from either school in math carries genuine weight in applications and is worth self-reporting.

Bard High School Early College (BHSEC)

BHSEC operates on a college course model in junior and senior year, meaning students take actual college courses rather than APs in most subjects. AP scores from BHSEC students typically come from 9th and 10th grade APs. A strong AP Calculus score from BHSEC is notable because it often represents self-directed preparation above and beyond the school’s already demanding coursework.

Hunter College High School

Hunter is a grades 7–12 school with its own highly competitive internal culture. If a Hunter student got a 3 on a math AP, it is worth taking seriously: the school context means the material was covered thoroughly, and the exam gap is real and addressable.

Trinity, Columbia Prep, and Trevor Day

Private school AP culture tends to involve fewer exams per student but more focused preparation per exam. A 5 from Trinity or Columbia Prep carries real weight. These schools also tend to have strong faculty who align coursework closely to the AP exam, so a 3 here genuinely indicates a gap rather than a curriculum mismatch.

Students at Columbia Prep and Trevor Day who are planning Calculus BC next year should use July to get ahead: the math prep that distinguishes strong AP results at private schools almost always starts over the summer.

Ethical Culture Fieldston, Dalton, Brearley, Spence, and Riverdale

Frequently asked questions

What time do AP scores come out on July 6, 2026?

The College Board releases 2026 AP scores starting at 8 AM Eastern Time on Monday, July 6, 2026. The rollout is regional — East Coast students can typically view their scores within a few minutes of 8 AM ET, with the window rolling westward through the morning. Log in at apscore.collegeboard.org using the College Board account your child used to register for the exams.

Can I cancel an AP score after seeing it?

You can withhold a score starting July 6, 2026. Withholding prevents the score from being sent to a specific college and costs $10 per score per institution. It does not delete the score from your College Board account permanently — the score remains available for you to send elsewhere at any time. Score cancellation (permanent deletion) is a separate process and is almost never the right choice, since withheld scores achieve the same practical result for admissions purposes.

Will colleges see my child’s score even if we don’t report it?

No. Colleges only see AP scores that are officially sent via the College Board’s reporting system. On the Common App, AP scores are self-reported (you type them in voluntarily), and you choose which scores to include. Official score reports are a separate step and must be requested by the student. If your child did not send an official score report and does not self-report a score on the Common App, the college has no record of that score.

What’s a good AP score for Ivy League admissions?

Competitive applicants to Harvard, Columbia, Yale, Princeton, and Penn typically present mostly 5s on their AP exams, especially in subjects related to their intended major. A 4 is generally fine and will not hurt a strong application. A 3 in a core subject is worth leaving off the self-report unless the school asks for all scores (very few require this). For students at Stuyvesant, Bronx Science, or comparable schools, the effective competitive bar is high because the comparison pool already skews toward 5s in STEM APs.

Should my child retake an AP exam if they got a 3?

Almost never. A 3 you did not send to colleges is invisible. The time required to retake an AP exam effectively — roughly another full year of preparation — is almost always better spent on the next level of coursework, SAT prep, or extracurriculars. The one exception is a student who needs a minimum score for credit at a specific college they plan to attend, and who has a clear plan to address the gap. Outside that scenario, move forward rather than backward.

How long do AP scores stay on the College Board account?

AP scores remain in your College Board account indefinitely. There is no expiration. You can log in years later and order official score reports sent to colleges, graduate schools, or employers. Individual colleges may have their own recency policies — a handful of schools specify that AP credit must be from within a certain number of years — but most accept scores from any year for credit and placement purposes.
