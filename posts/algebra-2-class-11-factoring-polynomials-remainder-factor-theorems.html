1. What factoring actually means

Factoring means rewriting a polynomial as a product of simpler polynomials. It is the exact inverse of “multiplying out.” When we multiplied \((x+2)(x+3)\) in earlier classes we got \(x^2 + 5x + 6\). Factoring is going the other direction: starting from \(x^2 + 5x + 6\) and getting back to \((x+2)(x+3)\).

The check is always the same: if you multiply your factored answer back out and you don’t recover the original polynomial, you factored it wrong. This one-line quality check catches almost every mistake.

Why we care. Factoring lets us simplify rational expressions, solve polynomial equations, find zeros of functions, and describe where a graph crosses the \(x\)-axis. Every one of those uses shows up on the Algebra II Regents, SAT, and Precalculus placement tests.

2. The five-technique factoring toolbox

Ninety-five percent of the factoring problems a student sees before Precalculus fall into one of five patterns. When we hand a student a polynomial in class, the first thing we ask them out loud is: “Which of the five tools does this look like?”

1. Greatest common factor (GCF)

Always look first. Pull out the largest number and the largest variable power that divides every term.

\(6x^3 + 9x^2 = 3x^2(2x + 3)\)

2. Trinomial \(x^2 + bx + c\)

Find two numbers that multiply to \(c\) and add to \(b\).

\(x^2 + 5x + 6 = (x+2)(x+3)\)

3. Difference of squares

Whenever you see \(a^2 - b^2\), factor as \((a-b)(a+b)\). Sum of squares does not factor over the reals.

\(x^2 - 9 = (x - 3)(x + 3)\)

4. Perfect square trinomial

If \(a^2 \pm 2ab + b^2\), it factors as \((a \pm b)^2\). The middle term is the giveaway: it’s twice the product of the square roots of the outer terms.

\(x^2 + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)^2\)

5. Grouping (four terms)

Group into two pairs, factor a common piece from each pair, then factor the common binomial.

\(x^3 + 2x^2 + 3x + 6 = (x+2)(x^2 + 3)\)

Master order

1) GCF first — always. 2) Count terms. Two terms → difference of squares. Three terms → trinomial or perfect square. Four terms → grouping. 3) Check your factors are themselves completely factored.

GCF → count → check.

Worked example — combining tools. Factor \(2x^3 - 8x\) completely.

  • GCF first: \(2x^3 - 8x = 2x(x^2 - 4)\).
  • Now factor the remaining piece. \(x^2 - 4\) is a difference of squares.
  • \(2x(x^2 - 4) = 2x(x - 2)(x + 2)\). Done.

Missing the GCF first is the number-one factoring mistake we see in class. Always pull it out before trying anything else.

3. Factors, zeros, and \(x\)-intercepts

Once a polynomial is factored, finding its zeros is a one-line move. If \(f(x) = (x - 2)(x - 3)\), the zeros are the values of \(x\) that make \(f(x) = 0\). Since a product is zero when any factor is zero:

\((x - 2)(x - 3) = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x = 2 \text{ or } x = 3\)

Those same values are the \(x\)-intercepts of the graph — the points where the parabola \(y = x^2 - 5x + 6\) crosses the \(x\)-axis: \((2, 0)\) and \((3, 0)\). This equivalence is the reason factoring matters in Algebra II: factors, zeros, roots, solutions, and \(x\)-intercepts are five different names for the same information.

4. The Remainder Theorem

Remainder Theorem

When a polynomial \(f(x)\) is divided by \((x - a)\), the remainder is \(f(a)\).

\(f(x) \div (x - a) \;\longrightarrow\; \text{remainder} = f(a)\)

This one theorem replaces polynomial long division for finding remainders. Instead of setting up the whole long-division tableau, we just plug \(a\) into the polynomial.

Worked example. Find the remainder when \(f(x) = x^3 + 2x^2 - 5x + 3\) is divided by \(x - 2\).

  • Identify \(a\). Since \(x - 2 = x - a\), we have \(a = 2\).
  • Evaluate \(f(2) = 2^3 + 2(2^2) - 5(2) + 3 = 8 + 8 - 10 + 3 = 9\).
  • The remainder is \(\mathbf{9}\) — no long division needed.

5. The Factor Theorem

Factor Theorem

\((x - a)\) is a factor of \(f(x)\) if and only if \(f(a) = 0\).

\((x - a) \text{ is a factor of } f(x) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; f(a) = 0\)

The Factor Theorem is the Remainder Theorem when the remainder happens to be zero. If plugging \(a\) into the polynomial gives zero, then \((x - a)\) divides evenly — so it is a factor. And vice versa.

Worked example. Is \((x - 2)\) a factor of \(f(x) = x^3 - 4x^2 + x + 6\)?

  • Calculate \(f(2) = 2^3 - 4(2^2) + 2 + 6 = 8 - 16 + 2 + 6 = 0\).
  • Since \(f(2) = 0\), yes — \((x - 2)\) is a factor of \(f(x)\).

6. The sign trap in \(x + a\) factors

Rule to memorize — always use the OPPOSITE sign

The Factor Theorem is stated for factors of the form \((x - a)\). When the factor is \((x + a)\), rewrite it as \((x - (-a))\) and test \(f(-a)\).

FactorTest value
\(x - 3\)\(f(3)\)
\(x + 3\)\(f(-3)\)
\(x + 4\)\(f(-4)\)
\(x - 5\)\(f(5)\)

Every year, half the mistakes on a Factor-Theorem test come from testing \(f(3)\) for the factor \((x + 3)\). Say the rule out loud when you set up each problem: “Factor \(x + 3\), so I test \(f(-3)\).” Always use the opposite of the number you see in the factor.

7. Completely factoring a cubic (Factor Theorem + synthetic division)

Here is where the Factor Theorem earns its keep. A cubic like \(f(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 11x - 6\) does not factor with any of our five standard tools. But we can chase down a factor one substitution at a time:

  1. Guess a small \(a\) — usually a factor of the constant term. Here the constant is \(-6\), so try \(\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 6\).
  2. Try \(x = 1\): \(f(1) = 1 - 6 + 11 - 6 = 0\). By the Factor Theorem, \((x - 1)\) is a factor.
  3. Divide by \((x - 1)\) using synthetic division. The quotient is \(x^2 - 5x + 6\), remainder \(0\).
  4. Factor the quadratic: \(x^2 - 5x + 6 = (x - 2)(x - 3)\).
  5. Write the complete factorization: \(f(x) = (x - 1)(x - 2)(x - 3)\).
  6. Read off the zeros: \(x = 1, \; x = 2, \; x = 3\).
Where the guesses come from. The Rational Root Theorem (Class 12 next week) tells us the only rational zeros of a polynomial with integer coefficients are ratios of factors of the constant term over factors of the leading coefficient. That’s why we start with the divisors of \(-6\) — those are the only candidates worth testing.

8. The Algebra II big picture

Class 11 is really about one chain of ideas that runs through all of Algebra II and every math course after it:

Polynomial  →  Factor  →  Zero  →  \(x\)-intercept

  • The polynomial is an algebraic expression in \(x\).
  • A factor writes the polynomial as a product of simpler pieces.
  • A zero is a value of \(x\) that makes the polynomial equal zero — you get zeros by setting each factor to zero.
  • An \(x\)-intercept is where the graph crosses the \(x\)-axis — the point \((a, 0)\) for each zero \(a\).

Once a student can move fluently across those four representations, the second half of Algebra II opens up: rational functions, polynomial inequalities, end behavior, and the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra all sit on top of this chain.

6GCF + trinomial

Factor completely: \(2x^2 + 10x + 12\).

\(2(x + 2)(x + 3)\).

GCF first: \(2(x^2 + 5x + 6)\). Then factor the trinomial inside.

7GCF + diff of squares

Factor completely: \(3x^3 - 12x\).

\(3x(x - 2)(x + 2)\).

GCF is \(3x\). What’s left is \(x^2 - 4\), a difference of squares.

8Grouping

Factor by grouping: \(x^3 + 2x^2 + 3x + 6\).

\((x + 2)(x^2 + 3)\).

Group: \((x^3 + 2x^2) + (3x + 6) = x^2(x + 2) + 3(x + 2)\). Common binomial \((x + 2)\) factors out.

9Trinomial with a

Factor: \(2x^2 + 7x + 3\).

\((2x + 1)(x + 3)\).

Multiply \(a \cdot c = 2 \cdot 3 = 6\). Two numbers multiplying to \(6\) and adding to \(7\): \(1\) and \(6\). Split: \(2x^2 + x + 6x + 3 = x(2x+1) + 3(2x+1) = (2x+1)(x+3)\).

10Perfect square

Factor: \(9x^2 + 12x + 4\).

\((3x + 2)^2\).

\(\sqrt{9x^2} = 3x\) and \(\sqrt{4} = 2\). Middle term \(2 \cdot 3x \cdot 2 = 12x\) — matches. Perfect square trinomial.

11Diff of squares

Factor: \(49 - x^2\).

\((7 - x)(7 + x)\).

Same pattern, just written with the constant first. \(a = 7\), \(b = x\).

12Zeros

Find the zeros of \(f(x) = x^2 + 4x - 5\).

\(x = -5\) and \(x = 1\).

Factor: two numbers multiplying to \(-5\) and adding to \(4\) are \(5\) and \(-1\). So \(f(x) = (x + 5)(x - 1)\). Set each factor to zero.

\(9\).

Find the remainder when \(f(x) = x^4 - 3x^2 + 2\) is divided by \(x + 2\).

\(6\).

Find the remainder when \(f(x) = x^3 - 2x + 4\) is divided by \(x + 1\).

\(5\).

17Remainder + interpret

Given \(f(x) = x^3 + kx^2 - 4x + 12\), find the value of \(k\) so that dividing by \(x - 2\) leaves a remainder of \(0\).

\(k = -3\).

By the Remainder Theorem, remainder \(= f(2)\). Compute: \(f(2) = 2^3 + k(2^2) - 4(2) + 12 = 8 + 4k - 8 + 12 = 4k + 12\). Set the remainder to \(0\): \(4k + 12 = 0 \Rightarrow k = -3\). Check: with \(k = -3\), \(f(2) = 8 - 12 - 8 + 12 = 0\). ✓

Factor Theorem & the sign trap (18–22)

18Factor test

Is \((x - 1)\) a factor of \(f(x) = x^3 - x^2 - 4x + 4\)?

Yes.

\(f(1) = 1 - 1 - 4 + 4 = 0\). Since \(f(1) = 0\), \((x - 1)\) is a factor.

19Sign trap

Is \((x + 3)\) a factor of \(f(x) = x^3 + 3x^2 - 4x - 12\)?

Yes.

For factor \(x + 3\), test \(f(-3)\) — opposite sign. \(f(-3) = -27 + 27 + 12 - 12 = 0\). So \((x + 3)\) is a factor.

20Sign trap

Is \((x + 1)\) a factor of \(f(x) = x^3 + 3x^2 - 4x - 12\)?

No.

Test \(f(-1) = -1 + 3 + 4 - 12 = -6 \ne 0\). Since \(f(-1) \ne 0\), \((x + 1)\) is not a factor.

21Sign trap

What value should you substitute into \(f(x)\) to check whether \((x + 7)\) is a factor?

\(f(-7)\).

Factor \(x + 7\) is the same as \(x - (-7)\), so \(a = -7\).

22Find a factor

Given \(f(x) = x^3 - 7x + 6\), test small integer values and find one linear factor.

\((x - 1)\) is a factor (also \((x - 2)\) and \((x + 3)\)).

Divisors of the constant \(6\): \(\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 6\). Test \(f(1) = 1 - 7 + 6 = 0\) ✓. Bonus: \(f(2) = 8 - 14 + 6 = 0\) ✓ and \(f(-3) = -27 + 21 + 6 = 0\) ✓ — you have all three factors of the cubic.

Completely factor — putting it all together (23–25)

23Cubic

Completely factor \(f(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 11x - 6\) and find its zeros.

\(f(x) = (x - 1)(x - 2)(x - 3)\). Zeros: \(x = 1, 2, 3\).

Try \(x = 1\): \(f(1) = 1 - 6 + 11 - 6 = 0\) — so \((x - 1)\) is a factor. Synthetic division by \(1\): coefficients \(1, -6, 11, -6\) → quotient \(x^2 - 5x + 6 = (x - 2)(x - 3)\).

24Cubic

Completely factor \(f(x) = x^3 + 2x^2 - 5x - 6\) and find its zeros.

\(f(x) = (x - 2)(x + 1)(x + 3)\). Zeros: \(x = 2, -1, -3\).

Test candidates. \(f(2) = 8 + 8 - 10 - 6 = 0\) ✓. Divide by \((x - 2)\) synthetically: quotient \(x^2 + 4x + 3 = (x + 1)(x + 3)\).

25Challenge

Completely factor \(f(x) = 2x^3 - x^2 - 8x + 4\) and find its zeros.

\(f(x) = (2x - 1)(x - 2)(x + 2)\). Zeros: \(x = \tfrac{1}{2}, \; 2, \; -2\).

By the Rational Root Theorem (Class 12 preview), candidates are \(\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 4, \pm \tfrac{1}{2}\). Test \(f(2) = 16 - 4 - 16 + 4 = 0\) ✓. Divide by \((x - 2)\) synthetically: quotient \(2x^2 + 3x - 2 = (2x - 1)(x + 2)\).

Word problems 1–10 — all Hard

W1Hard · Box volume

An open-top box is made from a square sheet of cardboard 12 in on each side by cutting a square of side \(x\) from each corner and folding up the sides. (a) Write a factored expression for the volume \(V(x)\) of the box in cubic inches. (b) Expand \(V(x)\) into standard polynomial form. (c) For what values of \(x\) does the box have zero volume? What do those values mean physically?

(a) \(V(x) = x(12 - 2x)^2\). (b) \(V(x) = 4x^3 - 48x^2 + 144x\). (c) Zero volume at \(x = 0\) and \(x = 6\).

The base of the finished box is a square of side \(12 - 2x\) (you cut \(x\) off each side), and the height is \(x\). So \(V = x(12 - 2x)^2\). Expanding: \((12 - 2x)^2 = 144 - 48x + 4x^2\), then multiply by \(x\) to get \(4x^3 - 48x^2 + 144x\). Factoring the standard form back: \(V(x) = 4x(x^2 - 12x + 36) = 4x(x - 6)^2\), zeros at \(x = 0\) (no cut, no height) and \(x = 6\) (cut removes the whole side, base disappears). Only \(0 < x < 6\) gives a real box.

W2Hard · Projectile

A ball is thrown upward from a 96 ft cliff with initial velocity 16 ft/s. Its height (ft) after \(t\) seconds is \(h(t) = -16t^2 + 16t + 96\). (a) Factor \(h(t)\) completely. (b) When does the ball hit the ground? (c) When is the ball at 96 ft again on the way down?

(a) \(h(t) = -16(t - 3)(t + 2)\). (b) At \(t = 3\) seconds. (c) At \(t = 1\) second.

(a) Factor out \(-16\): \(-16(t^2 - t - 6) = -16(t - 3)(t + 2)\). (b) Ground = height 0: \(-16(t - 3)(t + 2) = 0\) gives \(t = 3\) or \(t = -2\); only \(t = 3\) makes physical sense. (c) Set \(h(t) = 96\): \(-16t^2 + 16t = 0 \Rightarrow -16t(t - 1) = 0\), so \(t = 0\) (throw) or \(t = 1\) (returning to same height).

W3Hard · Revenue

A local bakery sells cupcakes. When priced at $4 each, they sell 200 per week. Each $1 increase in price loses them 20 sales per week. Let \(x\) be the number of $1 price increases. (a) Write a factored expression for weekly revenue \(R(x)\). (b) Expand to standard form. (c) At what values of \(x\) is revenue zero, and what do they mean?

(a) Factored revenue: \(R(x) = (4 + x)(200 - 20x)\) Equivalently: \(R(x) = 20(4 + x)(10 - x)\)

(b) Standard form: \(R(x) = -20x^2 + 120x + 800\)

(c) \(x = -4\): price = $0 (giveaway).
\(x = 10\): price = $14 (zero customers).

Set up revenue. Revenue = price × quantity: \(R(x) = (4 + x)(200 - 20x)\) Factor 20 from the second factor: \(R(x) = 20(4 + x)(10 - x)\)

Expand one step at a time. \(R(x) = 20(40 - 4x + 10x - x^2)\) Combine like terms: \(R(x) = 20(-x^2 + 6x + 40)\) Distribute 20: \(R(x) = -20x^2 + 120x + 800\)

Interpret the zeros. The factored form gives \(x = -4\) (a $0 price) and \(x = 10\) (zero customers). The vertex is at \(x = 3\), corresponding to a $7 price and maximum weekly revenue: \(R(3) = 20(7)(7) = \$980\)

W4Hard · Consecutive integers

The product of three consecutive positive integers is 336. (a) Let \(n\) be the middle integer; write a polynomial equation whose solution gives \(n\). (b) Use the Factor Theorem to find \(n\). (c) What are the three integers?

(a) \((n - 1)n(n + 1) = 336\), i.e., \(n^3 - n = 336\). (b) \(n = 7\). (c) The integers are \(6, 7, 8\).

(a) The three consecutive integers are \(n - 1\), \(n\), \(n + 1\); their product is \(n(n^2 - 1) = n^3 - n\). Set equal to 336: \(n^3 - n - 336 = 0\). (b) By the Factor Theorem, if \(n = 7\) is a root then \((n - 7)\) is a factor. Test \(f(7) = 343 - 7 - 336 = 0\) ✓. So \((n - 7)\) is a factor. Synthetic division gives \(n^3 - n - 336 = (n - 7)(n^2 + 7n + 48)\); the quadratic factor has negative discriminant (\(49 - 192 < 0\)), so \(n = 7\) is the only real solution. (c) \(6 \cdot 7 \cdot 8 = 336\) ✓.

W5Hard · Area optimization

A rectangle has a perimeter of 40 cm. Let \(x\) be its width. (a) Write a factored expression for its area \(A(x)\). (b) Expand to standard form. (c) At what widths is the area zero? For what width is the area maximized, and what is that maximum area?

(a) \(A(x) = x(20 - x)\). (b) \(A(x) = -x^2 + 20x\). (c) Zero at \(x = 0\) and \(x = 20\); maximum at \(x = 10\) with area 100 cm\(^2\) (a square).

Perimeter 40 means \(2(x + \text{length}) = 40\), so length \(= 20 - x\). Area \(= x(20 - x)\), a factored quadratic that expands to \(-x^2 + 20x\). Zeros come straight from the factored form. The vertex is halfway between the zeros: \(x = 10\), giving \(A(10) = 10 \cdot 10 = 100\) — the classic “a square gives the largest area for a fixed perimeter” result, proven here by factoring.

W6Hard · Divide the pumpkin patch

A farmer designs a rectangular pumpkin patch. The length is 3 ft more than twice the width \(w\). She wants a total area of 90 ft\(^2\). (a) Write a polynomial equation in \(w\). (b) Factor to solve. (c) Give the dimensions in feet.

(a) \(w(2w + 3) = 90\), i.e., \(2w^2 + 3w - 90 = 0\). (b) \((2w + 15)(w - 6) = 0\), so \(w = 6\). (c) Width 6 ft, length 15 ft.

Length \(= 2w + 3\); area \(= w(2w + 3) = 2w^2 + 3w\). Set equal to 90: \(2w^2 + 3w - 90 = 0\). Use the AC-method (Class 11 trinomial-with-a technique): \(2 \times -90 = -180\); factors of \(-180\) that add to \(3\) are \(15\) and \(-12\). Split: \(2w^2 + 15w - 12w - 90 = w(2w + 15) - 6(2w + 15) = (2w + 15)(w - 6)\). Zeros at \(w = -\tfrac{15}{2}\) (rejected, negative) and \(w = 6\). Length \(= 2(6) + 3 = 15\).

W7Hard · Remainder Theorem in physics

A drone’s vertical position above a launch pad (in meters) is modeled by \(p(t) = t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t + 4\), where \(t\) is seconds after launch. (a) Use the Remainder Theorem to find its height at \(t = 5\) without expanding. (b) Show that \(t = 4\) is a moment when the drone is at height 8 m. (c) Find every non-negative time at which the drone is at height 8 m.

(a) \(p(5) = 24\) m. (b) \(p(4) = 8\) ✓. (c) At \(t = 1\) s and \(t = 4\) s.

(a) Remainder Theorem: dividing \(p(t)\) by \((t - 5)\) gives remainder \(p(5) = 125 - 150 + 45 + 4 = 24\). (b) Compute \(p(4) = 64 - 96 + 36 + 4 = 8\). By the Remainder Theorem, dividing \(p(t)\) by \((t - 4)\) leaves remainder \(8\), so the drone is at height 8 m at \(t = 4\). (c) Set \(p(t) = 8\): \(t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t - 4 = 0\). Since \(t = 4\) is one solution, the Factor Theorem tells us \((t - 4)\) is a factor. Synthetic division by \((t - 4)\) on coefficients \(1, -6, 9, -4\): bring down 1; \(1 \cdot 4 = 4\), \(-6 + 4 = -2\); \(-2 \cdot 4 = -8\), \(9 + (-8) = 1\); \(1 \cdot 4 = 4\), \(-4 + 4 = 0\). Quotient \(t^2 - 2t + 1 = (t - 1)^2\). So \(t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t - 4 = (t - 4)(t - 1)^2\), giving \(t = 1\) (a double root — the drone momentarily hovers at 8 m on its way up) and \(t = 4\) (returning to 8 m).

W8Hard · Profit

A startup’s monthly profit (in thousands of dollars) is modeled by \(P(x) = x^3 - 4x^2 - 11x + 30\), where \(x\) is the number of thousands of units sold. (a) Verify that \(x = 2\) is a break-even point (profit = 0). (b) Completely factor \(P(x)\). (c) Give all break-even values of \(x\) and interpret them.

(a) \(P(2) = 8 - 16 - 22 + 30 = 0\) ✓. (b) \(P(x) = (x - 2)(x - 5)(x + 3)\). (c) Break-even at \(x = 2\), \(x = 5\), and \(x = -3\) (rejected — can’t sell negative units). Real break-even points: 2000 units and 5000 units. Between them, profit is negative; above 5000 units, profit grows.

(a) Direct substitution or Remainder Theorem. (b) Factor Theorem: \((x - 2)\) is a factor. Synthetic division gives quotient \(x^2 - 2x - 15\), which factors as \((x - 5)(x + 3)\). Full factorization: \((x - 2)(x - 5)(x + 3)\). (c) Only positive roots are physically meaningful, so break-even at 2000 and 5000 units.

W9Hard · Poster with border

A rectangular poster has length 2 in longer than its width \(w\). A 1-inch-wide printed border runs on all four sides; the image area inside the border is 24 in\(^2\). (a) Write a polynomial equation in \(w\). (b) Factor to solve. (c) Give the full poster dimensions.

(a) \((w - 2)w = 24\), i.e., \(w^2 - 2w - 24 = 0\). (b) \((w - 6)(w + 4) = 0\), so \(w = 6\) in. (c) Full poster is 6 in wide by 8 in long.

Full poster: width \(w\), length \(w + 2\). The image inside the border is 1 in smaller on each side, so its width is \(w - 2\) and its length is \((w + 2) - 2 = w\). Image area = \((w - 2) \cdot w = 24\), giving \(w^2 - 2w - 24 = 0\). Trinomial factoring: two numbers that multiply to \(-24\) and add to \(-2\) are \(-6\) and \(4\). So \((w - 6)(w + 4) = 0\); \(w = 6\) (reject \(w = -4\), no negative widths). Full poster is 6 in \(\times\) 8 in. Check: image is \(4 \times 6 = 24\) ✓.

W10Hard · Complete cubic factorization

A polynomial models the deflection of a beam: \(D(x) = 2x^3 - 3x^2 - 11x + 6\), where \(x\) is position (in ft) along the beam. (a) Verify that \(x = 3\) is a zero using the Remainder Theorem. (b) Completely factor \(D(x)\). (c) List all positions where the beam has zero deflection.

(a) \(D(3) = 54 - 27 - 33 + 6 = 0\) ✓. (b) \(D(x) = (x - 3)(2x - 1)(x + 2)\). (c) Zero deflection at \(x = 3\), \(x = \tfrac{1}{2}\), and \(x = -2\); on a physical beam that starts at \(x = 0\), the relevant zeros are \(x = \tfrac{1}{2}\) ft and \(x = 3\) ft.

(a) Remainder Theorem confirms \((x - 3)\) is a factor. (b) Synthetic division by \(x = 3\) on coefficients \(2, -3, -11, 6\): bring down 2; \(2 \cdot 3 = 6\), \(-3 + 6 = 3\); \(3 \cdot 3 = 9\), \(-11 + 9 = -2\); \(-2 \cdot 3 = -6\), \(6 + (-6) = 0\). Quotient \(2x^2 + 3x - 2 = (2x - 1)(x + 2)\). Full factorization: \((x - 3)(2x - 1)(x + 2)\). (c) Zeros at \(x = 3, \tfrac{1}{2}, -2\). Only non-negative positions apply to a physical beam.

11. FAQ for parents

What should my child already know before this class?

Fluency with the distributive property, multiplying binomials, and factoring simple trinomials like \(x^2 + 5x + 6\). If your student is comfortable with our Pre-Algebra Class 12 on expressions and the Algebra 1 quadratics unit, they are ready.

Where does this show up on tests?

The Factor Theorem and Remainder Theorem appear directly on the Algebra II Regents, the SAT (Advanced Math domain), and every precalculus placement test. In our own SAT sessions they show up disguised as “which of the following is a factor of the polynomial\(\ldots\)” multiple-choice questions.

How do I help without knowing the material?

Ask your student to say out loud what test value the factor tells them to plug in. If they can articulate “for factor \(x + 3\), I test \(f(-3)\),” they have the sign trap under control.
