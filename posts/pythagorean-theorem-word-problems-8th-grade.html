Pythagorean Theorem Word Problems for 8th Grade: 5 Examples Explained

When to use \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\), five worked examples with different purposes, the formulas and triples to know, two proofs, and the real history of Pythagoras, from a Babylonian tablet now kept at Columbia University to Fermat’s Last Theorem.

By Marcelo Ambrozio Ramos, Founder of School of Math New YorkNovember 24, 202627-minute read
Book a Free Math Evaluation

The short answer: use the Pythagorean theorem in a word problem when the situation contains two lengths that meet at a right angle and a third length that connects their endpoints. Sketch the triangle, label the side opposite the right angle as the hypotenuse, and circle the length the question asks you to find. Then write \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\), where \(c\) is always the hypotenuse, and solve. Finally, reread the question: the length you calculated is not always the answer it asks for.

This guide teaches that habit through five worked examples, each with a different purpose: finding a diagonal and then answering the real question, finding a missing leg, deciding whether the theorem applies at all, measuring distance on a coordinate grid, and working in three dimensions. Along the way you will meet the man whose name is on the theorem, the clay tablet a few subway stops from our classroom that shows the idea is far older than he is, and a proof simple enough to draw on a napkin.

A question you could picture on the Upper West Side

A rectangular community garden is 30 feet wide and 40 feet long. You walk along two edges to reach the opposite corner. Your friend takes the diagonal path. How many feet does your friend save?

Most eighth graders who know the theorem get to work quickly. The two edges meet at a right angle, so the diagonal is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs 30 and 40:

\[30^2 + 40^2 = 900 + 1600 = 2500 = 50^2\]

The diagonal is 50 feet. Many students circle 50 and move on. But read the question again. It does not ask how long the diagonal is. It asks how many feet your friend saves. Your walk along the edges is \(30 + 40 = 70\) feet, so the shortcut saves

\[70 - 50 = 20 \text{ feet.}\]

When a student in my class answers 50, I ask one question: “If you answered 50 feet, what question did you just answer?” The student usually laughs, because the answer is obvious once it is spoken aloud: they answered “How long is the diagonal?” That is a useful step, but it is not the question on the page. Learning to notice the difference is the single most valuable habit in word problems, and the Pythagorean theorem is a perfect place to practice it.

How do you know when to use the Pythagorean theorem in a word problem?

Word problems rarely announce “use the Pythagorean theorem.” Instead they describe a ladder, a ramp, a screen, a map, a box, or a shortcut across a field. Your job is to recognize the shape hiding inside the story. Here is the test I teach, in the same words every time:

Look for two lengths that meet at a right angle and a third length that connects their endpoints. Sketch the triangle, label the side opposite the right angle as the hypotenuse, and circle the length the question asks you to find.

Right angles hide in ordinary words. A wall meets the ground at a right angle. A flagpole stands straight up. City streets on a grid meet at right angles. The length and width of a rectangle meet at a right angle, which is why every rectangle’s diagonal makes two right triangles. “Due north, then due east” describes a right angle. “Directly above” and “directly below” describe a right angle with the ground. When you see these phrases, start sketching.

The formulas you need, in one place

The Pythagorean theorem states that in any right triangle with legs \(a\) and \(b\) and hypotenuse \(c\),

\[a^2 + b^2 = c^2.\]

Everything else in this guide is a rearrangement or an extension of that one sentence. Eighth graders in New York need the first four rows of the table below. The last rows preview Geometry and competition math, and students who see them early tend to find high school geometry much friendlier.

Table 1. Pythagorean formulas and when to use each

Find a missing leg\(a = \sqrt{c^2 - b^2}\)You know the hypotenuse and one leg: a ladder’s height, a kite’s height
Test for a right angle (converse)If \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\), the angle opposite \(c\) is rightYou know all three sides and must decide whether a corner is square

Two reminders before the examples. First, the theorem relates the squares of the sides, not the sides themselves. A 3-4-5 triangle works because \(9 + 16 = 25\), not because \(3 + 4\) has anything to do with 5. Second, when you take a square root at the end, keep only the positive value. Lengths are positive, so \(c^2 = 2500\) gives \(c = 50\), not \(\pm 50\).

Example 1: Find the diagonal, then answer the real question

Purpose: practice the full routine, including the final step most students skip.

Problem. A rectangular park lawn is 48 meters long and 20 meters wide. Maya walks from one corner to the opposite corner along the edges. Leo walks straight across the diagonal. Both walk at the same speed, 1.3 meters per second. How many seconds sooner does Leo arrive?

Sketch and label. The length and width of a rectangle meet at a right angle, so the diagonal is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs 48 and 20.

Circle the question. The question asks for seconds saved, not meters. That warns us there will be two extra steps after the theorem.

Solve.

\[c^2 = 48^2 + 20^2 = 2304 + 400 = 2704, \qquad c = \sqrt{2704} = 52 \text{ m.}\]

Maya walks \(48 + 20 = 68\) meters. Leo walks 52 meters. Leo’s path is \(68 - 52 = 16\) meters shorter. At 1.3 meters per second, 16 meters takes

\[\frac{16}{1.3} \approx 12.3 \text{ seconds.}\]

Answer and check. Leo arrives about 12 seconds sooner. Check: the hypotenuse, 52, is longer than each leg but shorter than their sum, 68, which is exactly what a shortcut should look like. If you had answered 52, you would have answered “How long is the diagonal?” If you had answered 16, you would have answered “How many meters shorter is the diagonal?” Only 12.3 answers the question on the page.

Teaching note. This is the garden problem with one more layer. When a word problem gives a rate, a price, or a time, expect the theorem to be the middle of the solution, not the end.

Example 2: Find a missing leg

Purpose: recognize when the hypotenuse is given, and subtract instead of add.

Problem. A painter leans a 13-foot ladder against the side of a brownstone on West 79th Street. For safety, the foot of the ladder is 5 feet from the base of the wall. How high up the wall does the ladder reach?

Sketch and label. The wall is vertical and the ground is level, so they meet at a right angle at the base of the wall. The ladder connects the top of one leg to the end of the other, so the ladder is the hypotenuse: \(c = 13\). The distance along the ground is one leg: \(b = 5\). The height is the other leg, \(a\).

Solve.

\[a^2 + 5^2 = 13^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad a^2 = 169 - 25 = 144 \quad\Rightarrow\quad a = 12 \text{ ft.}\]

Answer and check. The ladder reaches 12 feet up the wall. Check: 12 is less than 13, as a leg must be. The most common wrong answer is \(\sqrt{13^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{194} \approx 13.9\) feet, which would mean the ladder reaches higher than its own length. That is impossible, and noticing that impossibility is a skill worth praising.

Teaching note. Before solving, ask: “Is the length I am looking for the longest side?” If yes, add. If no, subtract. That one question prevents most missing-leg mistakes.

Example 3: Decide whether the theorem applies

Purpose: use the converse to test a right angle, and learn to say “the theorem does not apply here.”

Problem, part A. A carpenter is building a rectangular raised garden bed. She measures 6 feet along one side from a corner and 8 feet along the adjacent side, then measures straight across between those two marks: 10 feet. Is the corner square?

Solve. Here we know all three sides and are asked whether the angle is right. That is the job of the converse of the Pythagorean theorem: if the squares of the two shorter sides add up to the square of the longest side, the angle opposite the longest side is a right angle.

\[6^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100 = 10^2.\]

The corner is square. Builders have used versions of this check for a very long time; carpenters today still call it the 3-4-5 method, since 6-8-10 is just 3-4-5 doubled.

Problem, part B. A triangular flower bed in a neighborhood park has sides of 7 feet, 9 feet, and 12 feet. A student wants to use the Pythagorean theorem to find its height. Can she?

Solve. Test the longest side first:

\[7^2 + 9^2 = 49 + 81 = 130, \qquad 12^2 = 144.\]

Since \(130 \neq 144\), the triangle is not a right triangle, so the theorem does not describe its three sides. And because \(144 > 130\), the angle opposite the 12-foot side is obtuse. That does not end the problem forever: in Geometry, she can drop an altitude to create two right triangles, or later use the Law of Cosines. But she cannot plug 7, 9, and 12 into \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\) as if it were true.

Answer and check. Part A: yes, the corner is square. Part B: no, the theorem does not apply directly, because the triangle has no right angle.

Teaching note. New York’s eighth-grade standard includes understanding a proof of the theorem and its converse (NYSED Grade 8 crosswalk). Many test questions are built to reward students who check whether a triangle is right before using the theorem.

Example 4: Distance on a coordinate grid

Purpose: see that the distance formula is the Pythagorean theorem in disguise.

Problem. On a park map, each grid square is 50 feet on a side. The water fountain is at point \(A(-2, 1)\) and the playground gate is at \(B(4, 9)\). Paths run only east–west and north–south. A drone flies straight from the fountain to the gate. How much shorter is the drone’s flight than the shortest walk along the paths, in feet?

Sketch and label. Go from \(A\) straight east until you are under \(B\), then straight north to \(B\). Those two moves meet at a right angle at \((4, 1)\). The horizontal leg is \(4 - (-2) = 6\) units. The vertical leg is \(9 - 1 = 8\) units. The drone’s path is the hypotenuse.

Solve.

\[d = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} = 10 \text{ units.}\]

The walk is \(6 + 8 = 14\) units. The difference is \(14 - 10 = 4\) units, and each unit is 50 feet, so the drone’s flight is \(4 \times 50 = 200\) feet shorter.

Answer and check. 200 feet. Notice what we just did: we computed \(\sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}\) without ever memorizing the distance formula. That is the point. The distance formula is not a new idea; it is the Pythagorean theorem written in coordinates. A student who understands that will never be stuck if the formula slips from memory during a test.

Teaching note. Subtracting a negative coordinate is where errors live: \(4 - (-2) = 6\), not 2. Counting the squares on the grid is a perfect check.

Example 5: Three dimensions

Purpose: use the theorem twice, and turn a “does it fit?” question into a comparison.

Problem. A student wants to mail a 40-inch rolled poster in a box that measures 24 inches long, 18 inches wide, and 16 inches tall. The poster cannot bend. Will it fit if placed diagonally, from a bottom corner to the opposite top corner? If not, what is the longest rigid tube that fits?

Step 1: the floor. The length and width of the floor meet at a right angle, so the floor diagonal is

\[\sqrt{24^2 + 18^2} = \sqrt{576 + 324} = \sqrt{900} = 30 \text{ in.}\]

Step 2: up to the top corner. The vertical edge rises at a right angle from the floor. So the floor diagonal (30) and the height (16) are the legs of a second right triangle, standing up inside the box. Its hypotenuse is the space diagonal:

\[\sqrt{30^2 + 16^2} = \sqrt{900 + 256} = \sqrt{1156} = 34 \text{ in.}\]

Combining both steps gives the shortcut formula \(d = \sqrt{24^2 + 18^2 + 16^2}\), which equals 34 as well.

Answer and check. The longest straight line inside the box is 34 inches. A 40-inch poster tube will not fit; the longest rigid tube that fits is 34 inches (in practice a little less, to allow for the tube’s thickness). Check: 34 is longer than every edge and longer than the floor diagonal, as it should be.

Teaching note. New York’s eighth-grade standard explicitly includes real-world problems “in two and three dimensions” (NYSED). The box problem is the classic three-dimensional case, and it reappears in Regents Geometry, the SAT, and the AMC 8.

Pythagorean triples worth knowing

A Pythagorean triple is a set of three whole numbers that fit \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\). Test writers love them, because they let a problem have a clean answer. A student who recognizes 5-12-13 or 8-15-17 on sight saves time and gains a built-in check.

Table 4. Primitive Pythagorean triples and their common multiples

5, 12, 13\(25 + 144 = 169\)10-24-26, 15-36-39Ladder problems
8, 15, 17\(64 + 225 = 289\)16-30-34Ramps and screen sizes; Example 5’s second step
7, 24, 25\(49 + 576 = 625\)14-48-50SAT and AMC questions

Notice that Example 1 (48, 20, 52) is the 12-5-13 triple multiplied by 4, and Example 5’s second step (16, 30, 34) is 8-15-17 doubled. Once students see this, they start looking for triples everywhere, which is exactly the kind of pattern-seeking that makes strong problem solvers.

For curious students, there is a recipe that generates every primitive triple. Choose whole numbers \(m > n > 0\) that share no common factor and are not both odd. Then

\[a = m^2 - n^2, \qquad b = 2mn, \qquad c = m^2 + n^2\]

always works. With \(m = 2, n = 1\) you get 3-4-5; with \(m = 3, n = 2\), 5-12-13; with \(m = 4, n = 1\), 15-8-17. Expanding both sides proves it:

\[\begin{aligned}(m^2 - n^2)^2 + (2mn)^2 &= m^4 - 2m^2n^2 + n^4 + 4m^2n^2\\ &= m^4 + 2m^2n^2 + n^4 = (m^2 + n^2)^2.\end{aligned}\]

This formula appears in Book X of Euclid’s Elements, and it is a lovely first taste of number theory for a strong eighth grader.

Why it is true: two proofs a student can draw

New York expects eighth graders to understand a proof of the Pythagorean theorem, not just use it (NYSED). I agree strongly. A student who can explain why \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\) owns the theorem; a student who only memorized it is renting it. There are hundreds of proofs. Elisha Scott Loomis collected 367 of them in The Pythagorean Proposition, later republished by the National Council of Teachers of Mathematics (Cut the Knot). Here are two of my favorites.

Proof 1: Rearranging four triangles

Start with a large square whose side is \(a + b\). Place four copies of a right triangle with legs \(a\) and \(b\) inside it, one in each corner, so the hypotenuses form a tilted square in the middle. That middle square has side \(c\) and area \(c^2\). (Its corners are right angles because, at each point on the edge of the big square, the two acute angles of the triangle plus the corner of the tilted square make a straight line: \(x + y + 90° = 180°\), and \(x + y = 90°\) in a right triangle.)

Now slide the same four triangles into a different arrangement: pair them up into two rectangles. The uncovered region becomes two squares, one with side \(a\) and one with side \(b\). The big square has not changed, and the four triangles have not changed. So the uncovered area must be the same in both pictures:

\[c^2 = a^2 + b^2.\]

No algebra required. Some historians see an argument of this visual kind in the ancient Chinese text Zhoubi Suanjing, whose “gougu” diagram shows a square on the hypotenuse built from right triangles (MacTutor).

Proof 2: The proof by a future president

In 1876, while serving in the U.S. House of Representatives, James A. Garfield published a proof of his own in the New-England Journal of Education. Five years later he became the twentieth president of the United States (Mathematical Association of America). His idea uses a trapezoid made of three right triangles: two copies of the original triangle, with legs \(a\) and \(b\), and one isosceles right triangle whose equal sides are both \(c\).

The trapezoid has parallel sides \(a\) and \(b\) and height \(a + b\). Compute its area two ways and set them equal:

\[\frac{1}{2}(a + b)(a + b) = 2\left(\frac{1}{2}ab\right) + \frac{1}{2}c^2.\]

Multiply both sides by 2 and expand: \(a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = 2ab + c^2\). Subtract \(2ab\) from both sides:

\[a^2 + b^2 = c^2.\]

I love sharing this proof with students who think mathematics belongs only to professional mathematicians. A congressman from Ohio found a new proof of a theorem known for more than three thousand years because he was curious. So can a seventh grader on West 79th Street.

Who was Pythagoras, and did he discover the theorem?

Pythagoras is one of the most famous names in mathematics and one of the least documented lives. He left no writings of his own; everything we know comes from later authors (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy). According to the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, he lived from about 570 to about 490 BCE, spent his early years on the Greek island of Samos, and around age forty emigrated to Croton in southern Italy, where he founded a community with a distinctive way of life (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy). His followers, the Pythagoreans, became associated with the idea that number underlies the order of the world. Later tradition credits them with discovering that musical harmony rests on simple whole-number ratios, such as 2:1 for the octave and 3:2 for the fifth (MacTutor History of Mathematics).

Here is the surprising part, and I think it is the most important history lesson in this article: the theorem is much older than Pythagoras, and there is no solid evidence that he proved it. The Stanford Encyclopedia notes that none of the early sources, including Plato and Aristotle, connect Pythagoras to the theorem, and that the famous story of him sacrificing an ox to celebrate its discovery comes to us through much later writers such as Proclus, in the fifth century CE. It concludes that there is “not a jot of evidence” for a proof by Pythagoras (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy). MacTutor notes that the Babylonians knew the relationship about a thousand years earlier, while allowing that Pythagoras may have been the first to prove it; the Stanford Encyclopedia’s reading of the evidence is more skeptical (MacTutor).

A Babylonian tablet a few subway stops from SOMATH

One of the most important pieces of evidence lives in Manhattan. Plimpton 322, a small clay tablet from Larsa in present-day Iraq, dated to about 1820–1762 BCE, is held by Columbia University’s Rare Book & Manuscript Library in Morningside Heights (Columbia University Libraries). In the 1940s, Otto Neugebauer and Abraham Sachs showed that its columns of cuneiform numbers correspond to Pythagorean triples. The historian Eleanor Robson later argued that the tablet was most likely a teacher’s aid for generating right-triangle problems: in Columbia’s words, “the work of an excellent teacher, not a lone genius” (Columbia University Libraries).

I find that wonderful. Nearly four thousand years ago, a teacher was preparing right-triangle problems for students. That is exactly what this article is doing, a short ride up Broadway from where Plimpton 322 is kept.

A second tablet, YBC 7289 in the Yale Babylonian Collection, shows a square with its diagonals and a value for \(\sqrt{2}\), the diagonal of a unit square, accurate to about six decimal places (Mathematical Association of America). That is a Pythagorean calculation, done centuries before Pythagoras was born.

India, China, and Greece

The idea appears independently in other ancient cultures. The Indian Śulbasūtras, manuals for building fire altars, state the relationship for the diagonal of a square in the Baudhāyana Śulbasūtra, usually dated to around 800 BCE, and for the diagonal of a rectangle in later texts. They also list triples such as 5-12-13 and 8-15-17 (MacTutor). In China, the Zhoubi Suanjing, compiled between about 100 BCE and 100 CE, discusses the right-triangle relationship, called gougu after the names for the two legs (MacTutor).

What the Greeks added was proof. Around 300 BCE, Euclid’s Elements proved the theorem as Proposition 47 of Book I: “In right-angled triangles the square on the side opposite the right angle equals the sum of the squares on the sides containing the right angle.” Proposition 48, immediately after it, proves the converse (Euclid’s Elements, ed. D. E. Joyce, Clark University). Notice Euclid’s wording: “the square on the side” means an actual square drawn on that side. The theorem is about areas, which is exactly what our rearrangement proof shows.

The theorem’s most famous descendant

In 1637, Pierre de Fermat wrote in the margin of a book that while \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\) has infinitely many whole-number solutions, the equation \(a^n + b^n = c^n\) has none when \(n\) is greater than 2, and that he had a marvelous proof the margin was too narrow to contain. Mathematicians tried for more than 350 years to prove “Fermat’s Last Theorem.” Andrew Wiles finally did, in work published in 1995, and received the Abel Prize for it in 2016 (The Abel Prize). The Pythagorean theorem is where that story begins, and it is a fitting namesake for our Young Fermats middle school program.

Try it yourself: seven practice problems

These problems are new; none repeat the examples above. Sketch first, label the hypotenuse, circle the question, and only then tap “Answer.”

Problem 1 Find the hypotenuse

A phone screen is 9 centimeters wide and 12 centimeters tall. Screens are measured along the diagonal. What is this screen’s diagonal?

Answer

15 cm

Width and height meet at a right angle, so \(c = \sqrt{9^2 + 12^2} = \sqrt{81 + 144} = \sqrt{225} = 15\). This is 3-4-5 multiplied by 3.

Problem 2 Missing leg

Ava flies a kite on a 25-meter string, pulled straight. Her friend stands directly under the kite, 7 meters from Ava. Ignoring Ava’s height, how high is the kite?

Answer

24 m

“Directly under” gives a right angle at the friend’s feet. The string is the hypotenuse, so \(h = \sqrt{25^2 - 7^2} = \sqrt{625 - 49} = \sqrt{576} = 24\). This is the 7-24-25 triple.

Problem 3 Does it apply?

A triangle has sides 9, 12, and 16. Is it a right triangle? If not, is its largest angle acute or obtuse?

Answer

Not right; the largest angle is obtuse

\(9^2 + 12^2 = 81 + 144 = 225\), but \(16^2 = 256\). Since \(256 > 225\), the angle opposite the 16 side is obtuse. (A right triangle with legs 9 and 12 would need a hypotenuse of exactly 15.)

Problem 4 Coordinate distance

Find the distance between \(P(-3, -2)\) and \(Q(5, 4)\).

Answer

10

\(\Delta x = 5 - (-3) = 8\) and \(\Delta y = 4 - (-2) = 6\). So \(d = \sqrt{8^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{100} = 10\).

Problem 5 Three dimensions

A storage box measures 12 inches by 4 inches by 3 inches. What is the longest straight rod that fits inside?

Answer

13 in

Floor diagonal: \(\sqrt{12^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{160}\). Space diagonal: \(\sqrt{160 + 3^2} = \sqrt{169} = 13\). Or directly, \(\sqrt{144 + 16 + 9} = 13\). Keeping \(\sqrt{160}\) exact, instead of rounding, made the final answer come out clean.

Problem 6 Square diagonal

A baseball diamond is a square with 90-foot sides. How far is the throw from home plate to second base, to the nearest tenth of a foot?

Answer

About 127.3 ft

Home to second is the diagonal of the square: \(\sqrt{90^2 + 90^2} = 90\sqrt{2} \approx 127.3\). This is the 45°–45°–90° pattern: the hypotenuse is a leg times \(\sqrt{2}\).

Problem 7 Answer the real question

A square plaza is 60 feet on each side. How many feet do you save by walking diagonally across it instead of along two sides? Round to the nearest foot.

Answer

About 35 ft

Diagonal: \(60\sqrt{2} \approx 84.85\) feet. Two sides: 120 feet. Savings: \(120 - 84.85 \approx 35.15\), so about 35 feet. If you answered 85, you found the diagonal, not the savings.

What New York expects in eighth grade

Questions students and parents ask

How do you know when to use the Pythagorean theorem in a word problem?

Look for two lengths that meet at a right angle and a third length that connects their endpoints. Sketch the triangle, label the side opposite the right angle as the hypotenuse, and circle the length the question asks you to find. If there is no right angle, the theorem does not apply directly.

Which side is the hypotenuse?

The hypotenuse is the side opposite the right angle. It is always the longest side of a right triangle. In \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\), it is always \(c\).

When do I add and when do I subtract?

Add the squares when you are finding the hypotenuse: \(c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\). Subtract when you know the hypotenuse and are finding a leg: \(a = \sqrt{c^2 - b^2}\). Ask yourself whether the length you want is the longest side.

Does the Pythagorean theorem work for every triangle?

No. It is true only for right triangles. You can use its converse to test whether a triangle is right: if \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\) for the longest side \(c\), the triangle is right. If \(c^2\) is larger, the triangle is obtuse; if smaller, it is acute.

Did Pythagoras discover the Pythagorean theorem?

Probably not. Babylonian tablets such as Plimpton 322, now at Columbia University, show that the relationship was known more than a thousand years before Pythagoras, and the idea also appears in ancient India and China. There is no surviving evidence of a proof by Pythagoras; the earliest surviving Greek proof is in Euclid’s Elements, Book I, Proposition 47.

What grade learns the Pythagorean theorem in New York?

Eighth grade. New York’s Grade 8 standards NY-8.G.6, NY-8.G.7, and NY-8.G.8 cover understanding a proof and its converse, finding unknown side lengths in two and three dimensions, and finding distances on a coordinate plane.
