Theory refresher: the four R&W skill domains

Craft & Structure

These items test how a text is built and the flavor of its language. Words-in-Context questions ask you to slot the meaning that fits the sentence around it — substitute your own word first, then match to a choice. Text Structure & Purpose asks what a sentence, paragraph, or whole text is doing. Cross-Text Connections require you to compare how two texts frame the same idea; label each author’s stance first, then predict the interaction.

Information & Ideas

Read for the claim and the evidence attached to it. Central Ideas & Details is the main-idea family: pick the choice that captures the whole paragraph, not just a slice. Command of Evidence — textual or quantitative — asks which choice best supports (or in the graph version, best matches the data behind) the claim. Inferences reward the choice that must be true if the passage is true; discard anything the text does not license.

Standard English Conventions

Punctuation and grammar rules, tested by tight sentence edits. Boundaries covers commas, semicolons, colons, and dashes between clauses and around modifiers. Form/Structure/Sense covers subject-verb agreement, pronoun-antecedent agreement, verb tense, and modifier placement. Read what comes before and after the blank — the choice that respects both sides wins.

Expression of Ideas

Transitions questions test the logical relationship between two sentences: contrast, addition, cause, example, sequence. Predict the relationship in your own words before you look at the choices. Rhetorical Synthesis gives you a bullet list of notes and a stated goal — the correct choice is the one that uses only supplied facts to hit that specific goal (introduce, emphasize, compare, and so on).

An inference item followed by a run of conventions questions: relative-clause verbs, subject-verb and pronoun-antecedent agreement, and comma / colon boundaries around supplementary elements.

Question 18 · Information & Ideas · Inferences

Claim 1: veterans are overrepresented in civilian government jobs. Claim 2: shared organizational structures between the military and government may explain the overrepresentation. Question 19 · Standard English Conventions · Form/Structure/Sense (finite verb in a relative clause)

Open Question 19 in the College Board PDF above to read the full item.

Answer: A

Key idea. A relative clause needs a finite verb — a verb that can stand as the main verb of the clause.

Scan the choices: the question is testing verb form. The relative clause needs a full, tense-marked verb (not a participle alone). “Provided” is a past-tense finite verb that fits the clause. Non-finite forms (“providing,” “to provide,” etc.) can’t serve as the clause’s main verb here.

Why this works. Only a finite verb can head a relative clause, and the past-tense “provided” supplies it correctly.

Question 20 · Standard English Conventions · Form/Structure/Sense (subject-verb agreement)

Open Question 20 in the College Board PDF above to read the full item.

Answer: A

Key idea. Find the true grammatical subject, then match verb number.

Ignore the intervening prepositional phrases; find the head noun. The subject is singular (“landing”). A singular subject takes a singular verb — “allows.” Plural verbs are incorrect against a singular subject.

Why this works. The head noun of the subject is singular, so it demands the singular verb “allows.”

Question 21 · Standard English Conventions · Form/Structure/Sense (pronoun-antecedent agreement)

Open Question 21 in the College Board PDF above to read the full item.

Answer: B

Key idea. Find the antecedent, then choose a pronoun that matches in number.

The nearby noun the pronoun refers to (“woodcuts”) is plural. A plural antecedent requires a plural pronoun. “They” is plural and unambiguous here. Singular pronouns (“it,” “this”) fail number agreement.

Why this works. Only “they” agrees with the plural antecedent “woodcuts,” making choice B the only conventional fit.

Question 22 · Standard English Conventions · Boundaries (paired commas around interrupters)

Open Question 22 in the College Board PDF above to read the full item.

Answer: C

Key idea. Nonrestrictive supplementary elements — adverbs like “however” and participial phrases — must be set off with commas on both sides.

Sentence structure: main clause, “however,” participial phrase, rest of clause. “However” is a supplementary adverb interrupting the clause — needs commas on BOTH sides. The participial phrase that follows is also supplementary — needs a closing comma. Only choice C places all three commas correctly.

Why this works. The two interrupting elements each need paired commas; only choice C supplies them without dropping or misplacing any.

Question 23 · Standard English Conventions · Boundaries (title with a proper name)

Open Question 23 in the College Board PDF above to read the full item.

Answer: C

Key idea. When a title functions restrictively with a proper name (“plant cell biologist Yuree Lee”), no comma is needed between them.

The title identifies which person — restrictive use. Restrictive elements do NOT take commas. Choice C omits the comma between title and name. Any comma-inserting choice would incorrectly treat the name as nonrestrictive.

Why this works. The title-plus-name is a restrictive appositive that requires no punctuation, matching only choice C.

Question 24 · Standard English Conventions · Boundaries (colon between two clauses)

Open Question 24 in the College Board PDF above to read the full item.

Answer: C

Key idea. A colon can join two independent clauses when the second elaborates on the first.

Both parts of the sentence are full independent clauses. The second clause spells out what the first summarized. The correct mark is a colon (elaboration between two clauses). Choice C uses the colon; commas alone would be a splice and periods would break the elaboration signal.

Why this works. Only a colon signals “here comes the elaboration” between two clauses; that’s exactly what choice C supplies.

Question 25 · Standard English Conventions · Form/Structure/Sense (participial phrase)

Open Question 25 in the College Board PDF above to read the full item.

Answer: A

Key idea. A restrictive present participle modifying the subject noun is punctuated tightly to the noun.

The phrase “suggesting...” is essential to identify which hypothesis is under discussion. Restrictive participial phrases do NOT get commas. The non-finite “suggesting” is the right form; a finite verb would create two verbs for one subject. Choice A supplies the non-finite “suggesting” with no separating punctuation.

Why this works. Only choice A uses the non-finite participle to form a restrictive modifier of the subject — the conventional shape here.

Digital SAT Practice Test 9 Reading & Writing Module 1 FAQ

What does this Practice Test 9 Reading & Writing Module 1 walkthrough cover?

It covers all 33 questions in Reading & Writing Module 1 of Digital SAT Practice Test 9. Every item has the official College Board answer, a classified skill tag (Craft & Structure, Information & Ideas, Standard English Conventions, or Expression of Ideas), and an original SOMATH-written explanation of the rule and elimination logic.

What are the four Reading & Writing question categories?

The Digital SAT R&W section tests four skill domains: Craft & Structure (words in context, text structure & purpose, cross-text connections), Information & Ideas (central ideas & details, command of evidence, inferences), Standard English Conventions (boundaries and form/structure/sense), and Expression of Ideas (transitions and rhetorical synthesis).

Is Module 1 the same difficulty for every student?

Yes. Module 1 is the fixed-form section that every test taker sees. Performance on Module 1 determines whether the student sees the easier or harder version of Module 2, which is the adaptive section.

What is the key idea in Question 9?

Text 2 (Focarelli & Panetta) argues that merger research overweights short-term effects and that mergers can be economically beneficial over the long term. Text 1 (Fan) reports a short-term price rise after a merger. F&P would respond by pushing back with the long-term-benefit frame — choice C.

What is the key idea in Question 17?

The passage states that the best judicial opinions consider and rebut objections, but also that judges usually cite philosophers who agree with them. The logical inference is that judges could strengthen their opinions by including philosophers who disagree — choice B.

What is the key idea in Question 22?

The sentence has two supplementary elements (an interrupting adverb 'however' and a following participial phrase) inside a main clause. Each needs paired commas — choice C is the only choice that punctuates all three commas correctly.
