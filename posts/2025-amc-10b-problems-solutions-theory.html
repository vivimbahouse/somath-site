Whole-number quotientsProblem 1

The instructions on a 350-gram bag of coffee beans say that proper brewing of a large mug of pour-over coffee requires 20 grams of coffee beans. What is the greatest number of properly brewed large mugs of coffee that can be made from the coffee beans in that bag?
  • (A) 16
  • (B) 17
  • (C) 18
  • (D) 19
  • (E) 20

The theory involved

Division tells us how many equal groups fit into a total. When the question requires complete groups, use the integer part of the quotient rather than rounding to the nearest whole number.

Answer

Answer B: 17.

The SOMATH solution

  1. Let \(m\) be the number of full mugs. Each needs 20 grams, so the available beans impose \(20m\le350\).
  2. Divide by 20: \(m\le17.5\). Because \(m\) counts complete mugs, its greatest possible value is 17.
  3. Check both sides of the boundary: 17 mugs use \(17(20)=340\) grams, leaving 10 grams. Eighteen mugs would need 360 grams, which exceeds the bag.
  4. The answer is therefore 17 mugs. The remaining beans cannot make another properly brewed large mug.

What to remember: Ask what the remainder means. A leftover half-portion is not permission to round up when every serving must meet the stated requirement.

Repeating units-digit cyclesProblem 2

Jerry wrote down the ones digit of each of the first 2025 positive squares: \(1,4,9,6,5,6,\ldots\). What is the sum of all the numbers Jerry wrote down?
  • (A) 9025
  • (B) 9070
  • (C) 9090
  • (D) 9115
  • (E) 9160

The theory involved

A square's units digit depends only on its base's units digit. Work modulo 10, find a complete repeating block, and handle the final incomplete block separately.

Answer

Answer D: 9115.

The SOMATH solution

  1. The squares of numbers ending in \(1,2,\ldots,9,0\) end in \(1,4,9,6,5,6,9,4,1,0\). This ten-term block repeats because \((n+10)^2-n^2=20n+100\) is divisible by 10.
  2. Add one block: \(1+4+9+6+5+6+9+4+1+0=45\).
  3. Since \(2025=202(10)+5\), there are 202 complete blocks followed by the first five entries of another block. Those five contribute \(1+4+9+6+5=25\).
  4. Thus the requested sum is \(202(45)+25=9090+25=\boxed{9115}\). We are adding the units digits, not finding the units digit of the sum of the squares.

What to remember: Separate the full cycles from the leftover terms before calculating. A correct cycle sum still gives the wrong answer if the last five entries are omitted.

Pascal-type row sumsProblem 3

A Pascal-like triangle has 10 as the top row and 10 followed by 1 as the second row. In each subsequent row the first number is 10, the last number is 1, and as in the standard Pascal's Triangle, each other number in the row is the sum of the two numbers directly above it. The first four rows are shown below: \[\begin{array}{rrrrrrr}&&&10&&&\\&&10&&1&&\\&10&&11&&1&\\10&&21&&12&&1\end{array}\] What is the sum of the digits of the sum of the numbers in the 11th row?
  • (A) 11
  • (B) 13
  • (C) 14
  • (D) 16
  • (E) 17

The theory involved

When neighboring entries are added, it is often easier to follow the total of a row than its individual entries. Count how often each old entry contributes to the next row.

Answer

Answer D: 16.

The SOMATH solution

  1. The second row totals \(10+1=11\). From this row onward, every interior entry contributes to two neighboring sums in the next row.
  2. Each endpoint also contributes twice: once to the new endpoint and once to the neighboring interior sum. Thus every row sum doubles after row 2. For example, rows 3 and 4 total 22 and 44.
  3. Going from row 2 to row 11 takes nine doublings, not ten. The eleventh row therefore has sum \(11\cdot2^9=11\cdot512=5632\).
  4. The question asks for the digit sum of that total: \(5+6+3+2=\boxed{16}\). There is no need to construct the full eleventh row.

What to remember: A nonstandard first row can break a familiar formula. Start the recurrence at the first row where the actual doubling rule holds.

Place value in different basesProblem 4

The value of the two-digit number \(\overline{ab}\) in base seven equals the value of the two-digit number \(\overline{ba}\) in base nine. What is \(a+b\)?
  • (A) 7
  • (B) 9
  • (C) 10
  • (D) 11
  • (E) 14

The theory involved

In base \(b\), a two-digit numeral with digits \(u,v\) represents \(bu+v\). Digits must be smaller than the base, and the leading digit of a two-digit numeral cannot be zero.

Answer

Answer A: 7.

The SOMATH solution

  1. Translate both numerals into ordinary values: \(\overline{ab}_7=7a+b\) and \(\overline{ba}_9=9b+a\).
  2. Equating them gives \(7a+b=9b+a\), so \(6a=8b\), or \(3a=4b\). Since 3 and 4 are coprime, write \(a=4k\) and \(b=3k\).
  3. Both digits occur in the base-seven number, so \(a,b\le6\). Both are positive because each is a leading digit in one numeral. Only \(k=1\) works: \(a=4,b=3\).
  4. Then \(a+b=\boxed{7}\). Check the values: \(43_7=28+3=31\), while \(34_9=27+4=31\).

What to remember: An equation involving digits needs digit restrictions. Without the allowed ranges, the equation has many integer solutions that are not valid numerals.

Inscribed and central anglesProblem 5

In triangle \(ABC\), \(AB=10\), \(AC=18\), and \(\angle B=130^\circ\). Let \(O\) be the center of the circle containing \(A,B,\) and \(C\). What is the degree measure of \(\angle CAO\)?
  • (A) 20
  • (B) 30
  • (C) 40
  • (D) 50
  • (E) 60

The theory involved

An inscribed angle is half its intercepted arc. Two radii form an isosceles triangle, so a central angle determines the two equal base angles.

Answer

Answer C: 40.

The SOMATH solution

  1. The inscribed angle \(\angle ABC=130^\circ\) intercepts the arc from \(A\) to \(C\) not containing \(B\). That arc measures \(2(130^\circ)=260^\circ\), so it is the major arc.
  2. The other, minor arc \(AC\) measures \(360^\circ-260^\circ=100^\circ\). Therefore the smaller central angle \(\angle AOC\) is \(100^\circ\).
  3. Since \(OA=OC\), triangle \(AOC\) is isosceles. Its base angles are each \((180^\circ-100^\circ)/2=40^\circ\).
  4. Thus \(\angle CAO=\boxed{40^\circ}\). The side lengths 10 and 18 are not needed for this angle calculation.

What to remember: An obtuse inscribed angle intercepts a major arc. Do not put the reflex \(260^\circ\) angle inside triangle \(AOC\).

Splitting a trapezoid's areaProblem 6

The line \(y=\frac13x+1\) divides the square region defined by \(0\le x\le2\) and \(0\le y\le2\) into an upper and lower region. The line \(x=a\) divides the lower region into two regions of equal area. Then \(a\) can be written as \(\sqrt{s}-t\), where \(s\) and \(t\) are positive integers. What is \(s+t\)?
  • (A) 18
  • (B) 19
  • (C) 20
  • (D) 21
  • (E) 22

The theory involved

A trapezoid has area equal to its width times the average of its parallel side lengths. A vertical cut under a sloping line changes both the width and one height; equal widths need not give equal areas.

Answer

Answer C: 20.

The SOMATH solution

  1. At \(x=0\), the lower region has height 1. At \(x=2\), its height is \(1+\frac23=\frac53\). Its total area is \(2(1+\frac53)/2=\frac83\).
  2. Each desired piece must have area \(\frac43\). The piece between \(x=0\) and \(x=a\) is a trapezoid with width \(a\) and vertical heights 1 and \(1+\frac a3\).
  3. Its area is \(\frac a2(2+\frac a3)=a+\frac{a^2}{6}\). Set this equal to \(\frac43\): \(a^2+6a-8=0\), or \((a+3)^2=17\).
  4. The solution inside the square is \(a=\sqrt{17}-3\); the other root is negative. Thus \(s=17,t=3\), giving \(\boxed{20}\).

What to remember: Use area geometry before calculus. A trapezoid formula gives the exact equation with only algebra.

Equal paths and the Pythagorean theoremProblem 7

Frances stands 15 meters directly south of a locked gate in a fence that runs east-west. Immediately behind the fence is a box of chocolates, located \(x\) meters east of the locked gate. An unlocked gate lies 9 meters east of the box, and another unlocked gate lies 8 meters west of the locked gate. Frances can reach the box by walking toward an unlocked gate, passing through it, and walking toward the box. It happens that the total distance Frances would travel is the same via either unlocked gate. What is the value of \(x\)?
  • (A) \(3\frac27\)
  • (B) \(3\frac37\)
  • (C) \(3\frac47\)
  • (D) \(3\frac57\)
  • (E) \(3\frac67\)

The theory involved

Represent the fence as a horizontal line. A diagonal walk to a gate is the hypotenuse of a right triangle, while the walk along the fence contributes an additional linear distance.

Answer

Answer C: \(3\frac47\).

The SOMATH solution

  1. Place the locked gate at \((0,0)\), Frances at \((0,-15)\), and the box at \((x,0)\). The west gate is \((-8,0)\), and the east gate is \((x+9,0)\).
  2. Via the west gate, the diagonal distance is \(\sqrt{8^2+15^2}=17\). The remaining distance is \(x+8\), so the whole route is \(x+25\).
  3. Via the east gate, the diagonal is \(\sqrt{(x+9)^2+225}\), followed by 9 meters. Equate routes and subtract 9: \(\sqrt{(x+9)^2+225}=x+16\).
  4. Squaring gives \(x^2+18x+306=x^2+32x+256\). Hence \(14x=50\), so \(x=\frac{25}{7}=3\frac47\). Both sides before squaring are positive, so this solution is valid.

What to remember: Count both pieces of each route. Equating only the two diagonal distances ignores the unequal walks from the gates to the box.

Divisibility and digit patternsProblem 8

Emmy says to Max, "I ordered 36 math club sweatshirts today." Max asks, "How much did each shirt cost?" Emmy responds, "I'll give you a hint. The total cost was \(\$\underline{A}\,\underline{B}\,\underline{B}.\underline{B}\,\underline{A}\), where \(A\) and \(B\) are digits and \(A\ne0\)." After a pause, Max says, "That was a good price." What is \(A+B\)?
  • (A) 7
  • (B) 8
  • (C) 11
  • (D) 14
  • (E) 15

The theory involved

Convert dollars to integer cents before applying divisibility. Since \(36=4\cdot9\) and the factors are coprime, a number is divisible by 36 precisely when it is divisible by both 4 and 9.

Answer

Answer C: 11.

The SOMATH solution

  1. Use the intended equal-price, whole-cent interpretation for the shirts. The total in cents is the five-digit integer \(\overline{ABBBA}=10001A+1110B\), which must be divisible by 36.
  2. Divisibility by 9 requires \(2A+3B\equiv0\pmod9\), so \(A\) is a multiple of 3. Divisibility by 4 requires the last two digits, \(10B+A\), to be divisible by 4, so \(A\) is even.
  3. The only nonzero digit both even and divisible by 3 is \(A=6\). Now \(12+3B\) must be divisible by 9, giving \(B=2,5,\) or 8. Also \(10B+6\) must be divisible by 4, which requires \(B\) odd. Hence \(B=5\).
  4. The total is $655.56, and \(65556/36=1821\) cents per shirt, or $18.21. Therefore \(A+B=6+5=\boxed{11}\).

What to remember: State the money model: an equal unit price in whole cents is what turns this dialogue into a divisibility problem.

Counting integer triples by their sumProblem 9

How many ordered triples of integers \((x,y,z)\) satisfy the following system of inequalities? \[\begin{aligned}-x-y-z&\le-2\\-x+y+z&\le2\\x-y+z&\le2\\x+y-z&\le2\end{aligned}\]
  • (A) 4
  • (B) 8
  • (C) 11
  • (D) 15
  • (E) 17

The theory involved

When inequalities are symmetric, introducing a total can reduce a three-variable problem to a few cases. For nonnegative integer triples totaling \(m\), stars and bars gives \(\binom{m+2}{2}\).

Answer

Answer C: 11.

The SOMATH solution

  1. Set \(S=x+y+z\). The first inequality gives \(S\ge2\). Adding the last three gives \(S\le6\), so \(S\) is one of \(2,3,4,5,6\).
  2. The last three inequalities say \(S-2x\le2\), \(S-2y\le2\), and \(S-2z\le2\). Therefore each coordinate is at least \(\lceil(S-2)/2\rceil\). This also proves the coordinates are nonnegative.
  3. For \(S=2\), the lower bound is 0 and there are \(\binom42=6\) triples. For \(S=3\), all coordinates are at least 1, giving only \((1,1,1)\). For \(S=4\), subtract 1 from each coordinate; the remaining total is 1, giving 3 triples.
  4. For \(S=5\), every coordinate would be at least 2, impossible for a total of 5. For \(S=6\), only \((2,2,2)\) works. Total: \(6+1+3+0+1=\boxed{11}\).

What to remember: A bound on the sum and a bound on each coordinate together make an infinite-looking integer search finite.

Factoring with domain restrictionsProblem 10

Let \(f(n)=n^3-5n^2+2n+8\) and \(g(n)=n^3-6n^2+5n+12\). What is the sum of all integers \(n\) such that \(\frac{f(n)}{g(n)}\) is an integer?
  • (A) 2
  • (B) 3
  • (C) 4
  • (D) 5
  • (E) 6

The theory involved

Factor rational expressions, but preserve every restriction from the original denominator. If an integer plus a unit fraction is an integer, that fraction's integer denominator must be \(1\) or \(-1\).

Answer

Answer A: 2.

The SOMATH solution

  1. Factor the two cubics: \[\begin{aligned}f(n)&=(n-4)(n-2)(n+1),\\g(n)&=(n-4)(n-3)(n+1).\end{aligned}\] The original quotient is undefined at \(n=-1,3,4\).
  2. For all other \(n\), cancel common factors to obtain \(\frac{n-2}{n-3}=1+\frac1{n-3}\).
  3. For this to be an integer, \(n-3\) must divide 1. Thus \(n-3=1\) or \(-1\), giving candidates \(n=4\) and \(n=2\).
  4. Reject \(n=4\), since the original denominator vanishes there. At \(n=2\), the numerator is zero and the denominator is nonzero, so the quotient is the integer 0. The sum of the valid integers is \(\boxed{2}\).

What to remember: Canceling a factor simplifies values where the expression is defined; it does not fill a hole in the original domain.

Exactly two fixed pointsProblem 11

On Monday, 6 students went to the tutoring center at the same time, and each one was randomly assigned to one of the 6 tutors on duty. On Tuesday, the same 6 students showed up, the same 6 tutors were on duty, and the students were again randomly assigned to the tutors. What is the probability that exactly 2 students met with the same tutor both Monday and Tuesday?
  • (A) \(\frac1{16}\)
  • (B) \(\frac3{16}\)
  • (C) \(\frac14\)
  • (D) \(\frac38\)
  • (E) \(\frac12\)

The theory involved

Treat each day's assignment as a one-to-one matching between students and tutors, as intended by the contest. A derangement is a permutation in which nobody keeps their original match.

Answer

Answer B: \(\frac3{16}\).

The SOMATH solution

  1. Fix Monday's matching as the reference. On Tuesday there are \(6!=720\) equally likely one-to-one assignments.
  2. Choose the two students keeping their tutors in \(\binom62=15\) ways. The remaining four must all change tutors; otherwise more than two students would keep their Monday tutors.
  3. Count derangements of four by inclusion-exclusion: \[D_4=4!-\binom413!+\binom422!-\binom431!+1.\] Equivalently, \(D_4=24-24+12-4+1=9\). There are \(15\cdot9=135\) favorable assignments.
  4. The probability is \(\frac{135}{720}=\boxed{\frac3{16}}\). A binomial model with independent \(1/6\) matches is inappropriate because two students cannot have the same tutor in this matching.

What to remember: The phrase 'exactly two' means the other four must not match. Choosing the two and freely permuting the rest would overcount.

Comparing circle-packing densitiesProblem 12

The figure below shows an equilateral triangle, a rhombus with a \(60^\circ\) angle, and a regular hexagon, each of them containing some mutually tangent congruent disks. Let \(T,R,\) and \(H\), respectively, denote the ratio in each case of the total area of the disks to the area of the enclosing polygon. Which of the following is true?
  • (A) \(T=H=R\)
  • (B) \(H<R=T\)
  • (C) \(H=R<T\)
  • (D) \(H<R<T\)
  • (E) \(H<T<R\)

The theory involved

Area ratios do not change under scaling, so set every circle's radius to 1. Tangency, apothems, and \(30\!-\!60\!-\!90^\circ\) triangles then determine the polygons.

Answer

Answer C: \(H=R<T\).

The SOMATH solution

  1. In the equilateral triangle, the two bottom circle centers are 2 apart. Each center is \(\sqrt3\) horizontally from the nearest base vertex: its height is 1 and it lies on a \(30^\circ\) angle bisector. The triangle's side is \(2+2\sqrt3\), so its area is \(6+4\sqrt3\). Thus \(T=\frac{3\pi}{6+4\sqrt3}\).
  2. In the rhombus, the circle centers lie on the long diagonal. Each is 2 from its nearest \(60^\circ\) vertex because \(1/\sin30^\circ=2\). Their separation is 2, making the long diagonal 6. The short diagonal is \(6/\sqrt3=2\sqrt3\). The rhombus area is \(6\sqrt3\), so \(R=\frac{2\pi}{6\sqrt3}=\frac{\pi}{3\sqrt3}\).
  3. For the hexagon, the six centers form a regular hexagon of side 2 and circumradius 2. Each outer side is another radius away in the radial direction, so the enclosing hexagon's apothem is 3. Its side is \(2\sqrt3\), and its area is \(\frac12(12\sqrt3)(3)=18\sqrt3\). Therefore \(H=\frac{6\pi}{18\sqrt3}=\frac{\pi}{3\sqrt3}=R\).
  4. Finally, \(T>R\) is equivalent to \(9\sqrt3>6+4\sqrt3\), or \(5\sqrt3>6\), which is true. Hence \(\boxed{H=R<T}\).

What to remember: More circles do not automatically mean a higher covered fraction. Compare disk area with the enclosing area, using the same radius throughout.

An altitude crossed by a medianProblem 13

The altitude to the hypotenuse of a \(30\!-\!60\!-\!90^\circ\) right triangle is divided into two segments of lengths \(x<y\) by the median to the shortest side of the triangle. What is the ratio \(\frac{x}{x+y}\)?
  • (A) \(\frac37\)
  • (B) \(\frac{\sqrt3}{4}\)
  • (C) \(\frac49\)
  • (D) \(\frac5{11}\)
  • (E) \(\frac{4\sqrt3}{15}\)

The theory involved

A \(30\!-\!60\!-\!90^\circ\) triangle has side ratio \(1:\sqrt3:2\). Coordinates along the two perpendicular legs turn the altitude and median into simple linear equations.

Answer

Answer A: \(\frac37\).

The SOMATH solution

  1. Put the right-angle vertex at \(O=(0,0)\), the long-leg endpoint at \(A=(\sqrt3,0)\), and the short-leg endpoint at \(B=(0,1)\). The hypotenuse has equation \(y=1-x/\sqrt3\). Here coordinate letters are separate from the segment lengths in the question.
  2. The altitude from \(O\) is perpendicular to that hypotenuse, so its equation is \(y=\sqrt3x\). It reaches the hypotenuse at \(H=(\sqrt3/4,3/4)\).
  3. The median to the shortest side joins \(A\) to the midpoint \((0,1/2)\) of \(OB\). Its equation is \(y=1/2-x/(2\sqrt3)\). Intersecting it with \(y=\sqrt3x\) gives \(P=(\sqrt3/7,3/7)\).
  4. Because \(P\) and \(H\) lie on the same ray from \(O\), \(OP/OH=(3/7)/(3/4)=4/7\). The other segment is \(PH/OH=3/7\), which is the smaller fraction. Thus \(\frac{x}{x+y}=\boxed{\frac37}\).

What to remember: The intersection divides the altitude into two fractions. Use the smaller one because the question explicitly states \(x<y\).

Three captains, three colorsProblem 14

Nine athletes, no two of whom are the same height, try out for the basketball team. One at a time, they draw a wristband at random, without replacement, from a bag containing 3 blue bands, 3 red bands, and 3 green bands. They are divided into a blue group, a red group, and a green group. The tallest member of each group is named the group captain. What is the probability that the group captains are the three tallest athletes?
  • (A) \(\frac29\)
  • (B) \(\frac27\)
  • (C) \(\frac9{28}\)
  • (D) \(\frac13\)
  • (E) \(\frac38\)

The theory involved

Use symmetry to expose only the draws that matter. The three tallest athletes are exactly the three captains if and only if they receive three different colors.

Answer

Answer C: \(\frac9{28}\).

The SOMATH solution

  1. Imagine the tallest athletes draw first. This does not change the random allocation of the wristbands, because the original draws are without replacement and all assignments are equally likely.
  2. The tallest athlete may draw any color. Of the 8 bands remaining, 6 have a different color, so the second-tallest has probability \(6/8\) of avoiding the first color.
  3. Given different first two colors, all 3 bands of the third color remain among 7 bands. The third-tallest has probability \(3/7\) of getting that color.
  4. Multiply: \(\frac68\cdot\frac37=\boxed{\frac9{28}}\). With one of the top three in each group, no shorter athlete can displace them as captains.

What to remember: Replace the full team assignment with a necessary-and-sufficient condition on the three people who matter.

Telescoping a rational seriesProblem 15

The sum \[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac1{k^3+6k^2+8k}\] can be expressed as \(\frac ab\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are relatively prime positive integers. What is \(a+b\)?
  • (A) 89
  • (B) 97
  • (C) 102
  • (D) 107
  • (E) 129

The theory involved

Factoring a denominator may reveal a telescoping decomposition. Prove cancellation in a finite partial sum first; then let its leftover tail tend to zero.

Answer

Answer D: 107.

The SOMATH solution

  1. Factor \(k^3+6k^2+8k=k(k+2)(k+4)\). The evenly spaced factors suggest \[\begin{aligned}\frac1{k(k+2)(k+4)}\\=\frac18\left(\frac1k-\frac2{k+2}+\frac1{k+4}\right).\end{aligned}\] Combining the right side over a common denominator verifies the numerator is 1.
  2. In the sum through \(k=N\), the middle terms cancel because their coefficients are \(1-2+1=0\). The beginning leaves \(1+\frac12-\frac13-\frac14\).
  3. The ending terms are \(-\frac1{N+1}-\frac1{N+2}+\frac1{N+3}+\frac1{N+4}\), all multiplied by \(1/8\). They approach zero as \(N\) grows.
  4. The infinite sum is therefore \(\frac18(1+\frac12-\frac13-\frac14)=\frac18\cdot\frac{11}{12}=\frac{11}{96}\). The numerator and denominator are coprime, so \(a+b=11+96=\boxed{107}\).

What to remember: Do not subtract divergent infinite sums separately. Cancellation is justified inside a finite partial sum before taking the limit.

Coloring a cycle with repeated colorsProblem 16

A circle has been divided into 6 sectors of different sizes. Then 2 of the sectors are painted red, 2 painted green, and 2 painted blue so that no two neighboring sectors are painted the same color. One such coloring is shown below. How many different colorings are possible?
  • (A) 12
  • (B) 16
  • (C) 18
  • (D) 24
  • (E) 28

The theory involved

Treat the unequal sectors as six distinguishable positions around a cycle. Classify the possible positions of one repeated color, then fill the gaps without placing equal colors next to each other.

Answer

Answer D: 24.

The SOMATH solution

  1. Label the six sectors in cyclic order. The two red sectors cannot be adjacent. Their shorter cyclic separation is either two steps or three steps (opposite). There are 6 pairs of the first kind and 3 opposite pairs.
  2. For a two-step red pair, there is one sector between the reds on one side and a run of three on the other. The isolated sector is either blue or green. The three-sector run must alternate and use the remaining color counts, giving exactly one completion for each choice: 2 completions per red pair.
  3. For an opposite red pair, the remaining sectors form two runs of length two. Each run must be blue-green or green-blue, independently. That gives \(2\cdot2=4\) completions per opposite pair.
  4. The total is \(6(2)+3(4)=12+12=\boxed{24}\). We do not divide by rotations: the sectors have different sizes, so their positions are distinguishable.

What to remember: Whether rotations count as the same is part of the problem's model. Here the unequal sector sizes fix the positions.

Recovering terms from averagesProblem 17

Consider a decreasing sequence of \(n\) positive integers \(x_1>x_2>\cdots>x_n\) that satisfies the following conditions: The average of the first 3 terms in the sequence is 2025. For all \(4\le k\le n\), the average of the first \(k\) terms is 1 less than the average of the first \(k-1\) terms. What is the greatest possible value of \(n\)?
  • (A) 1013
  • (B) 1014
  • (C) 1016
  • (D) 2016
  • (E) 2025

The theory involved

Average times number of terms gives a partial sum. Subtract consecutive partial sums to recover an individual term. A greatest-possible answer needs both an upper bound and an example attaining it.

Answer

Answer B: 1014.

The SOMATH solution

  1. Let \(A_k\) be the average of the first \(k\) terms. Starting from \(A_3=2025\), the rule gives \(A_k=2028-k\) for \(k\ge3\).
  2. The partial sum is \(S_k=kA_k=2028k-k^2\). For \(k\ge4\), \[x_k=S_k-S_{k-1}=2029-2k.\] Thus \(x_4=2021,x_5=2019,\ldots\), decreasing by 2 each time.
  3. Positivity requires \(2029-2n\ge1\), so \(n\le1014\). At \(n=1014\), the final term is 1; the next term would be negative.
  4. To show the bound is attainable, choose \(x_1=2026,x_2=2025,x_3=2024\), followed by \(2021,2019,\ldots,1\). The first three average 2025, the sequence strictly decreases, and the partial sums satisfy the required rule. The maximum is \(\boxed{1014}\).

What to remember: A changing average controls the next term through a difference of totals, not through a difference of averages alone.

Grouping floor values between squaresProblem 18

What is the ones digit of the sum \[\lfloor\sqrt1\rfloor+\lfloor\sqrt2\rfloor+\lfloor\sqrt3\rfloor+\cdots+\lfloor\sqrt{2025}\rfloor?\] (Recall that \(\lfloor x\rfloor\) represents the greatest integer less than or equal to \(x\).)
  • (A) 1
  • (B) 2
  • (C) 3
  • (D) 5
  • (E) 8

The theory involved

The floor of a square root stays constant between consecutive perfect squares. Count how many inputs give each output before adding.

Answer

Answer D: 5.

The SOMATH solution

  1. For each integer \(j\), the value \(\lfloor\sqrt{k}\rfloor=j\) occurs for \(k=j^2,j^2+1,\ldots,(j+1)^2-1\). There are \((j+1)^2-j^2=2j+1\) such inputs.
  2. Because \(2025=45^2\), the blocks for \(j=1,\ldots,44\) are complete, and the final value 45 occurs once. The sum is \(\sum_{j=1}^{44}j(2j+1)+45\).
  3. Use \(\sum j=44\cdot45/2=990\) and \(\sum j^2=44\cdot45\cdot89/6=29370\). Thus the total is \(2(29370)+990+45=59775\).
  4. Its ones digit is \(\boxed5\). The isolated final term is essential: the entire block of 45s has not appeared yet.

What to remember: Check whether the last perfect-square block is complete. At an exact square, only its first term has arrived.

Similar pyramids and filling timesProblem 19

A container has a \(1\times1\) square bottom, a \(3\times3\) open square top, and four congruent trapezoidal sides, as shown. Starting when the container is empty, a hose that runs water at a constant rate takes 35 minutes to fill the container up to the midline of the trapezoids. How many more minutes will it take to fill the remainder of the container?
  • (A) 70
  • (B) 85
  • (C) 90
  • (D) 95
  • (E) 105

The theory involved

The container is a square-pyramid frustum. Extend its sides to a common apex: similar pyramids have volumes proportional to the cubes of corresponding side lengths. Constant flow makes time proportional to volume.

Answer

Answer D: 95.

The SOMATH solution

  1. At the trapezoid midlines, the horizontal square cross-section has side \((1+3)/2=2\). Extend the container's sides down to a virtual pyramid apex.
  2. The pyramids from that apex to the bottom, middle, and top cross-sections have linear scales \(1:2:3\). Their volumes are therefore in ratio \(1:8:27\).
  3. The lower half of the container has relative volume \(8-1=7\). The upper half has relative volume \(27-8=19\). Although the heights are equal, the upper half holds more water.
  4. If 7 volume units take 35 minutes, each unit takes 5 minutes. The remaining 19 units take \(19(5)=\boxed{95}\) minutes. This is the additional time, not the 130-minute total.

What to remember: For similar solids, cube the length scale. Equal changes in height do not produce equal volumes when the container widens.

Tangent circles and radical factorizationProblem 20

Four congruent semicircles are inscribed in a square of side length 1 so that their diameters are on the sides of the square, one endpoint of each diameter is at a vertex of the square, and adjacent semicircles are tangent to each other. A small circle centered at the center of the square is tangent to each of the four semicircles, as shown below. The diameter of the small circle can be written as \((\sqrt a+b)(\sqrt c+d)\), where \(a,b,c,\) and \(d\) are integers. What is \(a+b+c+d\)?
  • (A) 3
  • (B) 5
  • (C) 8
  • (D) 9
  • (E) 11

The theory involved

For externally tangent circles, the distance between centers equals the sum of their radii. Coordinates convert two tangency conditions into equations; a radical expression may then factor unexpectedly.

Answer

Answer A: 3.

The SOMATH solution

  1. Let each semicircle have radius \(r\). Put the square at \([0,1]\times[0,1]\), with the bottom semicircle centered at \((r,0)\) and the right one at \((1,r)\). Their tangency gives \((1-r)^2+r^2=(2r)^2\).
  2. Simplify to \(2r^2+2r-1=0\). The positive root is \(r=(\sqrt3-1)/2\). Let the small circle's radius be \(\rho\) and its center be \((1/2,1/2)\).
  3. The distance to \((r,0)\) is \(r+\rho\), so \[r+\rho=\sqrt{(r-\tfrac12)^2+\tfrac14}=\sqrt{2-\sqrt3}.\] Consequently the small diameter is \(2\rho=2\sqrt{2-\sqrt3}-\sqrt3+1\).
  4. Since \((\sqrt6-\sqrt2)^2=8-4\sqrt3\), we have \(2\sqrt{2-\sqrt3}=\sqrt6-\sqrt2\). Therefore \[\begin{aligned}2\rho&=\sqrt6-\sqrt2-\sqrt3+1\\&=(\sqrt3-1)(\sqrt2-1).\end{aligned}\] Take \(a=3,b=-1,c=2,d=-1\), giving \(a+b+c+d=\boxed3\).

What to remember: Connect centers before manipulating radicals. Remember that \(b\) and \(d\) may be negative integers.

A grid-coloring proof using directed cyclesProblem 21

Each of the 9 squares in a \(3\times3\) grid is to be colored red, blue, or yellow in such a way that each red square shares an edge with at least one blue square, each blue square shares an edge with at least one yellow square, and each yellow square shares an edge with at least one red square. Colorings that can be obtained from one another by rotations and/or reflections are to be considered the same. How many different colorings are possible?
  • (A) 3
  • (B) 9
  • (C) 12
  • (D) 18
  • (E) 27

The theory involved

Turn a local 'must have a neighbor' rule into directed arrows. A finite directed graph in which every vertex has an outgoing arrow contains a directed cycle. Grid parity and color order sharply restrict that cycle.

Answer

Answer C: 12.

The SOMATH solution

  1. From each cell choose one neighbor of its required next color: red to blue, blue to yellow, yellow to red. Repeatedly follow arrows. Because there are only nine cells, eventually a cell repeats, producing a simple directed cycle.
  2. The cycle length is divisible by 3 because colors repeat every three steps. It is also even because a square grid is bipartite: every move switches checkerboard parity. A simple cycle has at most nine cells, so its length must be 6. In a \(3\times3\) grid, a six-cell cycle is the perimeter of a \(2\times3\) or \(3\times2\) rectangle.
  3. Fix the center cell's color as blue. Rotations and reflections put one such cycle in the top two rows and orient its colors as follows, where the bottom row is not yet filled: \[\begin{array}{ccc}R&B&Y\\Y&B&R\\?&?&?\end{array}\] The six cycle cells already meet the neighbor rule. If the bottom-right cell were red, the bottom-middle would have to be blue; then the bottom-left is forced yellow but has no red neighbor. If the bottom-right were blue, the bottom-middle must be yellow and the bottom-left red, which has no blue neighbor. Hence the bottom-right is yellow.
  4. Now inspect the bottom-left. If it is red, the middle must be blue, giving \(RBY\). If it is blue, the middle can be red or blue, giving \(BRY\) or \(BBY\). If it is yellow, the middle must be red, giving \(YRY\). Direct inspection confirms all four completions work.
  5. These four are inequivalent under square symmetries. The \(BBY\) and \(YRY\) completions have distinct color counts. The \(RBY\) and \(BRY\) completions each have three cells of each color, but the center has two blue neighbors in the first and one in the second. Thus there are exactly four types with a blue center. Cyclically renaming the colors gives four with red center and four with yellow center. Symmetry cannot change the center's color, so the total is \(3(4)=\boxed{12}\).

What to remember: Do not divide a raw count by eight without checking symmetry. Some colorings have fewer than eight distinct rotations and reflections.

Digit deficits and divisibility by elevenProblem 22

A seven-digit positive integer is chosen at random. What is the probability that the number is divisible by 11, given that the sum of its digits is 61?
  • (A) \(\frac3{14}\)
  • (B) \(\frac3{11}\)
  • (C) \(\frac27\)
  • (D) \(\frac4{11}\)
  • (E) \(\frac37\)

The theory involved

When a digit sum is close to its maximum, count deficits from 9 rather than the digits themselves. Divisibility by 11 depends on the alternating sum of digits.

Answer

Answer A: \(\frac3{14}\).

The SOMATH solution

  1. Seven 9s sum to 63. To get 61, subtract a total deficit of 2 across seven positions. Each deficit is 0, 1, or 2, so every resulting number is still seven digits. Stars and bars gives \(\binom{8}{6}=\binom82=28\) equally likely numbers.
  2. Let \(E\) be the total deficit in the three even-numbered positions, and \(O\) the deficit in the four odd-numbered positions. Then \(O+E=2\).
  3. The odd-position digit sum minus the even-position sum is \((36-O)-(27-E)=9-O+E=7+2E\). Since \(E=0,1,\) or 2, this difference is 7, 9, or 11. Only 11 is divisible by 11, so all deficit must lie in the three even positions.
  4. Distribute a deficit of 2 among those three positions in \(\binom42=6\) ways. The probability is \(6/28=\boxed{\frac3{14}}\).

What to remember: Condition first. The sample space is the 28 numbers with digit sum 61, not all seven-digit integers.

Matching two grid-numbering systemsProblem 23

A rectangular grid of squares has 141 rows and 91 columns. Each square has room for two numbers. Horace and Vera each fill in the grid by putting the numbers from 1 through \(141\cdot91=12{,}831\) into the squares. Horace fills the grid horizontally: he puts 1 through 91 in order from left to right in row 1, puts 92 through 182 into row 2 in order from left to right, and continues similarly through row 141. Vera fills the grid vertically: she puts 1 through 141 in order from top to bottom in column 1, then 142 through 282 into column 2 in order from top to bottom, and continues similarly through column 91. How many squares get two copies of the same number?
  • (A) 7
  • (B) 10
  • (C) 11
  • (D) 12
  • (E) 19

The theory involved

Write an explicit value for each numbering rule at a general cell. Matching values produce a linear Diophantine equation, whose integer solutions are controlled by a greatest common divisor.

Answer

Answer C: 11.

The SOMATH solution

  1. Use zero-based row \(r=0,\ldots,140\) and column \(c=0,\ldots,90\). Horace writes \(91r+c+1\), while Vera writes \(141c+r+1\).
  2. Equality gives \(91r+c=141c+r\), or \(90r=140c\). Divide by 10 to obtain \(9r=14c\).
  3. Since 9 and 14 are coprime, the solutions are \(r=14t,c=9t\) for an integer \(t\). The grid bounds require \(0\le t\le10\).
  4. The eleven values \(t=0,1,\ldots,10\) each give a distinct cell, so the answer is \(\boxed{11}\). Both the top-left and bottom-right cells are included.

What to remember: Zero-based coordinates remove distracting offsets. Count the endpoints when converting an allowed parameter range into a number of cells.

First-step probability equationsProblem 24

A frog hops along the number line according to the following rules.
  • It starts at 0.
  • If it is at 0, then it moves to 1 with probability \(\frac12\) and it disappears with probability \(\frac12\).
  • For \(n=1,2,\) or 3, if it is at \(n\), then it moves to \(n+1\) with probability \(\frac14\), it moves to \(n-1\) with probability \(\frac14\), and it disappears with probability \(\frac12\).
What is the probability that the frog reaches 4?
  • (A) \(\frac1{101}\)
  • (B) \(\frac1{100}\)
  • (C) \(\frac1{99}\)
  • (D) \(\frac1{98}\)
  • (E) \(\frac1{97}\)

The theory involved

A first-step equation conditions on the next move and automatically accounts for paths that revisit earlier states. Give disappearance a success probability of zero and reaching the target a success probability of one.

Answer

Answer E: \(\frac1{97}\).

The SOMATH solution

  1. Let \(p_i\) be the probability of eventually reaching 4 starting from \(i\). Then \(p_4=1\). At 0, the first move gives \(p_0=\frac12p_1\), so \(p_1=2p_0\).
  2. For \(i=1,2,3\), condition on the next step: \[p_i=\tfrac14p_{i+1}+\tfrac14p_{i-1}+\tfrac12(0).\] Rearrange to \(p_{i+1}=4p_i-p_{i-1}\).
  3. Starting from \(p_1=2p_0\), compute \(p_2=8p_0-p_0=7p_0\), then \(p_3=28p_0-2p_0=26p_0\).
  4. Finally \(p_4=104p_0-7p_0=97p_0=1\). Thus the required probability is \(p_0=\boxed{\frac1{97}}\). This includes paths with any number of backward steps.

What to remember: The direct path is only one way to succeed. Recursive state probabilities include all possible returns without listing infinitely many paths.

Unfolding a reflected pathProblem 25

Square \(ABCD\) has sides of length 4. Points \(P\) and \(Q\) lie on \(\overline{AD}\) and \(\overline{CD}\), respectively, with \(AP=\frac85\) and \(DQ=\frac{10}{3}\). A path begins along the segment from \(P\) to \(Q\) and continues by reflecting against the sides of \(ABCD\) (with congruent incoming and outgoing angles). If the path hits a vertex of the square, it terminates there; otherwise it continues forever. At which vertex does the path terminate?
  • (A) \(A\)
  • (B) \(B\)
  • (C) \(C\)
  • (D) \(D\)
  • (E) The path continues forever.

The theory involved

Instead of reflecting the path, reflect the square across its sides. The path becomes a straight line through a tiled plane. A vertex hit corresponds to a lattice point whose coordinates are multiples of the square's side length.

Answer

Answer B: \(B\).

The SOMATH solution

  1. Set \(A=(0,0),B=(4,0),C=(4,4),D=(0,4)\). Then \(P=(0,8/5)\) and \(Q=(10/3,4)\). The initial slope is \((4-8/5)/(10/3)=18/25\).
  2. In the unfolded plane, the line is \(y=8/5+(18/25)x\). A grid vertex has coordinates \((4m,4n)\), giving \(25n=10+18m\).
  3. Modulo 25, this requires \(18m\equiv15\pmod{25}\). Since \(18\cdot7\equiv1\pmod{25}\), \(m\equiv5\pmod{25}\). The first positive solution is \(m=5\), and then \(n=4\). Thus the first vertex reached in the unfolded plane is \((20,16)\).
  4. Fold back into the original square. An odd multiple of 4 in the horizontal direction lands on the right side; an even multiple of 4 in the vertical direction lands on the bottom. Since \(m=5\) is odd and \(n=4\) is even, the vertex is the bottom-right corner, \(\boxed B\).

What to remember: Use congruences to locate the first vertex, then parity to identify which original corner it represents. A straight unfolded path replaces many separate reflections.
