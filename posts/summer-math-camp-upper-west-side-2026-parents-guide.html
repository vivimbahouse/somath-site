Does summer math actually matter? The research on summer learning loss

Every UWS parent has heard someone say “kids just need a break” over the summer. That is partly true. But for math specifically, the data on summer learning loss is consistent enough that ignoring it is a real decision, not a default.

Research compiled by RAND Corporation on summer learning loss finds that students, on average, lose roughly two to three months of math achievement over the summer — and the gap between beginning-of-year (BOY) and end-of-year (EOY) benchmark scores is larger in math than in any other subject. Reading skills are relatively durable over summer; math computation and procedural fluency decay faster because they require regular practice to maintain.

What that looks like in practice depends heavily on grade level:

  • A rising 4th grader (coming out of PS 87, PS 199, PS 452, or PS 9) who does no math over the summer typically enters September having forgotten a meaningful portion of their multiplication facts, fraction concepts, and multi-step word problem strategies. Their teacher spends the first four to six weeks of school re-teaching material from the previous spring. That is time not spent on new content.
  • A rising 9th grader preparing for high school faces a steeper cost. Students who do not maintain their Algebra I skills over the summer often find that their high school’s Geometry or Algebra II course assumes fluency they no longer have. That gap compounds through 10th and 11th grade, affecting the SAT math score that matters for college merit aid and the AP Calculus placement that matters for transcript rigor.

The implication is not that every child needs intensive summer math. For most students at the elementary level, one to two sessions per week is sufficient to prevent loss and build modest forward momentum. For students approaching a high-stakes test — SHSAT, Hunter, SAT — or who finished the school year with real gaps, more structure is justified. The honest position is: doing nothing has a cost, but doing the wrong thing (joyless worksheet packs, undifferentiated online drill) also has a cost, because it poisons the relationship with math for the following school year.

The decision framework: 6 questions UWS parents should answer before choosing

Before you compare programs, answer these six questions. Your answers will eliminate at least half the options for your specific child.

  1. What is my child’s current math level? Is this a student who is on grade level, below grade level (needing catch-up), or above grade level (enrichment / acceleration)? A student who needs catch-up should not be in the same program as a student who needs enrichment; the pacing and content are fundamentally different. Read our guide on enrichment vs. tutoring on the UWS if you are not sure which your child needs.
  2. What is the goal? Enrichment (going deeper and broader), catch-up (filling a gap from the school year), or test prep (SHSAT, Hunter, SAT/ACT, AP)? These require different programs.
  3. What structure does my child work best in? Some students thrive with a teacher-led small group and whiteboard problems. Others do better self-pacing through a workbook or adaptive online tool. Knowing this honestly will save you money.
  4. In-person or online? In-person is better for younger students and for students who need real-time feedback and accountability. Online is viable and sometimes excellent for motivated older students, and it is the obvious choice if your family travels in July. Consider a hybrid: in-person twice a week in June, online during the July travel window, back in-person in August.
  5. How many sessions per week can realistically happen? One or two sessions per week is plenty for maintenance and light enrichment. Three or more is appropriate for genuine catch-up or intensive test prep. Be honest about summer schedules, camp conflicts, and your child’s actual capacity for structured work in the heat of July.
  6. What is the budget? Options range from $0 (Khan Academy, public library programs, MoMath admission-only events) to $200–$400/month for chain centers to $60/hour for small-group instruction at SOMATH. The right budget depends on the goal: a child who needs SHSAT prep deserves more investment than a child who just needs light maintenance. See the cost comparison table in Section 7 for a side-by-side.

Mathnasium UWS

Drop-in workshop model · ~$400–$450/month membership · Self-paced worksheet curriculum

Mathnasium operates on a membership model: families pay a monthly fee and can bring their child in for sessions at any point during open hours. The curriculum is Mathnasium’s proprietary worksheet-based system, which assesses the student on entry and then works systematically through foundational gaps. Students work somewhat independently with periodic check-ins from center instructors.

What it does well: The drop-in flexibility is genuinely useful for UWS families with chaotic summer schedules. The Mathnasium curriculum is methodical about filling foundational gaps — if your child somehow missed fraction division in 5th grade, Mathnasium’s systematic approach will find and address it. For students who need scaffolded, patient remediation at their own pace, the model works.

What it does less well: The instructor-to-student ratio in a busy Mathnasium center is high — a student does not get sustained one-on-one or small-group attention the way they do in a SOMATH cohort. The worksheet-dominant model builds procedural fluency but does less for mathematical reasoning, word problem strategy, and the kind of flexible thinking that matters for SHSAT and SAT math. Students who need enrichment (going deeper and further) rather than remediation often find the pacing too slow.

Honest bottom line: Mathnasium UWS is a solid option for foundational catch-up in an accessible, low-pressure format. It is not the best choice for SHSAT prep, enrichment, or students who have already solidified their foundations and need to move forward.

Russian School of Mathematics (RSM) — UES Location

Classroom model · 12-week course structure · ~$200–$250/month during academic year; summer intensives vary · Proprietary curriculum

RSM’s nearest location to the UWS is on the Upper East Side. The RSM model is a structured classroom environment — groups of 10–15 students working through RSM’s proprietary math curriculum, which emphasizes problem-solving depth, number theory, and mathematical reasoning drawn from the Eastern European tradition. RSM is not a tutoring center or a remediation service; it is a structured enrichment program for students who are already motivated math learners.

What it does well: RSM’s curriculum is genuinely rigorous. Students who complete RSM’s sequence develop strong mathematical reasoning and problem-solving skills. The classroom format cultivates healthy mathematical competition among peers. For gifted students who want deep enrichment, RSM produces real results.

What it does less well: The UES location requires a meaningful commute from the UWS. The classroom format (10–15 students) means instructors cannot individualize instruction the way a small-group model can. RSM is not designed for catch-up or for students who are behind; the program assumes a certain level of readiness, and students who do not meet it often struggle and disengage. The annual commitment (~$2,400/year) is also significant.

For a detailed side-by-side, see our RSM vs. SOMATH comparison. The honest summary: RSM is excellent for the right student (motivated, at or above grade level, willing to commute); it is not the right first call for catch-up or test prep.

Kumon

Worksheet-based · ~$150–$200/month · Daily independent practice model

Kumon is the most widely available math program in NYC, with multiple Manhattan locations. The Kumon model is built around daily independent practice using Kumon’s graded worksheet progression. Students visit the Kumon center twice a week and complete Kumon worksheets every day at home. The curriculum progresses from very basic counting skills through calculus using Kumon’s proprietary sequence.

What it does well: Kumon is excellent at building fact fluency and procedural automaticity. Students who complete Kumon’s arithmetic sequence genuinely become fast, accurate calculators. The daily-practice model instills a math habit that is hard to argue with. The price point is the most accessible among structured programs.

What it does less well: Kumon is almost entirely procedural. It does not teach mathematical reasoning, word problem strategy, or the kind of creative problem-solving that SHSAT and SAT math require. Many Kumon students can execute the procedure flawlessly but freeze when the problem is presented in an unfamiliar format. For a child who needs fluency and procedural automaticity, Kumon is a legitimate choice. For a child who needs to think mathematically — which is what the NYC competitive landscape ultimately requires — Kumon alone is not enough.

Honest bottom line: Kumon works. But it works for a narrow slice of what summer math needs to accomplish. It is a good complement to richer instruction, not a replacement for it. See our full SOMATH vs. RSM, Kumon, and Mathnasium comparison for a side-by-side across all four.

Manhattan-wide options worth a commute from the UWS

MoMath Summer Programs — 635 Sixth Ave, Chelsea

The National Museum of Mathematics (MoMath) at 635 Sixth Ave in Chelsea is genuinely one of the best math resources in the city, and it is vastly underused by UWS families. The museum runs programming year-round, but the summer calendar is particularly rich. Key summer events include:

  • Family Fridays — free with museum admission, hands-on math activities for ages 6–12.
  • NYC Math Festival — scheduled for July 11, 2026, a free outdoor festival in Fearless Girl Plaza with math games, puzzles, and activities for all ages.
  • MATRIX — Math Training and Recreational Innovation eXperience — MoMath’s intensive summer program for high schoolers who want to go deep into mathematical topics not covered in school. Check the MoMath calendar for current session dates and pricing.

Museum admission runs approximately $25 for adults and $20 for children — making a Family Friday visit one of the cheapest legitimate math enrichment activities in Manhattan. The D/F/M to 23rd St or N/R/W to 23rd St takes about 20 minutes from the UWS. Absolutely worth the trip.

Math Programs at Columbia, NYU, and BMCC

Columbia University’s summer programs include academic enrichment for pre-college students, though specific math-focused offerings vary by year — check Columbia’s Summer Programs site directly for current 2026 offerings. NYU runs the NYU Summer at NYU and Math for America-adjacent programming; NYU Courant’s summer programs are more relevant to high school students interested in serious mathematics. BMCC (Borough of Manhattan Community College) occasionally offers summer math bridge programs for students entering college or transitioning to high school. These tend to serve older students (9th grade and up) and focus on remediation or acceleration rather than enrichment. Worth checking if your rising high schooler needs a more structured academic summer environment.

Independent School Summer Programs

Several Manhattan independent schools open their summer programs to outside students. Trinity School (139 W 91st St) and Columbia Grammar & Preparatory School run summer academic programs that sometimes include math enrichment — check their websites for 2026 offerings. Riverdale Country School runs a summer program in the Bronx. The summer plan by grade and goal

Rising 2nd–4th grade: foundation building

This is the most important window for preventing summer loss. Students in this range are building the number sense, fact fluency, and multi-step word problem skills that everything else rests on. A PS 87 or PS 199 rising 3rd grader who does nothing over the summer often enters September with weakened multiplication fluency and fraction intuition that their teacher then has to rebuild from scratch. The 3rd grade math parent guide covers the specific benchmarks to know.

Recommended approach: One to two sessions per week with a small group (SOMATH’s elementary track, or Beast Academy at home if the student is advanced and self-directed). Daily 15–20 minutes of Khan Academy practice is a useful supplement. Avoid pure worksheet programs for this age group — they build worksheet completion, not mathematical thinking.

For students identified for the Anderson School G&T or NEST+m track, this is also the window to deepen number sense and early algebraic thinking so that their advanced coursework in 4th and 5th grade rests on a strong foundation. See our guide on best gifted math programs on the UWS for program-specific recommendations.

Rising 5th–6th grade: gifted track and Anderson / NEST+m / Hunter HCHS preparation

Students entering 5th or 6th grade who are on the G&T track — or whose parents are considering it — face a specific challenge: the Hunter College High School test is taken in January of 6th grade, which means the summer before 6th grade is the most important academic summer in a student’s elementary career. The Hunter test is math-heavy and requires fluency with topics that most NYC public school 5th graders have not yet seen in full depth.

For non-Hunter students in this range, summer is an excellent window to solidify pre-algebra concepts — ratios, proportional reasoning, exponents, and the early algebraic thinking that makes the jump to 7th-grade algebra smooth rather than jarring. See our 5th grade and 6th grade math parent guides for grade-level benchmarks.

Recommended approach: In-person small-group enrichment (SOMATH’s middle school track) two sessions per week, with AoPS Beast Academy at home for advanced students. Hunter HCHS families should book a diagnostic evaluation in May or June — do not wait until September. Our guide to Hunter College High School test prep on the UWS covers the timeline in detail.

Rising 7th–8th grade: SHSAT-ready and algebra readiness

This is the highest-stakes summer for the majority of UWS families focused on specialized high schools. The SHSAT is administered in October of 8th grade, which means the summer before 8th grade is the ideal intensive preparation window — two months of focused work before school, activities, and after-school commitments take over. Students at MS 245 The Computer School, The Anderson School, JHS 54 Booker T. Washington, and other District 3 middle schools sit for this test. For the complete preparation timeline, see our SHSAT math 12-month roadmap.

Rising 7th graders who are not yet SHSAT-focused should use the summer to solidify and accelerate into pre-algebra and Algebra I. A student who enters 7th grade with strong pre-algebra is a student who can complete Algebra I by the end of 7th grade — positioning them to take Geometry in 8th grade and accelerate through the entire high school sequence. Our post on when to start math enrichment for NYC students covers this progression in detail.

Recommended approach: Three sessions per week for SHSAT prep students (two sessions of content, one session of timed practice); two sessions per week for enrichment/algebra readiness students. SOMATH’s middle school summer track covers both.

Rising 9th–10th grade: Algebra I/II, Geometry, and ISEE for transfer applicants

The summer before 9th grade is the last easy window to make a meaningful course placement difference. A student who enters 9th grade already comfortable with Algebra I content can often place into an accelerated section or a Geometry class immediately, setting up the rest of the high school sequence. Students applying to transfer to private schools (Trinity, Collegiate, Brearley, Dalton, Calhoun, Trevor Day, Riverdale) via the ISEE need to address math specifically — the ISEE Upper Level math section covers through Algebra II and early Geometry, and many students transferring in 9th or 10th grade have not yet had that material in school.

For students at this level, see our 8th grade math parent guide for the key algebra benchmarks to lock in before high school begins.

Recommended approach: Two sessions per week — content-focused for algebra/geometry, test-focused for ISEE prep students. This is a range where motivated students can do well with AoPS Online in addition to in-person sessions.

Rising 11th–12th grade: SAT/ACT, AP Calculus, AP Statistics

For students in this range, summer math is almost always test-focused: Digital SAT math prep, AP Calculus AB or BC review, or AP Statistics if the student is taking it in the fall. The Digital SAT math section covers through Algebra II and data analysis; the AP Calc section covers limits, derivatives, integrals, and series (for BC). Both require a solid algebra and pre-calculus foundation. A student whose Algebra II is shaky will find that AP Calculus and SAT math prep both hit the same wall at different times.

Recommended approach: Two sessions per week for SAT math prep or AP Calc pre-work. SOMATH’s high school summer track runs pre-AP Calc, AP Calc AB/BC review, and Digital SAT math. AoPS Online is the right supplement for students aiming at the most selective colleges. See our full UWS math enrichment parent’s guide for high school program details.

UWS-specific summer math considerations

A few things that are specific to the Upper West Side and relevant to program choice:

PS 87, PS 199, PS 452, PS 9, and PS 84 families should know that their schools generally run strong math programs during the year — but also that the summer transition into a new grade level (with a new teacher, potentially a new curriculum emphasis) is where gaps open. If your rising 3rd grader had an excellent PS 87 teacher this year, that does not mean their September teacher will assume the same depth of prior knowledge. The summer is the window to make the transition smooth.

Anderson School and NEST+m G&T track families face a specific summer calculus: their children are already accelerated, and “keeping up over summer” means something different for them. Anderson students who do nothing over the summer often find that their fall coursework moves quickly past material they half-remember from spring. For these students, two sessions per week of enrichment work — harder problems, deeper reasoning — is more appropriate than remediation or even standard review. Hunter HCHS testing timeline: The Hunter College High School admissions test is administered in January or February of 6th grade. That means the summer before 6th grade is the primary preparation window. Families who wait until October or November of 6th grade lose four to five months of preparation time. One of the most common mistakes UWS families make is over-scheduling the summer: five-days-a-week math programs that leave children exhausted and resentful by August. Summer math works best when it is concentrated, rigorous, and bounded — two 90-minute sessions per week of genuinely good instruction produces better results than five days of worksheet completion. The goal is to go into September stronger, not to simulate a school year.

FAQ

How much math should my child do over the summer?

For most students, one to two sessions per week (60–90 minutes each) is plenty to prevent summer learning loss and keep skills sharp without burning out. Three or more sessions per week is appropriate only for students who are actively catching up after a difficult school year, or for rising 8th graders in the final prep window before the October SHSAT. The goal is maintenance and moderate growth — not a summer school grind. If your child is leaving school in strong shape and just needs to stay sharp, two sessions per week of good instruction does that. If they are heading into a high-stakes test or have real gaps, three sessions may be warranted for a defined period (not the entire summer).

Is in-person better than online for summer math?

It depends on the student. Younger students (roughly K–6) generally benefit from in-person instruction: the whiteboard dynamic, real-time feedback, and peer interaction in a small group produce deeper engagement than a screen. Motivated middle and high schoolers — especially those who already work independently and are comfortable with self-direction — can do very well with structured online programs like AoPS Online or a live tutor via Brighterly. The honest answer: in-person is the default for elementary students; online is a viable and sometimes excellent choice for self-directed teenagers. A hybrid is also worth considering if your family travels in July.

What’s the cheapest legitimate summer math option in NYC?

Khan Academy is genuinely free and genuinely good for structured practice and video instruction. Their summer plan feature gives your child a structured daily curriculum at no cost. MoMath programs at 635 Sixth Ave in Chelsea are free with museum admission (roughly $25 for adults, $20 for children), and their summer programming — including Family Fridays and the NYC Math Festival on July 11 — is high quality. For families who need cost-free structured practice, Khan Academy is the honest first recommendation. For families who want in-person enrichment, MoMath visits are an inexpensive addition to any summer plan.

Do summer math programs help with SHSAT prep?

Yes — but only if the program is structured specifically around SHSAT content and strategy. Random worksheet practice does not build the test-taking skills the SHSAT requires. The SHSAT math section covers percents, ratios, fractions, geometry, and algebra through 8th grade, plus specific question formats that reward pattern recognition and strategy. When should summer math start?

Most UWS families start in mid-June, roughly one to two weeks after public school ends, and wrap up by mid-August before school-year preparation begins. This gives a clean 8-week window. Some families start as early as the first week of June for more intensive SHSAT or SAT prep. The key is not to wait until late July — by then the summer is half gone and the most valuable prevention window (the first four to six weeks of summer) has already passed. Book your evaluation in May or early June to lock in your preferred schedule and cohort.

How does SOMATH compare to Mathnasium UWS and Russian School of Math?

SOMATH’s primary differentiator is small group size (6–8 students maximum per cohort), consistent instructors who teach the same cohort all summer, and whiteboard-based problem-solving rather than worksheet or workbook completion. Mathnasium UWS uses a drop-in, self-paced worksheet model that works well for students who need flexible scheduling and systematic remediation. Russian School of Math (UES location) uses a structured classroom model with RSM’s proprietary curriculum — excellent for already-strong students who want rigorous enrichment, with a commute from the UWS.
