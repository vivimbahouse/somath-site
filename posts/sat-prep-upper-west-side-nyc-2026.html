The Digital SAT: what UWS families need to know about the format

As of March 2024, the SAT is fully digital. There is no paper version for domestic students. Your student will register through College Board’s SAT Suite, download the Bluebook app to their laptop or tablet ahead of test day, and take the exam on that device — the app works offline, so a spotty WiFi connection at the test center is not a factor.

The full test is 2 hours and 14 minutes, broken into two sections:

Section Modules Questions per module Time per module Total time
Reading & Writing 2 adaptive modules 27 questions each 32 minutes each 64 minutes
Math 2 adaptive modules 22 questions each 35 minutes each 70 minutes

The key structural change from the old paper SAT is adaptive testing. Module 1 in each section is the same for all students. The difficulty of Module 2 is determined by your Module 1 performance: students who do well on Module 1 receive a harder Module 2 (with higher score potential), while students who struggle receive an easier Module 2 (with a lower score ceiling). This means that how a student starts each section matters enormously — there is no recovery if Module 1 goes poorly. For a more detailed breakdown of test dates, score release schedules, and how the adaptive scoring algorithm works, see our post on Digital SAT structure, dates, and scores in 2026.

Bluebook app tip: Students should download Bluebook and run the full built-in practice test (College Board provides two official full-length digital practice tests in the app) well before test day. The interface includes a built-in graphing calculator for Math and a reference sheet — getting comfortable with both tools is part of prep, not a footnote.

The scoring scale is unchanged: 200–800 per section, 400–1600 combined. What has changed is pacing — the digital test is shorter than the old paper exam (the previous version was over 3 hours), and many students find the compressed timing more manageable once they have practiced the format.

SAT Math: a section-by-section breakdown

The Math section is where most students have the most room to improve with focused prep. Here is what the College Board’s Digital SAT Math section actually tests in 2026:

Module structure

2 modules · 22 questions each · 35 minutes each · 70 minutes total

Both modules allow a built-in graphing calculator. About 75% of questions are multiple-choice (four options); the remaining 25% are student-produced responses (no answer choices given, so no guessing). Module 2 adapts based on Module 1 performance.

Domain 1: Algebra (~35% of Math)

Approximately 13–15 questions across both modules

Linear equations in one variable, linear equations in two variables, linear functions, systems of linear equations and inequalities, and linear inequalities. This is the highest-weight domain. Students who have completed Algebra 1 have seen all the content, but the SAT tests it with more abstract phrasing and multi-step setups than a typical classroom exam. The connection between SAT Math and school math is real but imperfect — fluency with classroom Algebra does not automatically translate to SAT speed and accuracy without specific practice.

Domain 2: Advanced Math (~35% of Math)

Approximately 13–15 questions across both modules

Equivalent expressions, nonlinear equations and systems, quadratic and exponential functions, polynomial functions, function notation, and rational/radical equations. This is the domain that differentiates 650+ Math scorers from 750+ scorers. Students who have completed Algebra 2 or Precalculus have the raw content knowledge; the challenge is recognizing which algebraic manipulation to apply in a novel context. Students aiming for 750+ should spend the majority of their focused prep time here.

Domain 3: Problem-Solving & Data Analysis (~15% of Math)

Approximately 5–7 questions across both modules

Ratios, rates, proportions, percentages, unit conversion, data distributions (mean, median, range, spread), probability, inference from data, and two-way tables. These questions often require careful reading as much as computation. Students who struggle here usually rush the setup, not the arithmetic.

Domain 4: Geometry & Trigonometry (~15% of Math)

Approximately 5–7 questions across both modules

Area, perimeter, volume, the Pythagorean theorem, properties of circles (arc length, sector area, equations of circles), right-triangle trigonometry (sin, cos, tan, and their reciprocals), and basic radian measure. The reference sheet provided in Bluebook covers common geometry formulas, so memorization of formulas is less critical than knowing which formula applies and how to set up the problem. For students also taking AP Calculus, there is meaningful overlap with the geometry and function content covered in that course — see our comparison of AP Calc AB vs. BC for context on sequencing.

When should UWS students start SAT prep?

The most productive SAT prep window for most UWS students is spring of sophomore year through spring of junior year. Here is what that looks like in practice:

  • Freshman year: Focus on school math. No SAT prep needed yet, but taking a full-length diagnostic (available free through Khan Academy’s official SAT prep partnership with College Board) in the spring gives you a baseline.
  • Spring of sophomore year: Begin structured prep, especially in Math. Students who have completed Algebra 2 are content-ready for most of the SAT Math section. Students still in Algebra 2 can start on Reading & Writing and Algebra domains while finishing the coursework.
  • Fall/winter of junior year: Most intensive prep period. 8–12 weeks of focused work, 3–5 practice tests, targeted work on weak domains.
  • Spring of junior year (March, May, or June): First official SAT. This is the target sitting for most students.
  • Fall of senior year (August or October): Retake if needed. Most students improve 30–80 points on a retake with specific review between sittings.

Students at accelerated programs — Stuyvesant, Bronx Science, Bard High School Early College — who are ahead in math may be ready to begin SAT Math prep as early as 9th grade. Students at Beacon or Eleanor Roosevelt following a standard math sequence are usually best served by waiting until they are into or through Algebra 2. The curriculum gap between a student who has done Precalculus and one who is mid-Algebra 2 shows up sharply in the Advanced Math domain.

If your student attends Hunter College High School or a private school like Collegiate, Trinity, or Brearley where the math sequence moves quickly, earlier prep is often viable and sometimes necessary given the academic calendar compression these schools create.

For students already in the 1350–1450 combined range, a disciplined self-study regimen with 4–6 full practice tests is often as effective as a group course, at a fraction of the cost. FAQ

Is the SAT still paper-based in 2026?

No. The SAT has been fully digital since March 2024. Students take the exam on a laptop or tablet using the Bluebook app, which is downloaded in advance and works without an internet connection during the test. The 2026 Digital SAT is 2 hours and 14 minutes long. For a full breakdown of the format, see our post on Digital SAT structure, dates, and scores.

What score do you need for Columbia or Cornell?

Columbia’s middle 50% SAT range is approximately 1510–1570. Cornell’s middle 50% is roughly 1480–1550 depending on the college within Cornell. A target of 1500+ is a reasonable floor for both if you plan to submit your score. NYU’s middle 50% is approximately 1390–1530, Cooper Union engineering applicants should target 750+ Math, and CUNY Macaulay Honors typically requires 1300+ combined. Check each school’s Columbia admissions and NYU admissions pages directly for the most current data, as these ranges shift year to year.

Students at Beacon, Eleanor Roosevelt, or LaGuardia following a standard sequence are best served by waiting until they are into Algebra 2.

Does the SAT still matter if schools are test-optional?

Test-optional policies remain in place at many schools, including Columbia, as of 2026. But “test-optional” does not mean “test-neutral.” Research consistently shows that applicants who submit strong scores benefit from doing so. A score in the middle 50% range for a given school is worth submitting; a score in the bottom quartile is usually better withheld. The real question is whether your student’s score is strong enough to help their application — which is a function of their target school’s actual admit data, not the test-optional marketing language.
