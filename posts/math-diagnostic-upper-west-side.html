What a written math diagnostic actually is

A math diagnostic is a structured assessment that answers three questions in writing: what does this student know solidly, what do they know shakily, and what do they not yet know at all? The output is a report — not a conversation, not a quick verbal summary, not a level-number.

The word written matters. A verbal summary from a program manager after a 15-minute chat with your child is not a diagnostic; it is a sales conversation dressed as an assessment. A written report is different in three specific ways. First, it forces the assessor to commit to a claim on the record. Second, it belongs to the family and can be shared with the school teacher, a private-school placement counselor, or a future tutor. Third, six months later, it can be checked against reality: did the gaps the report identified actually close?

Everything that follows in a math program — placement, curriculum choice, pacing, homework selection, whether to enrich or remediate — depends on this document. Programs that skip the written diagnostic are essentially guessing at placement, and the cost of that guess is paid by the student over the following six months.

What a real diagnostic includes (the six components)

Not every “evaluation” that a UWS math program offers is actually a diagnostic. A real one has six components, and if any are missing, the report is incomplete.

  1. Conceptual understanding. Does the student know why a procedure works, not just how to execute it? A 5th grader who can compute 3/4 ÷ 1/2 = 3/2 but cannot explain why we “flip and multiply” has a conceptual gap even if the answer is correct. A real diagnostic tests both.
  2. Procedural fluency. Can the student execute the standard algorithms accurately and quickly? Speed matters here: a student who can do multi-digit multiplication correctly but takes three minutes per problem will struggle when the same skill has to run in the background of an algebra problem two years later.
  3. Problem-solving transfer. When the diagnostic gives the same underlying concept in an unfamiliar wrapping — a word problem, a puzzle, a real-world question — can the student still solve it? This is where enrichment-track students separate from procedure-track students, and it is often the most useful signal in the report.
  4. Grade-level coverage across all four strands. Number sense, operations, algebraic reasoning, and geometry/measurement. A student can be two years ahead in one strand and one year behind in another; a diagnostic that only tests one strand misses this entirely.
  5. Mathematical confidence and reasoning style. How does the student approach an unfamiliar problem — do they start writing immediately, do they freeze, do they ask a clarifying question? A real diagnostic observes the process, not just the final answer.
  6. A recommendation with reasoning. The report has to end with a specific proposed track (enrichment, targeted skill-building, test prep, or a hybrid) plus the reasoning behind it. A diagnostic without a recommendation is just a scorecard; it does not tell the family what to do next.

Diagnostic vs. placement test: not the same thing

Many UWS families conflate these, and the programs are not always careful to distinguish them.

Dimension Placement Test Written Diagnostic
Output A level, grade, or bucket (e.g., “Grade 4 track”) A written report explaining specific strengths, gaps, and next steps
Purpose Sort students into existing classes Understand the student and then decide what class (if any) fits
Format Multiple choice or short-answer, timed, standardized Mix of computation, conceptual questions, and open problem-solving; observed live
What the family gets A number or level A document they can keep, share, and check against outcomes later
Best case Correctly places the student in a good default class Reveals the exact concepts to target and predicts what the next 8–12 weeks should look like
When to use After placement has already been informed by a diagnostic Always — before any placement decision

A good program uses a diagnostic upstream of placement. A weak program uses placement alone and calls it a diagnostic. The tell is simple: ask for the written report. If it does not exist, or the answer is “we’ll go over it verbally,” the diagnostic step was skipped.

Why UWS matters: the specific stakes here

Upper West Side parents face a specific version of this problem. UWS public elementary schools — PS 87, PS 199, PS 9, PS 452, PS 166, and the Anderson School among them — are strong on the whole, but math instruction quality varies teacher to teacher and year to year. A child can be at grade level according to the school and still have real gaps in the four strands. A child can also be under-challenged for a full year without anyone noticing, because “on grade level” in a UWS public school masks a wide spread of actual ability.

Then there is the pipeline. UWS families with a 4th or 5th grader are typically thinking two moves ahead: middle-school placement, then the SHSAT in October of 8th grade, then specialized-high-school admission. The families who navigate this well tend to have a written record of their child’s mathematical development starting from 3rd or 4th grade. That record almost always begins with a diagnostic.

The families who navigate it poorly tend to have used a drop-in center or a private tutor for several years without ever getting a written assessment. When the SHSAT arrives and the child is 60 points short, there is no baseline to point at — nobody knows whether the gaps are recent or old, whether they were flagged in year one and ignored, or whether the child has been on the wrong track for three years. A diagnostic prevents that.

What to ask any UWS math program before you enroll

Six questions to ask on your first call with any math program on the Upper West Side. The pattern of answers tells you whether the program leads with a diagnostic or skips it.

  1. Do you provide a written diagnostic report, or a verbal summary? A real program hands over a written document. Verbal-only is a signal to keep looking.
  2. How long is the turnaround from evaluation to written report? SOMATH is 48 hours. Any program that cannot give you a specific number is not producing a written report as a matter of course.
  3. Can I take the report with me and share it with my child’s school teacher? If yes, it is a real report. If the “report” only exists inside the program’s internal system, it is a placement record, not a diagnostic.
  4. Does the diagnostic test conceptual understanding, or only computation? Ask this directly. A program that only tests computation will only teach computation.
  5. What happens if your diagnostic says my child needs something you don’t offer? The honest answer is “we’ll tell you and refer you elsewhere.” A program that cannot imagine that scenario is not doing an honest diagnostic.
  6. Is the evaluation free or paid? Free is standard on the UWS in 2026 and is the right expectation. A program that charges for the intake evaluation before enrollment is unusual and worth questioning.

FAQ

What is a math diagnostic?

A math diagnostic is a structured written assessment that identifies what a student can do fluently, what they can do with prompting, and what they cannot yet do at all. A real diagnostic tests conceptual understanding (does the student know why a procedure works?), procedural fluency (can they execute it accurately and quickly?), and problem-solving transfer (can they apply the concept to an unfamiliar problem?). It is written down, delivered as a report, and belongs to the family — not verbal reassurance from a program manager after a 15-minute chat.

How is a math diagnostic different from a placement test?

A placement test sorts students into a bucket — Grade 3 track, Grade 4 track, remedial, advanced. A diagnostic explains why. Placement tests output a single number or level. A diagnostic outputs a map: which topics are solid, which are shaky, which are missing, and what the student’s reasoning style looks like on unfamiliar problems. Every good program uses a diagnostic upstream of placement. A program that only does placement is skipping the analysis and going straight to the bucket.

Why do drop-in centers skip the diagnostic?

Because their business model is volume. A drop-in tutoring or homework-help center bills by session or by month regardless of whether the student is placed correctly. A written diagnostic creates accountability — if the report says “gap in fraction operations” and six months later the child still has that gap, the program has to answer for it. Programs that make revenue on churn have no incentive to write that report down. Programs that make revenue on outcomes (retention, referrals, real progress) lead with the diagnostic because it is what makes everything else work.

What does the SOMATH written diagnostic include?

The report is delivered within 48 hours of the evaluation and includes: current grade-level performance across the four strands (number sense, operations, algebraic reasoning, geometry/measurement); a list of specific concepts that are solid, shaky, or missing; observations about the student’s problem-solving style and mathematical confidence; a recommended track — enrichment, targeted skill-building, or a hybrid — with reasoning; the first three topics we would work on in that track; and an honest opinion on whether SOMATH is the right fit or another program (Mathnasium, RSM, Kumon, private 1:1) would serve the student better.

Can I use the diagnostic without enrolling?

Yes. The diagnostic is a standalone artifact that many UWS families take to their school teacher, their private-school placement counselor, or their SHSAT tutor. It is a written record of where the child is, in mathematical terms, on a specific date. If you enroll at SOMATH, the diagnostic drives the placement. If you do not, the report is still useful and remains yours.
