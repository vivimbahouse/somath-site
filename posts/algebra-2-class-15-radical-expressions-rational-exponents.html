Understanding radicals

A radical reverses a power. In \(\sqrt[n]{a}\), the index \(n\) tells you which root to take, and the radicand \(a\) is the expression inside. A square root has an understood index of 2. The coefficient in \(5\sqrt[3]{a}\) multiplies the entire cube root; it is not the index.

The principal even root is nonnegative. For example, \(\sqrt{100}=10\), not \(\pm10\). By contrast, solving \(x^2=100\) gives two values, \(10\) and \(-10\), because both squares equal 100. An odd root keeps the sign of its radicand.

In this lesson we work with real numbers. An even-root radicand must be nonnegative; an odd-root radicand can be any real number. A variable radical expression has a domain: the set of inputs for which the original expression is defined.

Square roots and cube roots

Recognizing perfect powers makes exact arithmetic faster. Squaring a number gives a perfect square; cubing gives a perfect cube. The operations reverse each other when the root convention is respected.

An even power loses the sign of a real input. Consequently \(\sqrt{(-a)^2}=|a|\), not necessarily \(-a\) or \(a\). Cubing keeps the sign, so \(\sqrt[3]{a^3}=a\) for every real \(a\).

For a nonnegative numerator and positive denominator, you may take square roots of the numerator and denominator separately. Cube-root fractions also allow negative numerators. Keep exact fractions rather than changing to decimals.

Simplifying radicals with perfect powers

Factor a radicand into a perfect power and a remaining factor. For square roots, extract pairs of equal factors. For cube roots, extract groups of three; for fourth roots, extract groups of four.

Use the largest convenient perfect-power factor to shorten the work. If a square root becomes \(2\sqrt{27}\), it is not yet fully simplified because 27 still contains a square factor of 9. Continue until no suitable perfect-power factor remains.

An outside coefficient multiplies whatever leaves the radical. Do not add it to the extracted factor. For an odd root of a negative number, the negative sign can come outside; for even roots we stay within the real-number domain.

Radicals with variables

Always distinguish an identity for all real variables from a shortcut that assumes nonnegative variables. For example, \(\sqrt{x^2}=|x|\). When \(x<0\), replacing \(|x|\) with \(x\) would give a negative principal square root.

Factor even powers into squares before extracting them. The identity \(\sqrt{x^6}=|x^3|\) works for every real \(x\), while \(\sqrt{x^4}=x^2\) needs no absolute-value bars because \(x^2\) is already nonnegative. Odd roots do not need this sign correction.

Domain restrictions come from the original expression. A square-root radicand must be at least zero, and a denominator cannot be zero. For a fraction inside a square root, use a sign chart rather than requiring numerator and denominator to be positive separately.

Q20 Practice 5 of 5

Find the real domain of \(\sqrt{\frac{x-1}{x+2}}\).

Answer

\((-\infty,-2)\cup[1,\infty)\).

  1. The denominator excludes \(x=-2\). The radicand must satisfy \((x-1)/(x+2)\geq0\).
  2. The quotient is positive when both factors are negative (\(x<-2\)) or both are positive (\(x>1\)). At \(x=1\), the quotient is zero, so include 1.

Adding and subtracting radicals

Like radicals have the same index and the same radicand after simplification. Combine only their coefficients. For example, \(a\sqrt m+b\sqrt m=(a+b)\sqrt m\) whenever the real radicals are defined.

Simplify every radical before deciding which terms are alike. Terms that look different at first may reduce to the same root. Unlike roots remain separate, even if both are square roots.

The square root does not distribute across addition or subtraction. In general, \(\sqrt{a+b}\) is not \(\sqrt a+\sqrt b\). Check the structure of the expression before using a product or quotient rule.

Multiplying radicals

For \(a\geq0\) and \(b\geq0\), \(\sqrt a\sqrt b=\sqrt{ab}\). Multiply outside coefficients separately, then simplify the new radical. The real-number restrictions matter: this square-root rule is not permission to use negative radicands.

When multiplying a radical by a sum, distribute to every term. When multiplying two binomials, form all four products and collect like terms. A radical can disappear when its product is a perfect square.

Remember the middle term when squaring a binomial. The identity \((u-v)^2=u^2-2uv+v^2\) applies to radicals too. Conjugate pairs instead use \((u+v)(u-v)=u^2-v^2\).

Dividing radicals

For \(a\geq0\) and \(b>0\), \(\sqrt a/\sqrt b=\sqrt{a/b}\). The denominator condition is strict because division by zero is undefined. Simplify a fraction inside a radical when it reveals a perfect square.

With coefficients, divide the coefficients and the radicals as separate factors. For cube roots, the quotient rule also works with negative radicands, as long as the denominator is nonzero.

After simplification, retain exclusions from the original denominator. An expression that looks defined at a formerly excluded value does not restore that value to the original domain.

Rationalizing monomial denominators

Rationalizing rewrites a fraction without a radical in its denominator. It does not change its value. Multiply numerator and denominator by the same nonzero expression, which is a form of 1.

For one square-root factor in the denominator, multiplying by that same root produces a rational radicand. Simplify first when possible, and reduce the final fraction after rationalization.

For a cube root, complete a group of three equal factors rather than a pair. For variable denominators, state the original domain before multiplying; do not use a multiplier that is zero on an allowed input.

Rationalizing binomial denominators

A conjugate changes the sign between two terms. Multiplying \(a+\sqrt b\) by \(a-\sqrt b\) gives \(a^2-b\), because the two mixed terms cancel. Multiply the entire numerator and denominator by the conjugate.

The same structure works when both terms are radicals: \((\sqrt a+\sqrt b)(\sqrt a-\sqrt b)=a-b\). If the numerator also contains a sum, distribute it fully rather than multiplying just one term.

With variables, check whether the conjugate could be zero at an input allowed by the original expression. The transformed quotient may need a separately stated value at such an input. A rewritten formula must not silently change the domain.

For \(f(x)=\frac{\sqrt x-\sqrt2}{\sqrt x+\sqrt2}\), state the original domain, rationalize where possible, and find \(f(2)\).

Answer

  1. The original denominator is positive for every \(x\geq0\), so 2 is allowed.
  2. Multiplying by the conjugate gives numerator \((\sqrt x-\sqrt2)^2\) and denominator \(x-2\), but the multiplier is zero when \(x=2\).
  3. Evaluate that input in the original expression: the numerator is zero and the denominator is \(2\sqrt2\). The rationalized quotient alone would incorrectly omit this value.

Rational exponents

For a positive base \(a\), \(a^{m/n}=(\sqrt[n]{a})^m=\sqrt[n]{a^m}\), with integers \(m\) and \(n>0\). The denominator specifies the root; the numerator specifies the power. Taking the root first often keeps the arithmetic smaller.

A negative exponent means a reciprocal, not a negative answer. With positive bases, the usual product, quotient, and power rules remain valid: add exponents when multiplying like bases, subtract when dividing, and multiply when raising a power to a power.

In the rational-exponent exercises, variable bases are explicitly positive. This avoids ambiguity about negative bases and fractional-power conventions. Do not extend an identity beyond its stated domain without checking root parity and nonzero-denominator requirements.

Solving radical equations

Isolate the radical before applying a power. For a square-root equation, note both the radicand restriction and the fact that the isolated root is nonnegative. These restrictions can rule out a proposed answer immediately.

Squaring both sides may introduce extraneous candidates. For instance, two opposite numbers have equal squares even though the numbers themselves are not equal. Solve the transformed equation, then substitute every candidate into the original one.

If two radicals remain, isolate one, square carefully, and isolate the remaining radical before squaring again if needed. Keep an equals sign on each balanced step. A check must use the original equation, not just the equation obtained after squaring.

\(x=17\).

Q52 Practice 2 of 5

Solve \(3\sqrt{2x-1}+2=17\).

Answer

\(x=13\).

  1. Subtract 2 and divide by 3 to isolate \(\sqrt{2x-1}=5\).
  2. Square: \(2x-1=25\), so \(x=13\). Check: \(3\sqrt{25}+2=17\).

Q53 Practice 3 of 5

Solve \(\sqrt{x+10}=x-2\), rejecting any extraneous candidate.

Answer

\(x=6\) only.

  1. The right side must be nonnegative, so \(x\geq2\). Squaring gives \(x+10=(x-2)^2\).
  2. Rearrange to \(x^2-5x-6=(x-6)(x+1)=0\), giving candidates 6 and \(-1\).
  3. At 6: \(\sqrt{16}=4=6-2\). At \(-1\): \(\sqrt9=3\ne-3\), so reject \(-1\).

Q54 Practice 4 of 5

Solve \(\sqrt{3x+4}=x\). Show why one squared-equation candidate fails.

Answer

\(x=4\) only.

  1. The root is nonnegative, so \(x\geq0\). Squaring gives \(x^2-3x-4=(x-4)(x+1)=0\).
  2. At 4, both sides are 4. At \(-1\), the left side is 1 but the right side is \(-1\).

Q55 Practice 5 of 5

Solve \(\sqrt{x+6}=\sqrt{x+1}+1\).

Answer

\(x=3\).

  1. The original domain is \(x\geq-1\). Square: \(x+6=x+2+2\sqrt{x+1}\).
  2. Thus \(4=2\sqrt{x+1}\), so \(\sqrt{x+1}=2\) and \(x=3\).
  3. Original check: \(\sqrt9=\sqrt4+1\), or \(3=3\).

Radicals in real-world applications

Roots recover a length from an area or volume. If a square has area \(A\), its side is \(\sqrt A\). If a cube has volume \(V\), its edge is \(\sqrt[3]V\). Interpret the result in length units, not square or cubic units.

The Pythagorean theorem connects radicals to distances. A right triangle with legs \(a,b\) has hypotenuse \(\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\). On a coordinate grid, calculate horizontal and vertical changes first, square them, add, and take the principal root.

Use the stated model and its units, then reject answers that cannot represent the quantity in the story. Physical lengths are positive. Keep radicals exact until a decimal is requested; round only at the end. Application models here are supplied for mathematical practice, not engineering design.

  3. Reject \(-8\) as a length. For \(x=6\), the legs are 6 and 8, and \(\sqrt{36+64}=10\).

Simplify \(\sqrt{48u^4v^2}\) for real \(u,v\).

Solve \(\sqrt{2x+9}=x\) and check both candidates from squaring.

Homework word problems

These eight increasingly challenging problems complete the 20-question homework. Define quantities, write a model, show your reasoning, and give units. Reject values that do not fit the original equation or the situation.

W1 Homework word problem 1 of 8

A square garden has area \(162\text{ m}^2\). Find its exact side length and the exact fencing length needed around all four sides.

Answer

Side: \(9\sqrt2\) m. Fencing: \(36\sqrt2\) m.

  1. The side is \(\sqrt{162}=\sqrt{81\cdot2}=9\sqrt2\). Multiply by 4 for the perimeter.

W2 Homework word problem 2 of 8

A cube-shaped gift box has volume \(432\text{ cm}^3\). Ribbon runs along four consecutive edges around one square face. Find the exact edge length and the ribbon length, with no extra ribbon for a knot.

Answer

Edge: \(6\sqrt[3]2\) cm. Ribbon: \(24\sqrt[3]2\) cm.

  1. Use \(e=\sqrt[3]{432}=\sqrt[3]{216\cdot2}=6\sqrt[3]2\).
  2. The stated ribbon path has four edges, so its length is \(4e\).

W3 Homework word problem 3 of 8

Two straight paths run due east and then due north, with lengths \(\sqrt{75}\) m and \(\sqrt{27}\) m. Find the total walking distance and the direct straight-line distance between start and finish.

Answer

Walking: \(8\sqrt3\) m. Direct: \(\sqrt{102}\) m.

  1. The walking distance is \(5\sqrt3+3\sqrt3=8\sqrt3\).
  2. The paths meet at a right angle. The direct distance is \(\sqrt{75+27}=\sqrt{102}\), which has no square factor greater than 1.

W4 Homework word problem 4 of 8

A rectangular panel has area \(30\text{ m}^2\) and length \(3\sqrt{10}\) m. Find its exact width with a rationalized denominator, then its exact perimeter.

Answer

Width: \(\sqrt{10}\) m. Perimeter: \(8\sqrt{10}\) m.

  1. Width is \(30/(3\sqrt{10})=10/\sqrt{10}=\sqrt{10}\).
  2. Then \(P=2(3\sqrt{10}+\sqrt{10})=8\sqrt{10}\).

W5 Homework word problem 5 of 8

A walker covers 18 km at a constant speed of \(3+\sqrt3\) km/h. Find the exact travel time with a rational denominator, then round to the nearest hundredth of an hour.

Answer

\(9-3\sqrt3\) hours, approximately \(3.80\) hours.

  1. Time is distance divided by speed: \(18/(3+\sqrt3)\).
  2. Multiply by the conjugate: \(18(3-\sqrt3)/(9-3)=3(3-\sqrt3)\).
  3. Round only the final value \(3.8038\ldots\).

W6 Homework word problem 6 of 8

A cube has volume \(125\text{ cm}^3\). A larger, similar cube has 8 times that volume. Using \(S=6V^{2/3}\), find both surface areas and their ratio. Explain why the ratio is not 8.

Answer

Original: \(150\text{ cm}^2\). Larger: \(600\text{ cm}^2\). Ratio: \(4:1\).

  1. The volumes are 125 and 1000. Thus \(6(125)^{2/3}=6(25)=150\) and \(6(1000)^{2/3}=6(100)=600\).
  2. An eightfold volume multiplies each length by 2 and each area by \(2^2=4\). Equivalently, \(8^{2/3}=4\).

W7 Homework word problem 7 of 8

A 13-foot ladder leans against a wall. Its foot is \(x\) feet from the wall and its top is \(x+7\) feet above the ground. Model the height as \(\sqrt{169-x^2}\) and find both distances.

Answer

The foot is 5 feet from the wall; the top is 12 feet high.

  1. Write \(\sqrt{169-x^2}=x+7\), with \(x>0\) and \(x\leq13\).
  2. Square: \(169-x^2=x^2+14x+49\). Then \(x^2+7x-60=(x+12)(x-5)=0\).
  3. Reject \(-12\) as a distance. For \(x=5\), \(\sqrt{169-25}=12=5+7\).

W8 Homework word problem 8 of 8

Two square murals have areas \(x+32\) and \(x+7\) square meters. The larger mural’s side is exactly 1 meter longer. Find \(x\), both areas, and both side lengths.

Answer

\(x=137\). Areas: \(169\text{ m}^2\) and \(144\text{ m}^2\). Sides: 13 m and 12 m.

  1. The model is \(\sqrt{x+32}-\sqrt{x+7}=1\), with \(x>-7\) for two positive areas.
  2. Isolate and square: \(x+32=x+8+2\sqrt{x+7}\). Thus \(24=2\sqrt{x+7}\).
  3. Now \(\sqrt{x+7}=12\), so \(x=137\). Check the original difference: \(\sqrt{169}-\sqrt{144}=13-12=1\).

Quick questions and answers

What is a radical expression?

It is an expression containing a root, such as a square root or cube root. The index identifies the root, and the radicand is the quantity inside the radical.

Why does the square root of x squared need an absolute value?

The principal square root is nonnegative. For a real x, the square root of x squared equals its absolute value, so the result remains correct when x is negative.

When can radicals be added or subtracted?

After simplification, their indices and radicands must match. Combine the coefficients of like radicals; leave unlike radicals separate.

How do I rationalize a binomial denominator?

Multiply the whole numerator and denominator by the conjugate. Use the difference-of-squares identity, simplify the numerator, and preserve the original domain.

What does a fractional exponent mean?

For a positive base, the denominator of the exponent gives the root and the numerator gives the power. A negative exponent takes the reciprocal.

Why do radical equations need an original-equation check?

Squaring can turn unequal opposite numbers into equal squares, introducing extraneous candidates. Substitute each candidate into the original equation and respect its domain.
