Why denominators must match

The denominator tells the size of each part: halves are large pieces, thirds are smaller pieces, and sixths are smaller still. The numerator counts how many of those parts we have. We can only add or subtract numbers that count the same kind of thing, so the parts must be the same size first.

In \(\frac{1}{2}\) + \(\frac{1}{3}\), a half and a third are different-sized parts, so we cannot just count them together. Cut both bars into sixths: \(\frac{1}{2}\) = \(\frac{3}{6}\) and \(\frac{1}{3}\) = \(\frac{2}{6}\). Now every part is a sixth, and 3 sixths plus 2 sixths is 5 sixths, so \(\frac{1}{2}\) + \(\frac{1}{3}\) = \(\frac{5}{6}\). Add the numerators and keep the common denominator. Never add the denominators: the size of the parts does not change when we put them together.

Practice: try these five questions

Q1

Why is it wrong to add \(\dfrac{1}{4}\) + \(\dfrac{1}{3}\) by adding the tops and the bottoms to get \(\dfrac{2}{7}\)? Find the correct sum.

Answer

\(\dfrac{7}{12}\); fourths and thirds must first be renamed as twelfths

  1. Fourths and thirds are different-sized parts, so they cannot be counted together. Adding the denominators would invent a new part size, sevenths, that neither fraction uses.
  2. Quick test: \(\frac{2}{7}\) is less than \(\frac{1}{3}\) by itself, but adding \(\frac{1}{4}\) must make the total larger than \(\frac{1}{3}\). So \(\frac{2}{7}\) cannot be right.
  3. Rename with twelfths: \(\frac{1}{4}\) = \(\frac{3}{12}\) and \(\frac{1}{3}\) = \(\frac{4}{12}\). Then \(\frac{3}{12}\) + \(\frac{4}{12}\) = \(\frac{7}{12}\).

Q2

Rename \(\dfrac{1}{2}\) and \(\dfrac{1}{5}\) as tenths. Then add them.

Answer

\(\dfrac{7}{10}\)

  1. Find the LCD. Multiples of 2: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. Multiples of 5: 5, 10. The smallest shared multiple is 10, so LCD = 10.
  2. Build equivalent fractions: \(\frac{1}{2}\) = \(\frac{1 \times 5}{2 \times 5}\) = \(\frac{5}{10}\); \(\frac{1}{5}\) = \(\frac{1 \times 2}{5 \times 2}\) = \(\frac{2}{10}\).
  3. Add the numerators and keep the denominator: \(\frac{5}{10}\) + \(\frac{2}{10}\) = \(\frac{7}{10}\).
  4. 7 and 10 have no common factor greater than 1, so \(\frac{7}{10}\) is in simplest form.
  5. Check by subtracting: \(\frac{7}{10}\) − \(\frac{1}{5}\) = \(\frac{1}{2}\).

Q3

One bar is cut into fourths with 1 part shaded. Another bar is cut into eighths with 3 parts shaded. Rename so the parts are the same size, then find \(\dfrac{1}{4}\) + \(\dfrac{3}{8}\).

Answer

\(\dfrac{5}{8}\)

  1. Find the LCD. 8 is a multiple of 4, so the least common denominator is 8.
  2. Build equivalent fractions: \(\frac{1}{4}\) = \(\frac{1 \times 2}{4 \times 2}\) = \(\frac{2}{8}\); \(\frac{3}{8}\) already has denominator 8.
  3. Add the numerators and keep the denominator: \(\frac{2}{8}\) + \(\frac{3}{8}\) = \(\frac{5}{8}\).
  4. 5 and 8 have no common factor greater than 1, so \(\frac{5}{8}\) is in simplest form.
  5. Check by subtracting: \(\frac{5}{8}\) − \(\frac{3}{8}\) = \(\frac{1}{4}\).

Q4

Which pair can be added right away without renaming: \(\dfrac{2}{5}\) and \(\dfrac{3}{5}\), \(\dfrac{1}{3}\) and \(\dfrac{1}{6}\), or \(\dfrac{3}{4}\) and \(\dfrac{3}{8}\)? Add that pair.

Answer

\(\dfrac{2}{5}\) and \(\dfrac{3}{5}\); the sum is \(\dfrac{5}{5}\) = 1

  1. Only \(\frac{2}{5}\) and \(\frac{3}{5}\) already have the same denominator, so their parts are already the same size.
  2. Add the numerators and keep the denominator: \(\frac{2}{5}\) + \(\frac{3}{5}\) = \(\frac{5}{5}\) = 1 whole.
  3. The other pairs have different denominators, so they must be renamed first.

Q5

Lena says \(\dfrac{1}{2}\) + \(\dfrac{1}{4}\) = \(\dfrac{2}{6}\). Explain her mistake and find the correct sum.

Answer

\(\dfrac{3}{4}\); Lena added the denominators

  1. Lena added the numerators and the denominators. The denominator names the size of the parts, so it should not be added.
  2. Her answer \(\frac{2}{6}\) = \(\frac{1}{3}\) is smaller than \(\frac{1}{2}\), the first number she started with, so it cannot be a correct sum.
  3. Rename halves as fourths: \(\frac{1}{2}\) = \(\frac{2}{4}\). Then \(\frac{2}{4}\) + \(\frac{1}{4}\) = \(\frac{3}{4}\).

Finding a common denominator

A common denominator is a number that both denominators divide into evenly. The least common denominator (LCD) is the smallest one. To find it, list the multiples of each denominator and circle the first number that appears in both lists. For 4 and 6: multiples of 4 are 4, 8, 12, 16 and multiples of 6 are 6, 12, 18, so the LCD is 12.

Two shortcuts help. If the larger denominator is already a multiple of the smaller one, the larger denominator is the LCD (for 3 and 9, the LCD is 9). If the denominators share no factor other than 1, the LCD is their product (for 3 and 5, the LCD is 15). Any common denominator gives the right answer, but the least one keeps the numbers small and means less simplifying at the end.

  4. 11 and 24 have no common factor greater than 1, so \(\frac{11}{24}\) is in simplest form.

Building equivalent fractions

Equivalent fractions are different names for the same amount. To build one, multiply the numerator and the denominator by the same nonzero number. For \(\frac{2}{3}\), multiplying both by 4 gives \(\frac{8}{12}\). This works because multiplying by \(\frac{4}{4}\) is multiplying by 1, and multiplying by 1 never changes a value. On a fraction bar, cutting each third into 4 smaller pieces makes 12 pieces, and the shaded amount stays exactly the same.

To rename a fraction with a chosen denominator, ask: what times the old denominator gives the new one? For \(\frac{2}{3}\) with denominator 12, 3 × 4 = 12, so multiply the numerator by 4 too: \(\frac{2}{3}\) = \(\frac{8}{12}\). For \(\frac{3}{4}\), 4 × 3 = 12, so \(\frac{3}{4}\) = \(\frac{9}{12}\). Whatever you do to the bottom, do to the top.

Q14

Q15

Is \(\dfrac{3}{5}\) equal to \(\dfrac{9}{15}\)? Is \(\dfrac{2}{3}\) equal to \(\dfrac{6}{8}\)? Explain each.

Answer

Yes, \(\dfrac{3}{5}\) = \(\dfrac{9}{15}\); no, \(\dfrac{2}{3}\) ≠ \(\dfrac{6}{8}\)

  1. \(\frac{3}{5}\): 5 × 3 = 15 and 3 × 3 = 9, so \(\frac{3}{5}\) = \(\frac{9}{15}\). The top and bottom were multiplied by the same number.
  2. \(\frac{2}{3}\): the numerator was multiplied by 3 (2 × 3 = 6), but the denominator would have to be 3 × 3 = 9, not 8. So \(\frac{2}{3}\) = \(\frac{6}{9}\), and \(\frac{6}{8}\) = \(\frac{3}{4}\) is a different amount.

Adding fractions with unlike denominators

Adding fractions with unlike denominators uses four steps. (1) Find the least common denominator. (2) Build equivalent fractions with that denominator. (3) Add the numerators and keep the common denominator. (4) Check and simplify. For \(\frac{3}{8}\) + \(\frac{1}{6}\): the LCD of 8 and 6 is 24; \(\frac{3}{8}\) = \(\frac{9}{24}\) and \(\frac{1}{6}\) = \(\frac{4}{24}\); then \(\frac{9}{24}\) + \(\frac{4}{24}\) = \(\frac{13}{24}\). Since 13 and 24 share no factor greater than 1, the answer is \(\frac{13}{24}\).

Two checks catch most mistakes. First, when both fractions are positive, the sum must be greater than each fraction you added. Second, if the numerator ends up larger than the denominator, the sum is more than 1 whole: write it as a mixed number, such as \(\frac{29}{24}\) = \(1\frac{5}{24}\). Add the numerators only after the denominators match.

Subtracting fractions with unlike denominators

Subtraction follows the same four steps as addition; only step 3 changes. Find the LCD, build equivalent fractions, subtract the numerators while keeping the common denominator, then check and simplify. For \(\frac{5}{6}\) − \(\frac{1}{4}\): the LCD of 6 and 4 is 12, so \(\frac{5}{6}\) = \(\frac{10}{12}\) and \(\frac{1}{4}\) = \(\frac{3}{12}\). Then \(\frac{10}{12}\) − \(\frac{3}{12}\) = \(\frac{7}{12}\).

Check a subtraction by adding: the answer plus the amount you took away must give back the starting fraction. Here \(\frac{7}{12}\) + \(\frac{3}{12}\) = \(\frac{10}{12}\) = \(\frac{5}{6}\). Always look for a common factor at the end. For example, \(\frac{3}{4}\) − \(\frac{1}{4}\) = \(\frac{2}{4}\), and dividing the top and bottom by 2 gives \(\frac{1}{2}\). Subtract the numerators only after the denominators match.

Adding and subtracting mixed numbers

A mixed number has a whole part and a fraction part. To add mixed numbers, give the fraction parts a common denominator, add the wholes, and add the fractions. If the fraction part becomes 1 or more, regroup it into a whole. For \(2\frac{1}{3}\) + \(1\frac{3}{4}\): with denominator 12 this is \(2\frac{4}{12}\) + \(1\frac{9}{12}\) = \(3\frac{13}{12}\), and since \(\frac{13}{12}\) = \(1\frac{1}{12}\), the answer is \(4\frac{1}{12}\).

To subtract, match the denominators first. If the first fraction part is smaller than the one you are taking away, rename one whole as a fraction with the common denominator and add it to the fraction part. For \(4\frac{1}{4}\) − \(2\frac{2}{3}\): this is \(4\frac{3}{12}\) − \(2\frac{8}{12}\); since \(\frac{3}{12}\) < \(\frac{8}{12}\), rename \(4\frac{3}{12}\) as \(3\frac{15}{12}\), then subtract to get \(1\frac{7}{12}\). One whole equals \(\frac{12}{12}\), so regrouping changes the form, not the value.

Solving fraction word problems

Read the story, then choose the operation before you calculate. Ask: am I combining amounts, finding what remains, or comparing two amounts? Combining means add. Finding what is left after some is used, eaten, cut, or walked means subtract. Comparing (how much more, how much longer, how much farther) also means subtract the smaller amount from the larger one.

In the recipe example, milk and water are combined, so add: \(\frac{2}{3}\) + \(\frac{3}{4}\) = \(\frac{8}{12}\) + \(\frac{9}{12}\) = \(\frac{17}{12}\) = \(1\frac{5}{12}\) cups. In the trail example, the distance walked is taken from the total, so subtract: \(1\frac{1}{2}\) − \(\frac{5}{8}\) = \(\frac{12}{8}\) − \(\frac{5}{8}\) = \(\frac{7}{8}\) mile. Use a common denominator, and always finish with a label such as cups, miles, or hours.

Strong problem solvers use three moves. Estimate first by rounding each fraction to a benchmark: 0, \(\frac{1}{2}\), or 1. For \(\frac{7}{8}\) − \(\frac{1}{3}\), \(\frac{7}{8}\) is close to 1 and \(\frac{1}{3}\) is close to \(\frac{1}{2}\), so expect an answer near 1 − \(\frac{1}{2}\) = \(\frac{1}{2}\). Does the inverse operation return the start? \(\frac{13}{24}\) + \(\frac{8}{24}\) = \(\frac{21}{24}\) = \(\frac{7}{8}\), so yes. Finish with the checklist: common denominator, simplest form, reasonable answer.

Kai practices piano for \(1\dfrac{1}{4}\) hours on Saturday and \(\dfrac{2}{3}\) hour on Sunday. How long does he practice over the weekend?

Answer

\(1\dfrac{11}{12}\) hours

  1. The two practice times are combined, so add: \(1\frac{1}{4}\) + \(\frac{2}{3}\).
  2. Find the LCD. Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12. Multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12. The smallest shared multiple is 12, so LCD = 12.
  3. Build equivalent fractions: \(\frac{1}{4}\) = \(\frac{1 \times 3}{4 \times 3}\) = \(\frac{3}{12}\); \(\frac{2}{3}\) = \(\frac{2 \times 4}{3 \times 4}\) = \(\frac{8}{12}\).
  4. Rewrite the problem: \(1\frac{3}{12}\) + \(\frac{8}{12}\).
  5. Add the wholes and the fractions: 1 + 0 = 1, and \(\frac{3}{12}\) + \(\frac{8}{12}\) = \(\frac{11}{12}\). That gives \(1\frac{11}{12}\).
  6. Check by subtracting: \(1\frac{11}{12}\) − \(\frac{2}{3}\) = \(1\frac{1}{4}\).

W6

Zoe's plant is \(5\dfrac{1}{2}\) inches tall. Eli's plant is \(3\dfrac{3}{4}\) inches tall. How much taller is Zoe's plant?

Answer

\(1\dfrac{3}{4}\) inches

  1. “How much taller” compares two heights, so subtract: \(5\frac{1}{2}\) − \(3\frac{3}{4}\).
  2. Find the LCD. 4 is a multiple of 2, so the least common denominator is 4.
  3. Build equivalent fractions: \(\frac{1}{2}\) = \(\frac{1 \times 2}{2 \times 2}\) = \(\frac{2}{4}\); \(\frac{3}{4}\) already has denominator 4.
  4. Rewrite the problem: \(5\frac{2}{4}\) − \(3\frac{3}{4}\).
  5. Since \(\frac{2}{4}\) < \(\frac{3}{4}\), rename one whole as \(\frac{4}{4}\): \(5\frac{2}{4}\) = \(4\frac{6}{4}\).
  6. Subtract the wholes and the fractions: 4 − 3 = 1, and \(\frac{6}{4}\) − \(\frac{3}{4}\) = \(\frac{3}{4}\). That gives \(1\frac{3}{4}\).
  7. Check by adding: \(1\frac{3}{4}\) + \(3\frac{3}{4}\) = \(5\frac{1}{2}\).

A bag holds 4 pounds of rice. The cook uses \(1\dfrac{2}{3}\) pounds. How much rice is left in the bag?

\(2\dfrac{1}{3}\) pounds

  2. 4 has no fraction part, so rename one whole: 4 = \(3\frac{3}{3}\).

\(\dfrac{11}{12}\) cup

\(1\dfrac{7}{8}\) miles

Quick questions and answers

Why do fractions need a common denominator before adding or subtracting?

The denominator names the size of the parts. Halves and thirds are different-sized parts, so they cannot be counted together until both are renamed with the same part size, such as sixths.

How do I find the least common denominator?

List the multiples of each denominator and choose the smallest number that appears in both lists. If one denominator is a multiple of the other, the larger one is the LCD.

How do I build an equivalent fraction?

Multiply the numerator and the denominator by the same nonzero number. Multiplying by a fraction such as 4/4 is multiplying by 1, so the value does not change.

Do I add the denominators?

No. After the denominators match, add or subtract only the numerators and keep the common denominator. Then simplify and rewrite improper fractions as mixed numbers.

When do I regroup with mixed numbers?

When adding, regroup if the fraction part is 1 or more, such as 13/12 = 1 1/12. When subtracting, if the first fraction part is smaller, rename one whole as a fraction like 12/12 before subtracting.

How can I check my answer?

Estimate with benchmarks 0, 1/2, and 1 before solving, then use the inverse operation: add back what you subtracted, or subtract one addend from the sum.
