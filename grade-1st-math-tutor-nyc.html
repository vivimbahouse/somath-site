The curriculum

What 1st graders learn in NYC public and private schools

First grade math in NYC covers addition and subtraction within 20, place value to 100, comparing two-digit numbers, telling time to the half-hour, and measuring length with non-standard and standard units. The Common Core-aligned curriculum used in most NYC district schools (Bridges, Everyday Math, and increasingly Illustrative Mathematics through NYC Solves) introduces number bonds, ten-frames, and part-part-whole models. Private schools (Trinity, Dalton, Ethical Culture Fieldston) use similar frameworks but often move faster on multi-digit addition and introduce basic multiplication concepts by spring.

Where kids get stuck

The three struggles we see most often in 1st grade math

1. Counting on instead of using number bonds

Many 1st graders solve 8+5 by counting up from 8 on their fingers. This works — until they have to solve 28+5, or eventually 128+5. The stronger habit is decomposing 5 into 2+3 so 8+2=10, then 10+3=13. Kids who don't build this habit in 1st grade spend the next four years counting on fingers under the desk.

2. Reversing digits when writing two-digit numbers

Writing 51 instead of 15 is developmentally normal at age 6, but if it's still happening consistently in April of 1st grade, it's a signal to slow down and rebuild place-value understanding with base-10 blocks. Left unaddressed, it becomes a source of errors that persists into 2nd and 3rd grade addition.

3. Guessing at word problems instead of drawing the model

First graders who read a word problem and immediately guess an operation (usually addition) are relying on pattern-matching that will break as soon as problems get more complex. The habit to build is drawing a simple tape diagram or part-part-whole box before doing any arithmetic — even for problems the child could solve in their head.

Milestones by month

The five 1st grade math milestones every NYC parent should watch for

  • By November: Addition and subtraction facts within 10, fluently and without finger-counting.
  • By January: Place value to 100 — able to read, write, and compare two-digit numbers using > < = symbols.
  • By March: Addition within 20 using number bond strategies (making 10s), not just counting on.
  • By April: Telling time to the hour and half-hour on an analog clock.
  • By May: Solving one-step word problems by drawing a model, not guessing an operation.

Common questions

1st grade math in NYC: parent FAQ

Is my 1st grader too young for math tutoring?

No. First grade is actually the ideal age to start structured math enrichment — the concepts are simple enough that kids can build habits (number bonds, tape diagrams, place-value reasoning) that last through high school. Waiting until 3rd or 4th grade when gaps appear means fixing bad habits rather than building good ones. At SOMATH we run Little Newtons A specifically for 1st and 2nd graders.

How much math practice should a 1st grader do at home?

10-15 minutes per day of focused practice, not 30-minute homework sessions. Consistency beats duration at this age. The best practice is number-line games, ten-frame flashcards, and reading number stories together — not worksheets. Save worksheets for reinforcing what's already been taught in class.

What if my 1st grader is ahead of grade level?

About 20% of the 1st graders we evaluate are already operating at 2nd-grade level in some strand (usually mental math or word problems) while still building foundations in another (usually place value or measurement). SOMATH's small-group format lets us differentiate within the group — a strong student works on 2nd-grade extensions while a peer consolidates 1st-grade content, same room, same session.

Are 1st grade state test scores meaningful?

New York State does not administer standardized math tests until 3rd grade, so 1st grade performance is measured entirely through classroom assessments, teacher observation, and school-internal benchmarks (many NYC private schools and gifted programs use ERB or WISC scores from Pre-K/K).
